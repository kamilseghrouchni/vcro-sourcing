# Gut microbiome compositional and functional features associate with Alzheimer's disease pathology

## Abstract

### BACKGROUND

The gut microbiome is a potentially modifiable risk factor for Alzheimer's disease (AD); however, understanding of its composition and function regarding AD pathology is limited.

### METHODS

Shallow‐shotgun metagenomics was used to analyze the fecal microbiome of participants in the Wisconsin Microbiome in Alzheimer's Risk Study, leveraging clinical data and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers. Differential abundance and ordinary least squares regression analyses were performed to find differentially abundant gut microbiome features and their associations with CSF biomarkers of AD and related pathologies.

### RESULTS

Gut microbiome composition and function differed between individuals with and without AD dementia. The compositional difference was replicated in an independent cohort. Differentially abundant gut microbiome features were associated with CSF biomarkers of AD and related pathologies.

### DISCUSSION

These findings enhance our understanding of alterations in gut microbial composition and function in AD, and suggest that gut microbes and their pathways are linked to AD pathology.

### Highlights

Gut microbiome composition and function differ between people with Alzheimer's disease (AD) dementia and cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals.Co‐occurring gut microbes show differential abundance across AD‐related groups (AD vs CU, amyloid status+ vs amyloid status−, and apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status+ vs APOE ε4 status−).Gut microbiome composition also differs between people with AD dementia and CU individuals in a larger validation cohort.Differentially abundant gut microbiome composition and function between AD and CU groups are correlated with cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers for AD and related pathologies.

## BACKGROUND

1

The human gut microbiome is widely recognized as an important modifiable factor in health and disease. The composition of the gut bacteria influences overall gut health by maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and immune homeostasis via the balanced composition and production of microbial metabolites such as short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs). 1 , 2 , 3 Evidence across developmental and neurodegenerative contexts also indicates that the microbiome may play a role in regulating neurological conditions through physiological pathways including bi‐directional vagus nerve signaling and maintenance or degradation of the intestinal barrier, and through inflammatory and metabolic systems. A growing body of literature suggests that changes in the gut microbiome and its metabolism may be influential in exacerbating the development of Alzheimer's disease (AD).

Compositional differences in gut microbiota, including relative abundance and diversity, have been observed between control and AD groups. 4 Other studies have reported that gut microbiome composition is altered among people with AD dementia, individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or preclinical AD compared with cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 To better determine the relationship between the gut microbiome and AD pathology, studies have leveraged measures of AD biomarkers obtained via cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, 9 positron emission tomography (PET), 10 and plasma. 11 In addition, inflammatory markers have been utilized to find associations with gut inflammation–driven AD pathology. 12 , 13

The shift toward functional analysis provides deeper insights into the metabolic or functional potential of the microbiome; given that multiple lines of evidence indicate that gut microbial pathways and associated metabolites influence disease development, it is important to jointly examine both the gut microbiome and its metabolism as a mode of understanding the natural history of AD and identifying potential therapeutic targets. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 A small number of studies have examined gut microbiome composition and function together with markers of AD pathology in humans, 8 , 18 , 19 , 20 but many other studies to date are limited to the composition of gut microbes in a single cohort. With multiple cohorts involved and a focus on multi‐omic analysis, the Alzheimer Gut Microbiome Project (AGMP) initiative continues to leverage the gut microbiome and metabolome to better understand metabolic processes that influence AD pathology.

To identify differences in composition and function as well as relationships between gut microbiome and AD pathology, this study collected stool samples from participants enrolled in the Microbiome in Alzheimer's Risk Study (MARS) and analyzed them in collaboration with the AGMP. Differences in microbiome diversity as well as abundance were compared between AD‐related groups (diagnosis, amyloid status, and apolipoprotein E [APOE] ε4 carriage), and the co‐occurrence of the common gut microbiota features was analyzed among comparison groups. In addition, validation of gut microbiota composition that was differentially abundant between AD compared to CU was performed in a larger cohort of participants who are part of the AGMP. Furthermore, determination of microbiome functional differences was compared between people with AD dementia and CU individuals. Finally, associations between any differentially abundant gut microbiome features were examined in relation to CSF biomarkers of AD and related pathologies to identify the potential microbes and microbial pathways that relate to AD pathology. We hypothesized that gut microbiome alterations in composition and function would be present among people with AD dementia compared to CU, as well as associated with AD pathology.

## METHODS

2

### Participants

2.1

Participants (n = 232, Table 1) included in this study were recruited from the Wisconsin Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC) Clinical Core and the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer's Prevention (WRAP). 21 The WRAP study enrolls participants between 40 and 65 years of age at study entry, and the cohort is enriched for parental history of AD dementia. The Wisconsin ADRC Clinical Core enrolls participants who span the AD continuum. Participants undergo APOE genotyping using competitive allele‐specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–based KASP genotyping assays (LGC Genomics, Beverly, MA), 22 as well as biannual blood draws and assessments of cognitive function. A subset of the cohort also completed CSF collection and PET neuroimaging for AD biomarker analysis. Participants co‐enrolled in MARS had fecal samples collected for gut microbiome analysis. Participants completed questionnaires including medical history and diet, at the time of fecal sample collection.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review: Although mechanistic studies show the relationship between gut microbiome and Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology, human studies on the role of gut microbiome composition and function in AD pathology are limited. Interpretation: This study leveraged the human gut microbiome metagenomics and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers and found that many of the gut microbiome compositional and functional features that are differentially abundant between AD and CU correlate with CSF biomarkers for AD and related pathologies. Future directions: Stratification of key human gut microbes and microbial pathways associated with AD and related pathologies may be utilized in other preclinical and clinical studies. Further research is needed on metabolomics, genomics, and exposomics that mediate gut microbes and related pathways specific to AD pathology.

Participant diagnosis of AD was determined by a multidisciplinary consensus diagnostic panel and based on National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) criteria. 23 , 24 Amyloid status was assessed using dynamic C11‐Pittsburgh compound B (PiB) or F18‐Florbetapir (FBP) PET scans and CSF biomarkers of amyloid beta (Aβ)42 and Aβ40 assayed via the Roche NeuroToolKit (NTK). Amyloid positivity (A+) was determined with PET imaging via PiB quantification (global cortical distribution volume ratio [DVR] >1.19), 25 FBP expert visual rating, and with CSF amyloid peptide ratio (Aβ42/Aβ40 <0.046). 26 Prior to admission into the WRAP, Wisconsin ADRC, and MARS studies, participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Study procedures were approved by the University of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board (IRB; WRAP and Wisconsin ADRC umbrella protocol ID: 2013‐0178; MARS protocol ID: 2015‐1121).

An independent cohort of participants who are part of the AGMP and who were recruited from multiple NIA‐funded ADRCs across the United States (n = 448, Table S1), was included for validation of differential abundance (DA) analysis. Clinical data used in the analysis were derived from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC). AGMP participants from Wisconsin were excluded to ensure a unique validation sample.

### Fecal sample collection and metagenomic data sequencing

2.2

Fecal samples were collected as described previously. 4 Briefly, participants collected their stool samples at home with standardized fecal collection kits provided by the study team. Participants returned their samples in insulated containers, which were chilled with frozen gel packs. Returned samples were immediately weighed and scored on the Bristol stool scale. Fecal samples were then subsampled using sterile straws and stored at −80°C until processing.

Fecal samples were processed for DNA extraction as described previously. 27 Briefly, samples were extracted using the MoBio PowerMag Soil DNA isolation kit with a magnetic bead plate. Extracted genomic DNA (gDNA) was quantified with Quant‐iT PicoGreen double‐stranded DNA (dsDNA) assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), and underwent a miniaturized KAPA HyperPlus library preparation using an iTru indexing strategy. 28 , 29 Libraries were normalized and pooled based on concentration, PCR‐cleaned, and then size‐selected (300‐700 bp) using the Sage Science PippinHT. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 as a paired‐end 150‐cycle run at the University of California San Diego (UCSD) IGM Genomics Center as part of AGMP initiative. 30

### Metagenomic data processing

2.3

The metagenomic data processing was performed as described previously. 31 The sequence data were filtered for all adapters known to fastp (version 0.23.4) in paired‐end mode by explicitly specifying a known adapters file. 32 Fastp also removed sequences shorter than 45 nucleotides with a flag to filter the minimum length of each sequence. Each sample was then filtered against each genome in the human pangenome, 33 as well as both T2T‐CHM13v2.0, 34 and GRCh38, 35 using minimap2 36 (version 2.26‐r1175) with a short read mode. The data were first run in paired‐end mode, and then run in single‐end mode, per genome. Each successive run was converted from SAM to FASTQ using samtools 37 (version 1.17). The single‐end data were repaired using fastq_pair 38 (version 1.0) specifying a table size of 50 M. Compute support was provided with GNU Parallel 39 (version 20180222). Single‐end FASTQ output from samtools was split into R1 and R2 with a custom Rust program, with rust‐bio for parsing 40 (version 1.4.0). Data were multiplexed with sed and demultiplexed using a custom Python script. Shotgun sequencing data were then uploaded to and processed through Qiita 41 (Study ID 13663). Sequence adapter and host filtering were executed using qp‐fastp‐minimap2 version 2022.04. Subsequently, Woltka 42 version 0.1.4 (qp‐woltka 2022.09) with the Web of Life 2 database was employed for taxonomic and functional predictions. Genomic coverages were computed, and features with less than 25% coverage were excluded. 43 To enhance data quality, a prevalence filter using QIIME 2 v2023.5 44 was applied, eliminating features present in less than 10% of samples and samples with a sampling depth of less than 500,000 reads to mitigate the inclusion of erroneous and low‐quality reads. The resulting feature table was utilized for downstream analysis.

### Biomarker measurements

2.4

#### CSF biomarkers

2.4.1

CSF samples were collected via lumbar puncture in the morning after fasting for 8–12 h as described previously. 26 CSF biomarkers were measured using the NTK, a panel of exploratory robust prototype assays (Roche Diagnostics International Ltd, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). The following biomarkers were quantified on the Cobas e 601 module (Roche Diagnostics International Ltd): ​​Aβ42, phosphorylated tau‐181 (p‐tau181, total tau (t‐tau), S100 calcium binding protein B (S100B), and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6); and the remaining biomarkers were assayed on the Cobas e 411 analyzer: Aβ40, neurofilament light protein (NfL), neurogranin, α‐synuclein, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), chitinase‐3‐like protein 1 (YKL‐40), and soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (sTREM2). Each biomarker was measured as markers of AD and related pathologies, which were amyloid pathology (Aβ42/Aβ40), tau pathophysiology (p‐tau181 and t‐tau), neurodegeneration (NfL), synaptic dysfunction and injury (neurogranin and α‐synuclein), inflammation (IL‐6), and glial activation (S100B, astrocytic activation [GFAP, YKL‐40], and microglial activation [sTREM2]).

#### PET biomarkers

2.4.2

Dynamic 11C‐PiB scans and late‐frame 18F‐FBP scans were acquired using a Siemens ECAT EXACT HR+ tomograph as described previously. 45 , 46 11C‐PiB DVRs were estimated with Logan graphical analysis and a cerebellar gray matter reference region. Global cortical DVR was calculated using eight Automated Anatomical Labeling Atlas 3–defined regions (anterior cingulate cortex, angular gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, supramarginal gyrus, superior temporal gyrus, and ventromedial prefrontal cortex); global DVR >1.16 was used to determine PiB A+ status. 18F‐FBP images were evaluated by an expert visual rater (S.C.J.) to determine A+ status.

### Statistical analysis

2.5

The overall summary of schematic flow diagram of data layers and statistical approaches used for the main findings of the study is depicted in Figure S1.

#### Participant demographics

2.5.1

Statistical analysis of participant demographics was performed across clinical diagnoses using the Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test for continuous variables and Pearson's chi‐square test for categorical variables. Analysis with multiple comparisons was corrected for multiple tests employing the Bonferroni correction method. Because multiple comparisons were made between groups for continuous variables (i.e., age and BMI), we applied Bonferroni correction to control for the family‐wise error rate. The corrected significance threshold for continuous variables was set to 0.05 divided by the number of continuous variables tested (α = 0.05/2 = 0.025). Similarly, for categorical variables (i.e., sex, race, APOE genotype, APOE ε4 allele, Bristol stool type, amyloid status, and medication status), we applied the Bonferroni correction. The significance threshold for categorical variables was set to 0.05 divided by the number of categorical variables tested (α = 0.05/7 = 0.00714).

#### Diversity analysis of the gut microbiota composition

2.5.2

Gut microbiome diversities were calculated using QIIME 2 tools. 44 Alpha diversity indices including Shannon, 47 Evenness, 48 and Faith's phylogenetic diversity (PD) 49 were calculated to determine the diversity of gut microbial species within each sample. The Shannon index estimates diversity by considering both species richness (the number of unique taxa) and the evenness (the distribution of abundances across species) of their abundance. Evenness focuses solely on the proportional distribution of taxa. Finally, Faith's PD captures the phylogenetic differences among taxa. Beta diversity indices including Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, 50 and Weighted, 51 and Unweighted 52 UniFrac were calculated to determine the diversity of gut microbial communities between different samples for each AD‐related group. Bray–Curtis dissimilarity measures both the shared abundance of microbes between samples and the total number of microbes present. Although Unweighted UniFrac considers only the phylogenetic distances between microbes in the samples, Weighted UniFrac also accounts for their relative abundances. We adjusted p‐values for multiple comparisons across the AD risk phenotypes using the Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05/3 = 0.0167).

#### DA analysis of the gut microbiome composition and function

2.5.3

The Bayesian Inferential Regression for Differential Microbiome Analysis (BIRDMAn) pipeline was used for microbiome DA analysis. 53 Microbiome features (composition and function) were outcome variables and each AD‐related group (clinical diagnosis, amyloid status, and APOE ε4 status) was a predictor adjusting for covariates including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), Bristol type, medication status, and time between fecal collection and diagnosis/amyloid measurement. Log ratios of Top (features with greater abundances in AD, A+ status, and APOE ε4 carriage) and Bottom (features with lower abundances in AD, A+ status, and APOE ε4 carriage); ranked features of each AD‐related group (log[Top features/Bottom features]) were calculated and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to test whether log ratios differed significantly across levels of each predictor, with p‐values corrected using the Bonferroni adjustment method (α = 0.05/3 = 0.0167).

#### Co‐occurrence analysis of gut microbiota features across clinical diagnosis, amyloid status, and APOE ε4 status groups

2.5.4

Co‐occurrence analysis was performed to find common gut microbiota features across different AD conditions. Venn diagrams were created to illustrate overlapping taxa associated with multiple AD‐related groups within each category—Top (more abundant in AD‐related groups) and Bottom (less abundant in AD‐related groups)—at each taxonomic level (phylum, family, genus, and species).

#### Comparative validation of gut microbiota composition in two distinct cohorts

2.5.5

Log ratios of Top and Bottom features of each AD group (log[Top features/Bottom features]) were also calculated and analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test in MARS and validation cohorts from AGMP to identify similarities in microbiome features that differ between the AD and CU groups in both cohorts. Because the AGMP cohort had clinical diagnosis data available, these analyses were completed using diagnosis as a predictor in both the MARS and AGMP samples to validate our compositional findings in a second independent cohort.

#### Functional analysis of gut microbiome pathways in a clinical diagnosis group

2.5.6

To identify microbiome‐related functional differences across the AD continuum, differentially abundant KEGG Orthology (KO) 54 pathways and their associated species were determined using BIRDMAn. 53 Microbiome features (KO pathways) were outcome variables and clinical diagnosis was a predictor adjusting for covariates including age, sex, BMI, Bristol type, medication status, and time between fecal collection and diagnosis measurement. Log ratios of Top and Bottom functional features (log[Top features/Bottom features]) were calculated and statistical significance was determined by the Mann–Whitney U test between AD and CU groups. Robust aitchison principal component analysis (RPCA) from Gemelli (version 0.0.10) was additionally used to identify sparse compositional KO 54 microbiome pathway features that are separated by sample variations. 55 RPCA is known to handle sparse and high‐dimensional datasets and is sensitive to datasets with outliers. 55 Microbiome datasets are often sparse and zero‐inflated; thus we employed RPCA to identify microbiome features that could explain the separation between groups. RPCA results were visualized with scores plots and biplots. Statistical analysis on RPCA was performed with permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) 56 between groups. The PERMANOVA was conducted to compare two groups (AD and CU) across multiple microbiome pathway features, with the p‐value corrected for multiple comparisons.

#### Association analysis of gut microbiome features and CSF biomarkers in a clinical diagnosis group

2.5.7

To explore the relationships between key microbial compositional features and functional pathways identified via BIRDMAn and CSF biomarkers of AD and related pathologies, we applied an ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression approach. Prior to fitting the linear regression model, CSF biomarkers were z‐score standardized using the StandardScaler (version 0.24.1) from the scikit‐learn library. 57 To address issues with sparse, compositional data, we used the multiplicative_replacement function from the scikit‐bio (version 0.5.7) skbio.stats.composition module to preprocess the metagenomics data. This function replaces zeros with small positive values, preserving the compositional nature of the data. Subsequently, a centered log ratio (CLR) transformation was applied to the metagenomics data to account for compositionality. Finally, OLS linear regression was performed on the microbial features that were previously found to be differentially abundant in AD versus CU, in relation to each CSF biomarker. Features' β coefficients were visualized using heatmaps. To account for multiple comparisons, p‐values were corrected using the Bonferroni method (composition: α = 0.05/2,706 = 1.848e‐5 and function: α = 0.05/396 = 1.263e‐4).

All other statistical analyses were performed using Python libraries SciPy (1.13.0), 58 scikit‐learn (1.4.2), 57 and NumPy (version 1.26.4). 59 All figures were generated using Python libraries Matplotlib (version 3.6.0) 60 and Seaborn (version 0.11.2). 61

## RESULTS

3

### Participant demographics

3.1

Participant characteristics are shown by clinical diagnosis in Table 1. Participants were between 47 and 90 years of age, 96% were White (4% Black), and 61% were female. The mean age differed significantly in dementia AD versus CU (p = 0.014). The percent ratio of APOE ε3/ε3 genotype was significantly lower and the percent ratio of APOE ε4/ε4 genotype was higher in dementia AD compared to CU (p < 0.001). Amyloid positivity was higher in the AD dementia group (p < 0.001). Statistical significance was determined after adjusting for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction.

### Diversity results in gut microbiota composition

3.2

The alpha diversities did not differ significantly differ between CU and AD participants within the diagnosis group, between amyloid negative and positive individuals within the amyloid status group, or between individuals in the APOE ε4 negative/non‐carrier status (APOE ε4−) group and those in the APOE ε4 positive/carrier status (APOE ε4+) group after correcting for multiple testing (Figure S2).

All beta diversity indices showed significant differences or a trend toward differences in species‐level composition between groups based on clinical diagnosis (Figure 1A–C; Bray–Curtis: F = 2.35, P = 0.009, Weighted UniFrac: F = 4.07, p = 0.009, Unweighted UniFrac: F = 2.09, p = 0.08). There were no differences in beta diversity indices between amyloid‐positive and amyloid‐negative individuals (Figure 1D–F; Bray–Curtis: F = 1.62, p = 0.19, Weighted UniFrac: F = 1.51, p = 1.00, Unweighted UniFrac: F = 0.88, p = 1.00). Individuals positive and negative for APOE ε4 demonstrated significant differences with the Bray–Curtis metric (F = 2.11, p = 0.009) and a trend toward differences with the Weighted UniFrac metric (F = 3.57, p = 0.06), but not with the Unweighted UniFrac metric (F = 1.26, p = 1.00) (Figure 1G–I).

### Gut microbiota composition in clinical diagnosis, amyloid status, and APOE ε4 status groups

3.3

DA analysis between AD and CU in the clinical diagnosis group showed a distinct gut microbiota composition at each taxonomic level (Figure 2A–D). At the phylum level, the abundance of phylum Firmicutes_A was lower in AD compared to CU (Bottom), and the abundance of phyla Bacteroidota, Patescibacteria, and Fusobacteriota was higher in AD compared to CU (Top) (Figure 2A). At the family level, families such as Clostridiaceae, Turicibacteraceae, Pasteurellaceae, Dialisteraceae, Enterococcaceae, and Ruminococcaceae were in the Bottom group and Fusobacteriaceae, Nanogingivalaceae, Gemellaceae, and Bacteroidaceae were in the Top group (Figure 2B). At the genus level, genera including Clostridium_P, Ruminococcus, and Cryptobacteroides were in the Bottom group and Fusobacterium_A, Fusobacterium, Nanogingivalis, and Gemella were in the Top group (Figure 2C). At the species level, species included in the Bottom group were Cryptobacteroides spp., Clostridium_P perfringens, Turicibacter sanguinis, Prevotella hominis, and Prevotella copri, and in the Top group were Fusobacterium_A mortiferum, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Fusobacterium animalis, Nanogingivalis gingivitcus, Collinsella stercoris, and Collinsella tanakaei (Figure 2D).

## DISCUSSION

4

In this study, we compared gut microbiome composition and function across AD‐relevant groups, including those with clinical diagnoses, amyloid status, and APOE ε4 carrier status. Our goal was to identify gut microbial features linked to AD and their relationships with CSF biomarkers of AD and related pathologies.

Alpha diversity indices showed no significant differences across AD‐related groups. However, beta diversity, particularly Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, revealed significant compositional differences between AD and CU, and between APOE ε4+ and APOE ε4− groups, but not between A+ and A− individuals. These findings suggest that although the overall diversity within these groups remains relatively stable, the specific composition and abundance of microbial taxa differ significantly in the clinical diagnosis and APOE ε4 status groups. In addition, the Weighted UniFrac metric distinguished AD from CU, but not with the Unweighted UniFrac metric, suggesting that the relative abundance of major taxa rather than community membership is important in driving these differences. Prior studies have found that alpha and beta diversities do not differ between AD versus CU 62 , 63 and A+ versus A−, 64 whereas other studies showed significant differences in alpha and beta diversity indices across the AD continuum in humans 4 , 7 and mice. 65 , 66 , 67 The present study's diversity analysis suggests that amyloid positivity alone may not strongly influence gut microbiota composition, whereas cognitive impairment (i.e., disease stage) may have a greater impact. The analysis also highlights the importance of considering different diversity metrics to capture the nuances of gut microbiome alterations in AD.

AD participants had lower abundance of phylum Firmicutes; families Ruminococcaceae, Clostridiaceae, and Turicibacteraceae; and genera Dialister, Clostridium, and Turicibacter; and greater abundance of phylum Bacteroidetes (a.k.a. Bacteroidota); families Bacteroidaceae and Gemellaceae; and genera Bacteroides and Gemella. This aligns with prior meta‐analyses on gut microbiome compositional differences in AD across 11 studies. 7 Results across studies are mixed; some have reported similar results, 6 , 68 whereas others have identified contradicting findings that fewer Bacteroidetes and Bacteroides and more Firmicutes were present in MCI or AD compared to CU. 69 , 70 Variability across studies may reflect differences in sample size, population heterogeneity, disease stage, sequencing methods, or confounders. 71 We mitigated methodological sources of bias through robust sequencing and analytical methodologies. Our validation of the DA analysis in a larger AGMP cohort largely recapitulates what we found in the smaller MARS sample, demonstrating consistent associations of specific taxa with AD diagnosis and suggesting that alterations in gut microbiome composition may be present in AD dementia.

The DA and co‐occurrence analyses identified microbial signatures that may influence both amyloid pathology and APOE activity to contribute to AD pathology. To address the confounding effects of APOE ε4, we analyzed AD diagnosis, amyloid status, and APOE ε4 status separately, including co‐occurrence analyses. Studies have shown gut microbiota differences between CU A+ and CU A−. 8 , 11 , 72 One of the studies showed that the phylum Bacteroidetes was enriched in CU A+ participants and that genera Faecalibacterium and Bilophila, negatively correlated with the global brain Aβ burden, were enriched in CU A− participants. 11 The co‐occurrence of certain gut microbiome features across the three predictors suggests that while AD diagnosis, amyloid pathology, and APOE may each have distinct microbial associations, amyloid and APOE may act together, in part through gut microbial pathways, to contribute to AD development.

Moving beyond the limitations of compositional analysis, this study further examined the functional pathways of gut microbiome in a clinical diagnosis group between AD and CU. Pathways exclusively associated with the AD group were largely driven by Bacteroides spp. (B. finegoldii, B. thetaiotaomicron, and B. ovatus). Multiple studies have reported the association between Bacteroides and AD. 4 , 73 , 74 , 75 Administration of B. fragilis to AD mice increased Aβ plaques, and inhibition of microglial clearance of Aβ was observed after introduction to B. fragilis. 76 Bacteroides has been reported as dominant in older adults compared to younger or healthier controls. 77 , 78 However, contrasting findings have been reported related to Bacteroides 8 , 79 and further strain‐specific studies are needed to understand the role of Bacteroides in AD pathology. Lipoic acid metabolism in B. thetaiotaomicron emerged as particularly significant due to its potential role in modulating oxidative stress and energy metabolism, both critical in AD pathophysiology. Mounting evidence has suggested that lipoic acid harbors antioxidant capacities, including abilities to scavenge free radicals, regenerate other antioxidants, and mitigate oxidative stress, essential factors in neurodegenerative diseases. 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 Lipoic acid is also crucial for energy metabolism, supporting mitochondrial function and glucose utilization essential for neuronal health, potentially protecting against energy deficits in AD. 84 , 85 , 86 Alterations in lipoic acid metabolism might reflect compensatory mechanisms in response to elevated oxidative stress or mitochondrial dysfunction in AD. This study emphasizes the metabolic role of Bacteroides spp. in AD pathophysiology, warranting further targeted research.

Finally, to determine the biomarker implications of our findings, we highlighted the relationships of gut microbiome features with CSF biomarkers of AD and related pathologies. Key microbiome species, particularly Fusobacterium nucleatum, F. animalis, and Nanogingivalis gingivitcus, were enriched in AD and A+ individuals, correlating with increased tau pathophysiology (p‐tau181 and t‐tau) and/or synaptic dysfunction and injury (neurogranin and α‐synuclein). Notably, F. nucleatum, an oral bacterium linked to periodontal diseases and colorectal cancer, 87 , 88 produces lipopolysaccharides (LPS) that activate microglia and elevate proinflammatory cytokines, increasing Aβ accumulation and tau phosphorylation in mouse models. 89 , 90 Conversely, Dialister hominis, which co‐occurred between CU and A−, negatively correlated with AD pathology (p‐tau181), neuronal damage (t‐tau), and synaptic dysfunction and injury (neurogranin and α‐synuclein). These findings are similar to previous works, which identified genus Dialister (less in AD) to be more abundant in CU individuals. 91 , 92 Similar correlations were observed for Turicibacter sanguinis, which was less abundant in AD compared to CU individuals. In both animal models and human studies, T. sanguinis has been reported to be less abundant in AD compared to controls. 4 , 93 , 94 These results suggest protective microbiota features in CU individuals, while others may increase susceptibility to AD.

Furthermore, biomarkers including neurodegeneration (NfL), synaptic dysfunction and injury (neurogranin and α‐synuclein), and tau pathophysiology (p‐tau181 and t‐tau) showed a positive relationship with B. thetaiotaomicron and its functions. Studies have linked B. thetaiotaomicron abundance with AD, as it was significantly higher in AD mice and related to poorer spatial learning. 95 Increased abundance of B. thetaiotaomicron was reported in AD participants. 96 , 97 However, in a non‐AD model, B. thetaiotaomicron was suggested to regulate enteric neuronal cell populations and neurogenic function. 98 The biosynthesis of antibiotics by B. thetaiotaomicron showed a positive relationship with biomarkers of AD and related pathologies (NfL, neurogranin, α‐synuclein, p‐tau181, and t‐tau). Long‐term antibiotic biosynthesis may lead to gut dysbiosis, potentially exacerbating neuroinflammatory processes and contributing to the pathophysiology of AD through mechanisms involving impaired neuronal health and exacerbated tau pathology. 99 , 100 , 101 , 102 These findings suggest that alterations in gut microbiome composition and function are associated with AD and related pathological markers measured in CSF.

Although the observed correlations were weak and were no longer significant after multiple test corrections, the multifactorial nature of AD and coordinated activity across the gut microbiome suggest that the combined effects of intestinal bacteria could result in clinically meaningful resilience or susceptibility to AD pathology.

The main limitation of our study is the small number of people with AD dementia relative to CU participants. Because clinical measures, fecal samples, and CSF biomarkers were all collected asynchronously, matching temporally proximal measurements of each data layer reduced the cohort's sample size and therefore yielded low statistical power. This may explain weak CSF–microbiome correlations and lack of effect significance after multiple test corrections. Similarly, the sample had low representation among A+ and APOE ε4+ individuals, which could bias the magnitude of effects observed. Although we employed rigorous statistical methods to address this limitation, it is possible that some true effects were not detected or were underestimated. In addition, the modest effect sizes observed in certain analyses could reflect genuine biological variability but may also be influenced by limited power, warranting cautious interpretation. These factors may impact the generalizability of our findings, particularly to broader populations with differing demographic or clinical characteristics. Future studies will benefit from larger samples with these pathogenic characteristics, allowing for a clearer distinction between disease effects and symptom effects. Moreover, due to the cross‐sectional approach, we were unable to capture longitudinal effects of the microbiome on AD progression. Although the ADRC cohort facilitated the assessment of microbiome differences across populations, limited biomarker data precluded CSF–microbiome association testing in this group. In addition, due to the observational design of the study, these results are correlational; future mechanistic studies are needed to identify causal relationships between gut microbiome features and biomarkers for AD pathology. Finally, beyond person‐level biological factors that may influence microbial composition and function, future studies are needed to examine the environmental exposures and the exposome more broadly, as they may impact the development of AD through gut microbiome mechanisms.

In conclusion, this study suggests that gut microbiome composition and function differ between people with AD dementia and CU individuals. These findings identify specific gut microbes and microbial pathways as potential targets for stratification, offering insights into their relationship with AD pathology and their potential role in developing microbiome‐based AD therapeutics.
