# Abnormal energy metabolism in ALS: a key player?

## Abstract

### Recent findings

The alteration of various metabolic pathways contributes to the heterogeneity of the clinical phenotype of ALS. Recent work showed that different ALS mutations selectively impact these pathways and translate to the disease phenotypes in patients and disease models. Strikingly, a growing number of studies point towards an early, even presymptomatic, contribution of abnormal energy homeostasis to the ALS pathogenesis. Advances in metabolomics generated valuable tools to study altered metabolic pathways, to test their therapeutic potential, and to develop personalized medicine. Importantly, recent preclinical studies and clinical trials demonstrated that targeting energy metabolism is a promising therapeutic approach.

### Summary

Abnormal energy metabolism is a key player in ALS pathogenesis, emerging as a source of potential disease biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

## INTRODUCTION

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a debilitating neurodegenerative disease that affects the nerve cells responsible for controlling voluntary movements. Specifically, both upper and lower motor neurons progressively degenerate, leading to muscle weakness, stiffness, and eventually paralysis. ALS is an extremely severe disease, resulting in the death of the patient usually within 2 to 5 years after the diagnosis [1]. ALS etiology and the exact mechanisms underlying selective motor neuron death are not yet fully understood.

Although ALS is classically viewed as a neurological disorder of the adult motor system, research from the past two decades demonstrated that ALS is multifactorial and associated with systemic alterations that extend beyond the motor axis. Among the non-motor clinical symptoms that impact the quality of life of the ALS patients, metabolic dysfunctions are the most common ones, together with cognitive impairments, linking ALS with frontotemporal dementia [1,2]. Many metabolic alterations influence disease susceptibility and negatively impact ALS prognosis [2].

This review discusses the contribution of whole-body energy homeostasis defects to ALS pathology and their place in the cascade of pathological events. Furthermore, we examine the link between the genetic background and the emergence of metabolic manifestations. Last but not least, we discuss how targeting energy metabolism might lead to identifying biomarkers and developing new therapeutic strategies.

## WEIGHT LOSS: THE HALLMARK OF METABOLIC DYSFUNCTION IN AMYOTROPHIC LATERAL SCLEROSIS

The balance between energy intake and expenditure is crucial to maintain a healthy body weight with stable energy stores and to prevent metabolic disorders like obesity and diabetes [3]. In ALS, energy homeostasis is imbalanced (Fig. 1). Prior to diagnosis, a significant proportion of ALS patients display a lower body mass index (BMI) [4▪▪], and two-thirds lost already weight at diagnosis [5–7]. The progressive weight loss is a strong indicator of a faster disease progression and a shorter survival, acting as an independent prognosis factor [4▪▪,5,6,7]. Besides, lower body fat mass increases the risk of developing ALS [8], while higher body fat content and subcutaneous fat are associated with a decreased risk of ALS and mortality [9–11].

Energy homeostasis is imbalanced in ALS patients and is characterized by concomitant decreases in energy intake and increased energy expenditure. The hypothalamus, a hub controlling many aspects of systemic energy metabolism, is impacted at the earliest stage of ALS. ALS, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.**

The etiology of premorbid weight loss in ALS is multifactorial. Hence, the progressive denervation-induced muscle atrophy and the reduction in food intake due to dysphagia do not fully explain it. Indeed, weight loss is also observed in patients without early swallowing problems and affects patients with spinal and bulbar symptoms onset [5,12]. Interestingly, loss of appetite was reported by ALS patients and contributes to decreased caloric intake, independently of dysphagia and other bulbar symptoms. Moreover, loss of appetite correlates with weight loss and worsens ALS prognosis [13,14]. Another factor participating in weight loss is increased energy expenditure, which is present in approximately 60% of ALS patients and leads to reduced fat storage [15,16]. Strikingly, this hypermetabolic state is associated with a faster functional decline and a shorter survival [17,18].

In the brain, the hypothalamus is a key hub controlling energy metabolism (Fig. 1). It senses and integrates internal signals to adapt food intake and energy expenditure [3]. Histopathological analysis of the hypothalamus of ALS patients showed TDP-43 inclusions [19,20,21▪▪], while imaging techniques revealed marked atrophy and disrupted connectivity [20,22,23,24,25▪,26]. These changes are also present in presymptomatic ALS gene carriers and correlate with lower BMI in ALS patients, suggesting a potential involvement in weight loss [19,22,25▪]. However, the underlying neuronal mechanisms was not explored until recently. Bolborea and collaborators documented the degeneration of melanin-concentrating hormone (MCH) neurons, located in the lateral hypothalamic area, in ALS mouse models and in sporadic ALS patients [21▪▪]. Strikingly, the central infusion of MCH in male SOD1 mice rescued weight loss through increased food intake, while energy expenditure remained unchanged [21▪▪]. These results suggest a potential critical role of this neuronal population in mediating weight loss in ALS.

Peripheral signaling to the hypothalamus, involvement of other brain regions controlling energy metabolism, altered thermogenesis, and dysfunctional gut microbiome could also mediate weight loss in ALS and need to be investigated further (Fig. 1).

## THE AMYOTROPHIC LATERAL SCLEROSIS METABOLIC PROFILE CORRELATES WITH DISEASE SUSCEPTIBILITY AND PROGNOSIS

Besides dramatic weight loss, ALS patients frequently develop glucose intolerance [27], insulin resistance [28], increased lipid levels [29], and fat redistribution [9] (Fig. 1). These alterations form a profile that is generally associated with obesity and higher cardiovascular risk. These paradoxical abnormalities significantly influence ALS risk and prognosis, but the direction of the associations remains conflictual and highly debated.

Altered energy metabolism impacts biomarkers of lipid metabolism, including triglycerides, total cholesterol and its carriers: low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c). Most studies reported increased blood lipid levels [30]. Observational studies showed that hypercholesterolemia, increased LDL-c levels, and higher LDL-c/HDL-c ratios were associated with a lower risk of developing ALS and with a better prognosis [29,31,32]. However, more recent studies questioned this protective effect. First, a meta-analysis did not find any association between lipids and overall survival [33]. Second, recent Mendelian randomization studies demonstrated that genetically predicted increased LDL-c and cholesterol levels are associated with increased risk [34–37]. Third, a large population-based case-control study showed that higher cholesterol levels, but not HDL-c or LDL-c, were positively associated with the ALS risk. Furthermore, higher HDL-c and LDL-c levels were associated with higher mortality in ALS patients in the adjusted survival analysis [38]. High HDL-c levels were linked with either an increased [33,39,40] or a decreased [41] risk of ALS. Interestingly, Van Mantgem and collaborators showed that higher HDL-c levels are negatively correlated with BMI and might reflect weight loss [33]. Furthermore, among patients with hypermetabolism, those with high HDL have a significantly shorter survival [40]. Concerning triglycerides, the results are more consistent. Hypertriglyceridemia was found in ALS patients and was associated with prolonged survival [29,38,42]. However, the beneficial effect was no longer significant when adjusted for BMI, indicating that higher triglycerides may be partially explained by the association with higher BMI [38].

In addition, untargeted metabolomics helped to identify differential altered metabolic pathways in ALS patients [43]. Recently, Goutman and collaborators analyzed the correlation between blood metabolic profiles and BMI trajectories [4▪▪]. The authors first showed that ALS patients were characterized by a significant BMI loss 5 years but not 10 years before study entry and that decreased BMI was associated with shorter survival and distinct metabolomic profile [4▪▪]. The metabolites correlating with BMI trajectories were mostly related to lipid metabolism, identifying sphingomyelins, ceramides, bile acids and plasmalogens, which were already linked to the ALS pathogenesis [44▪▪]. These pathways highlight new mechanisms that could be directly or indirectly related to the weight loss in ALS.

## EARLY INVOLVEMENT OF METABOLIC ABNORMALITIES IN AMYOTROPHIC LATERAL SCLEROSIS

The repeated association of blood lipid levels with the risk to develop ALS contributed to the hypothesis that premorbid metabolic alterations could play an early role in ALS pathogenesis. A long-lasting question is whether metabolic dysfunctions precede motor symptoms in ALS patients. Recently, Dorst and collaborators tackled this question with a cohort of 60 presymptomatic ALS gene mutation carriers [45▪▪]. These patients did not present any clinical signs or changes in neurofilament light chain (NfL) blood levels. NfL is considered as a good biomarker to define the presymptomatic stage in ALS as its elevation often precedes motor deficits [46,47]. Strikingly, the authors showed that presymptomatic ALS gene carriers display abnormal body composition, indicating elevated catabolism and loss of metabolically active cells compared to metabolically active tissue. Surprisingly, resting energy expenditure was reduced in ALS gene carriers but increased when approaching the expected age of onset [45▪▪]. According to the authors, these observations could reflect a presymptomatic reduction of physical activity, which was previously reported in ALS patients [48]. Interestingly, pathological alterations were already described in the hypothalamus of presymptomatic patients [22], suggesting a possible involvement of this key structure in the earliest metabolic dysfunctions.

Despite playing a key role in energy metabolism, skeletal muscle and its contribution to ALS pathology was largely neglected in ALS research. Mutant SOD1 mouse models demonstrated early metabolic reprogramming in glycolytic muscles associated with mitochondrial dysfunctions [49,50]. In ALS patients, only one recent study explored muscle-specific metabolic defects [51▪]. At the time of diagnosis, Lanznaster and collaborators observed alterations in the metabolome of skeletal muscles of ALS patients. Specifically, dysregulated metabolites were linked to carbohydrate metabolism and amino acid pathways, previously associated with ALS [52]. Interestingly, mitochondrial dysfunctions are associated with metabolism deregulation in the muscles of ALS patients [51▪]. Because of the small cohort size, this study reinforces the need for more investigations into muscles of ALS patients at early stages.

As the metabolic profile varies drastically along the life of ALS patients, there is an urgent need to distinguish the presymptomatic and the symptomatic phase of the disease. A more defined and characterized timing of the pathological events will diminish the discrepancies observed in the previous studies. Furthermore, it will guide earlier treatment decisions and optimize the timing of enrollment of ALS patients in clinical trials. As a consequence, longitudinal data on the metabolic profile of ALS patients will strengthen the clinical potential of interfering with the energy metabolism in ALS.

## THE COMPLEX LINK BETWEEN AMYOTROPHIC LATERAL SCLEROSIS GENETIC BACKGROUND AND METABOLIC ALTERATIONS

Genetic background is a key determinant of ALS phenotypic heterogeneity. Up to 20% of all ALS cases can be explained by genetic variations, including 10% of familial and approximately 10% of sporadic forms with a known genetic cause. During the past decade, the progress in genetics and the creation of large international consortia enabled the discovery of more than 30 ALS-associated genes and dozens of risk loci [53].

Recent investigations on glucose metabolism using 18F-FDG-PET identified hypo- and hypermetabolism in different brain regions depending on the genetic status of ALS patients. SOD1, C9ORF72, and sporadic ALS patients have different glucose metabolism profiles, which might reflect differential pathological involvement of cortical regions [54,55]. For instance, C9ORF72 carriers have more widespread metabolic alterations than SOD1 carriers [54]. Although some contradictory results emerged for mutant SOD1-ALS patients, they could be explained by the differences in patient cohorts and, notably, the distribution of SOD1 variants. Furthermore, metabolic profiling of presymptomatic ALS gene carriers revealed prevalent alterations in all mutations, but SOD1 and C9ORF72 gene carriers have each distinct profile compared to other ALS gene carriers [45▪▪,56▪▪]. C9ORF72-ALS patients have a lower cardiovascular risk profile, characterized by lower BMI, lower fasting serum glucose, and higher HDL, while SOD1-ALS patients have an opposite profile [45▪▪,56▪▪].

Interestingly, other less frequent ALS mutations also show variant-specific metabolic alterations. For instance, patients carrying the C/C genotype of the rs12608932 variant of the UNC13A gene have a worse ALS prognosis and a higher rate of weight loss at diagnosis than other related genotypes [57]. Furthermore, recent genome-wide association studies described ACSL5 as a new genetic factor increasing ALS risk and associated with rapid weight loss [58–60]. The ACSL5 gene encodes an enzyme playing an essential role in fatty acid metabolism [61]. Additional loci were characterized and linked to weight loss, suggesting that many other genetic factors, yet to be identified, might contribute to energy imbalance in ALS.

The hereditability of ALS is about 60%, indicating a significant contribution of environmental and lifestyle risk factors [53]. Epigenetic marks reflect the indirect interactions between the genome and the environment. A recent blood-based DNA methylation study of almost 10,000 ALS samples revealed the implication of metabolic and cholesterol pathways and confirmed several ALS risk factors identified in the clinic, such as lower BMI [62▪▪]. Although it is impossible to conclude about the causality, this study provides new insight into the relationship between epigenetic modifications and energy metabolism, adding a new layer of complexity.

## FUTURE OPPORTUNITIES TO COUNTERACT METABOLIC ABNORMALITIES

Due to the increasing evidence that an imbalance in the energy homeostasis contributes to ALS pathology, targeting the energy metabolism emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy. So far, most approaches concentrate on enhancing the supply of energetic substrates, manipulating metabolic fluxes, or optimizing mitochondrial function (Fig. 2). Recently, some studies suggested that adjusting the levels of specific neuropeptides linked to energy metabolism in the hypothalamus could lead to beneficial effect in mouse models [20,21▪▪,63]. Despite encouraging preclinical results, most metabolic therapeutics failed to translate into an effective treatment. This could be attributed to several factors, including an over-reliance on the mutant SOD1G93A mouse model, inconsistent findings regarding the direction of the associations with ALS pathology, poor study design, inadequate patient characterization at inclusion, confounding factors, absence of early biomarkers and/or lack of knowledge about the dynamics of metabolic alterations. Despite these challenges, there are some promising approaches that warrant further exploration.

> **FIGURE 2: Therapeutic opportunities to treat energy metabolism alterations in ALS. Several therapeutic strategies were tested in preclinical models with encouraging results, but failed to translate to the clinic or were not tested so far. To date, the most promising approach for ALS patients is a dietary intervention with a high-caloric / high-fat diet. ALS, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.**

To date, the most promising approach to compensate for the energetic imbalance is a dietary intervention (Fig. 2). This simple but powerful therapeutic strategy is based on preclinical data demonstrating that a high-fat diet can improve the metabolic status, can ameliorate the disease phenotype, and can extend the survival of mutant SOD1 and TDP-43 mouse models [64,65]. In 2014, the first clinical trial demonstrated that high-caloric, carbohydrate-rich nutrition improves survival [66]. In 2020, a randomized, placebo-controlled, multicenter trial described the beneficial effect of a high-caloric fatty supplementation on weight loss and survival. However, this effect was only observed in fast-progressing ALS patients for reasons that are still unknown [67]. Interestingly, this dietary intervention significantly decreased NfL serum levels and slowed down the decrease of the ALS functional rating scale - revised (ALSFRS-R) score, indicating a neuroprotective effect [68▪]. More recently, Dorst and collaborators tested whether these beneficial effects were due to the additional calories or to the large amount of fat [69]. This pilot study showed that both carbohydrate-rich and fat-rich dietary interventions are safe and induce weight gain. However, weight gain was less pronounced in the carbohydrate-rich group, probably due to more frequent loss of appetite and a feeling of fullness, which decreased food intake. Remarkably, the doubling of dosage in the fat-rich group did not lead to larger weight gain but increased the frequency of gastrointestinal symptoms [69]. The authors highlighted the importance of eating behavior and warn against extreme dosages of fat-rich diets, already associated with poor outcomes in ALS mouse models [70]. While there is currently a lack of clinical evidence, other dietary interventions, such as ketogenic diet and intake of polyunsaturated fatty acids, might hold therapeutic promise [2].

In September 2022, the food and drug administration approved the combination of sodium phenylbutyrate (PB) and taurursodiol (TURSO) as Relyvrio (AMX0035) for the treatment of ALS [71,72]. PB is a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor modulating chromatin state and transcriptional activity [73], while TURSO is a secondary bile acid, also controlling HDAC activity [74]. It is established that epigenetic regulation contributes to cellular metabolic abnormalities in cancer and obesity [75,76]. Fels and collaborators explored this link by treating sporadic ALS fibroblasts with PB-TURSO [77▪]. Bioenergetic alterations are well characterized in these cells [78–80], and the authors observed that PB and TURSO synergistically change the expression of numerous genes and metabolites linked to mitochondrial functions [77▪]. Previously, we demonstrated that restoration of histone acetylation levels using a HDAC inhibitor in a FUS mouse model prolongs survival and reduces weight loss and motor decline [81]. Furthermore, using a multiomics approach, we confirmed that HDAC inhibition restores metabolic alterations linked to glucose and lipid metabolism in the spinal cord of FUS mice [81,82]. Hence, modification of energy metabolism through epigenetic control appears as a promising new therapeutic approach for ALS.

## CONCLUSION

The early onset of metabolic manifestations in the presymptomatic stage suggests a role in the initial cascade of events leading to ALS pathology, potentially before motor neuron degeneration. As a consequence, energy metabolism studies might identify new biomarkers and therapeutic targets. To achieve efficient therapies, gaining a deeper understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms and better defining the presymptomatic stage is crucial. Besides, future clinical trials should consider the heterogeneity of metabolic alterations and the genetic background of patients to propose a personalized treatment. Targeting energy metabolism should be integrated into a more global therapeutic strategy targeting different pathological mechanisms.
