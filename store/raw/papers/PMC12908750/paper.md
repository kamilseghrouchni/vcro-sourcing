# Untargeted metabolomics based on LC-MS and GC-MS reveal metabolic reprogramming and putative biomarkers in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

## Abstract

### Background:

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disorder with unknown etiology. The absence of reliable biochemical and imaging markers often delays diagnosis and limits treatment effectiveness. As metabolic reprogramming is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of ALS, a comprehensive untargeted metabolomics analysis was employed to identify critical metabolic perturbations in ALS and explore novel candidate biomarkers with potential utility in clinical diagnosis.

### Methods:

Plasma from two independent cohorts comprising 399 participants (170 ALS patients, 200 healthy controls, and 29 ALS-unrelated neurological disease controls) was included. Cohort 1 was recruited from Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Ruijin Hospital (April 2020–September 2022), and cohort 2 from Sichuan Academy of Sciences-Sichuan Provincial Hospital, Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiaotong University Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital, and The First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University (October 2022–February 2023). Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based metabolomics approaches were used to identify metabolic alterations and potential diagnostic biomarkers for ALS. Complementary multivariable and univariable statistical approaches were applied to characterize disease-specific metabolic reprogramming in ALS. In addition, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to assess the discriminatory power of differential metabolites, and binary logistic regression analysis was used to construct a multivariate biomarker model.

### Results:

Metabolic changes of ALS were mainly observed in amino acids, fatty acyls , and purines. Inosine and hypoxanthine were found to be the most significantly and critically dysregulated metabolites in ALS. Aminoacyl-transfer ribonucleic acid (tRNA) biosynthesis and amino acid metabolism were regarded as the most significantly perturbed pathways. Across both cohorts, 26 metabolites were consistently changed. Notably, a biomarker panel comprising hypoxanthine, inosine, and trigonelline was constructed using binary logistic regression, achieving excellent diagnostic performance in distinguishing ALS from controls, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.982 in cohort 1 (sensitivity 0.970, specificity 0.940) and 0.934 in cohort 2 (sensitivity 0.942, specificity 0.791).

### Conclusion:

The disturbed pathways and biomarker candidates identified in this study may provide novel insights into ALS pathogenesis and improve diagnostic strategies.

## Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as “Lou Gehrig’s disease”, is a fatal neurodegenerative disease characterized by the progressive degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons in the cerebral cortex, brainstem, and spinal cord.[1] Clinically, ALS manifests with symptoms such as limb weakness, muscle cramps, fasciculations, dysphagia, and dysarthria.[1] Due to the rapid progression of the disease, most patients develop respiratory muscle weakness within three to five years of disease onset and eventually succumb to respiratory failure.[1,2] Despite extensive research, the etiology and pathogenesis of ALS remain unclear. Over 40 genes have been causally implicated in ALS pathogenesis.[2] Furthermore, variability in the initial site of onset, along with complex genetic factors, contributes to the clinical heterogeneity of the disease. Moreover, the lack of reliable biochemical and imaging diagnostic markers substantially impedes early diagnosis and timely intervention in ALS. Therefore, there is an urgent need for more accurate diagnostic indicators in clinical practice.

Metabolomics is an emerging technology that has been extensively used in biomedical research to comprehensively characterize low-molecular-weight compounds (<1500 Da) within biological samples.[3,4] These endogenous molecules represent the downstream products of the expression of genome, transcriptome, and proteome, providing dynamic snapshots of pathophysiological states.[3] Metabolites can also serve as modulators of biological processes and phenotypes by directly regulating gene and protein activities.[4,5] In recent years, metabolomics has proven to be a non-invasive and cost-effective approach for identifying putative biomarkers and elucidating novel pathogenesis of various diseases. Although metabolic abnormalities have been well documented in ALS, most metabolomics studies have predominantly been constrained to preclinical models. Studies involving patient samples are limited, often including only 20–30 cases due to the disease’s low incidence,[6–11] which leads to poor reproducibility and challenges in drawing consistent conclusions. In addition, most of the existing metabolomics studies on ALS have used a single analytical platform,[6–11] limiting both metabolite coverage and reproducibility. Integration of multiple analytical platforms can significantly expand the coverage of metabolite detection. Moreover, previous metabolomics studies have been conducted predominantly in European and American populations, with limited data available in Asian patients, particularly in Chinese populations.

This study aimed to identify key metabolic signatures and pathways associated with ALS, thereby enhancing our understanding of the role of metabolic dysregulation in its pathogenesis. In addition, we sought to identify critical metabolites that could serve as promising diagnostic biomarkers.

## Methods

### Participants

Given the inherent high dimensionality of metabolomic datasets, we estimated the required sample size using MetaboAnalyst’s, https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/ power analysis module. This computational tool supports multivariable-based sample size estimation for population-based and clinical metabolomic studies. Preliminary data from 60 samples (30 healthy controls [HC] and 30 ALS) were used to estimate the average power across all metabolites. The false discovery rate (FDR), representing the significance criterion (alpha level), was set to 0.05. The power curve illustrating predicted average power versus sample size is presented in Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664. The analysis indicated that a sample size of 100 per group would yield a predicted power of 0.881.

In total, 399 plasma samples (170 ALS, 200 HC, and 29 neurological disease controls [NDC]) were collected from four collaborating hospitals. To reduce bias, samples were assigned to either the discovery or validation phase based on recruitment time and center. Cohort 1 (discovery phase) consisted of ALS patients and HC recruited from April 2020 to September 2022 at Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine. Cohort 2 (validation phase) included ALS, HC, and NDC subjects recruited from October 2022 to February 2023 at Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiaotong University Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital, Sichuan Academy of Sciences-Sichuan Provincial Hospital, and the First Affiliated Hospital, Dalian Medical University. The discovery phase exclusively compared ALS patients against matched HC to identify disease-associated metabolic perturbations. In the validation phase, the NDC group was introduced to further evaluate the specificity and diagnostic utility of the identified biomarkers. This staged design enabled rigorous assessment of biomarker specificity and diagnostic utility while controlling for potential confounding effects from disease heterogeneity and comorbid neurological conditions.

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sichuan Academy of Sciences-Sichuan Provincial Hospital (No. 2023-113), and conducted in accordance with The Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) for human research. Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized caregivers.

The ALS patients enrolled in this study were sporadic, with no reported family history of the disease. All patients met the diagnostic criteria for probable laboratory-supported, probable, or definite ALS, as defined by El Escorial Revised Criteria.[12] Patients with severe systemic disorders (e.g., cancer, renal failure, uncontrolled diabetes), other severe neurological or psychiatric disorders, or a family history of ALS were excluded. HC were free from neurological or severe systemic disorders. The age-matched ALS-unrelated NDC group included participants who met the corresponding diagnostic criteria and had no severe systemic disorders or coexisting neurological diseases. Detailed information, including age, sex, and diagnosis of subjects in the NDC group, is given in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C738. Demographic information of ALS patients in each cohort, including age, gender, disease duration, and Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R) score (disease severity) is provided in Table 1. Extended blood biochemistry profiles with comparative analyses of cohort 1 are provided in Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C739 with intergroup comparisons.

### Preparation of plasma samples

Fasting venous blood samples were collected via direct venipuncture into ethylene-diamine tetraacetic acid-containing vacutainer tubes. Plasma was transferred to a sterile centrifuge tube and immediately stored at −80°C until further analysis. Plasma samples were thawed on ice and processed as previously described.[13] A volume of 120 μL of plasma was deproteinized with 480 μL of methanol containing 11 internal standards (ISs) [Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C740]. After vortex and centrifugation at 14,000g for 15 min (4°C), the supernatant was divided into two aliquots and dried in a CentriVap vacuum centrifugal concentrator (Labconco, Kansas City, USA). In addition, quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling 20 μL of plasma from each sample and processed identically to analytical samples. QC samples were analyzed after 10 sample runs to evaluate the reliability of the results.

### Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based metabolomics analysis

Dried samples were reconstituted in 65 μL of 25% (v/v) aqueous methanol. Ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC, Waters, Manchester, UK) coupled with tripleTOF™ 5600 Plus (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) MS system was used for untargeted metabolomics analysis. In electrospray ionization positive (ESI+) mode, the extracts were separated on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C8 1.7 μm column (Waters) with mobile phases containing 0.1% formic acid in water and acetonitrile. In electrosprayionization (ESI)-mode, the extracts were separated on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 1.8 μm column (Waters) with mobile phases containing 6.5 mmol/L NH4HCO3 in water and 95% methanol. Detailed elution gradient and MS parameters were set as described in previous studies.[13,14]

### Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)-based metabolomics analysis

For GC-MS analysis, dried extracts were dissolved in 50 μL methoxyamine solution (20 mg/mL in pyridine) and incubated at 37°C for 1.5 h, followed by the addition of 40 µL N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide for silylation. Analysis was conducted using a GC-MS-QP 2010 PLUS system coupled with an AOC-20i automatic sampler (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) in a full-scan mode. DB-5MS fused-silica capillary column 0.25 µm (J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA) was used to separate the derivatized metabolites. The detailed parameters were followed as previously established.[15]

### Metabolite identification and raw data processing

For the LC-MS data, metabolite identification was conducted using OSI/SMMS software (Dalian Chem Data Solution Information Technology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China), based on retention time, exact mass (mass error tolerance <10 parts per million [ppm]), and MS/MS spectra generated from QC samples against reference data in both in-house and commercial databases.[16] For the GC-MS data, metabolite identification was initially performed via library searches (using NIST 11, Mainlib, Replib, Fiehn, and other libraries) and retention index calibration. The structural confirmation of most metabolites was ultimately validated using authentic chemical standards. Detailed information regarding the metabolite identification was provided in Supplementary Tables 4–6, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C741, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C742, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C743.

LC-MS data processing was performed using PeakView software v1.2.0.3 (AB SCIEX, Framingham, USA) for total ion chromatogram analysis. MarkerView software v1.2.1.1 (AB SCIEX) for peak extraction and alignment, and SCIEX OS software v2.1.6.59781 (AB SCIEX) for manual peak integration. GC-MS data were processed using Postrun Analysis software (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) for peak detection and GC-MS browser software (Shimadzu) for batch processing and peak integration. Metabolites were retained according to the 80% rule.[17] Peak areas were normalized using multiple ISs before further statistical analysis.

### Statistical analysis

SIMCA-P 14.1 software (Umetric AB, Umea, Sweden) was used to perform multivariable analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA). PCA is an unsupervised dimensionality reduction method that identifies intrinsic variation in the dataset without prior knowledge of sample grouping, used to detect outliers, assess data quality, and reveal potential batch effects. PLS-DA is a supervised method that incorporates group information (e.g., ALS vs. controls) to more effectively identify differences between groups, whereas OPLS-DA is an improved version of PLS-DA that separates orthogonal components from the systematic variability and further enhances the model’s interpretability. In both PLS-DA and OPLS-DA models, metabolites with a variable importance in projection (VIP) score >1 were considered to contribute substantially to the separation between groups. To avoid overfitting, we performed permutation tests to validate the robustness of the model.

Intergroup comparisons of age distributions were performed using nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis H test), with continuous variables expressed as medians. Intergroup differences in sex distribution were assessed using the chi-squared tests. The statistical significance of differential metabolites was evaluated using a two-stage analytical framework. First, data distribution characteristics were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk normality tests. Subsequently, non-parametric comparisons between groups were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests, with the Benjamini–Hochberg correction applied to control the FDR from multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics v23.0 (IBM, Newyork, USA). Significant metabolites were defined when they met both multivariate and univariate criteria: a Benjamini–Hochberg corrected P <0.05 from the Mann–Whitney U tests, and a VIP >1.0 from the PLS-DA component loadings. Heat maps were constructed using Multi Experiment Viewer v4.7.4 (J. Craig Venter Institute, CA, USA). Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the Pathway Analysis module of the MetaboAnalyst 5.0 platform (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca). Metabolic pathways were constructed using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (https://www.kegg.jp/). The Debiased Sparse Partial Correlation (DSPC) network was constructed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 and visualized by Cytoscape v3.9.0 (Cytoscape Consortium, CA, USA). Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to establish potential metabolite panels, and the predictive performance of the model was assessed by estimating the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), which is commonly used for assessing the discriminative performance of prediction models by SPSS Statistics v23.0 (IBM, Newyork, USA). Box-whisker plots were generated by GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA), and Mann–Whitney U/Kruskal–Wallis tests were performed, followed by uncorrected Dunn’s test.

## Results

### Participants and data quality assessment

A total of 399 participants were enrolled and divided into two independent cohorts [Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. In the discovery phase, 101 ALS patients and 100 HC were included. In the validation phase, 69 ALS, 100 HC, and 29 NDC were enrolled. No significant differences in age or sex were observed between the groups within each cohort. The distribution of disease duration and ALSFRS-R scores in all patients is visualized in Supplementary Figure 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664.

To assess data quality and visualize overall metabolic profiles between ALS patients and HC, PCA models were performed. QC samples clustered tightly in both cohorts, suggesting data robustness and reproducibility [Supplementary Figure 4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. After instrumental analysis, raw data processing, and structure identification, 382 metabolites from LC-MS and 142 from GC-MS were identified [Supplementary Tables 7 and 8, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C744, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C745], respectively.

### Discovery of metabolic signatures of ALS

To investigate the metabolic changes in patients with ALS, we compared the metabolic profiles of ALS patients and HC. As depicted in Supplementary Figure 4A and B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664, a clear discrepancy was observed in the plasma metabolome between ALS patients and HC on the PCA score plots, suggesting the presence of remarkable differences in endogenous metabolites between the two groups. To identify differential metabolites associated with ALS, we performed PLS-DA, and a Mann–Whitney U test, with FDR correction for multiple comparisons.

Using rigorous threshold criteria (FDR <0.05, VIP >1), we identified 126 differential metabolites in ALS compared to HC by LC-MS [Supplementary Figure 5A and B, Supplementary Table 9, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C746]. To explore the potential metabolic interactions of these metabolites, a DSPC network of the 126 metabolites was constructed using Metaboanalyst 5.0 and visualized with Cytoscape v3.7.1 software. The DSPC network analysis generated 4 subnetworks, comprising 83 metabolites, with subnetwork 1 consisting of 74 metabolites. Amino acids, fatty acids, and carnitines showed dense interactions, whereas phospholipids displayed independent partial correlations [Supplementary Figure 5C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. Notably, inosine and hypoxanthine were positioned at the center of the correlation network diagrams, suggesting their role as the primary hubs with intensive interactions [Supplementary Figure 5C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. In GC-MS analysis, a total of 41 metabolites were differentially expressed between ALS and HC (FDR <0.05, VIP >1, Supplementary Figure 5D and E, Supplementary Table 10, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C747). A subnetwork comprising 39 differential metabolites with 166 edges was generated by DSPC analysis. Consistent with results from LC-MS, amino acids identified by GC-MS analysis also demonstrated dense interactions. Notably, trans-4-hydroxyproline, the indicator of muscle damage, had the most edges connecting to other metabolites within the network [Supplementary Figure 5F, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664].

By integrating the outputs of two analytical platforms, we found that amino acids (16.77%) and fatty acyls (24.55%) constituted a significant proportion of these differential metabolites, followed by carbohydrates, steroids, and purines [Supplementary Figure 6A, Supplementary Tables 9 and 10, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C746, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C747]. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement in aminoacyl-transfer ribonucleic acid (tRNA) biosynthesis, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism and phenylalanine metabolism [Supplementary Figure 6B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664], with phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis and linoleic acid metabolism showing the most pronounced perturbations [Supplementary Figure 6C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. To further visualize the interconnectivity between the enriched pathways, a pathway network was constructed. The connectivity network of 31 interrelated pathways (defined by >25% shared metabolites) demonstrated that multiple amino acid pathways were highly interconnected through ammonia recycling. Furthermore, these amino acid metabolism-related pathways also exhibited complex interrelationships with glucose metabolism [Supplementary Figure 6D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664].

### Validation of ALS-related metabolic signatures using an independent cohort

To further confirm ALS-related metabolites, an independent cohort including ALS, HC, and NDC was enrolled and subjected to both LC-MS and GC-MS analysis. We subsequently constructed an OPLS-DA model with Pareto scaling to delineate ALS-related metabolic signatures and assess their potential as biomarkers. In line with the results of cohort 1, OPLS-DA models demonstrated distinct separation between ALS and HC in both LC-MS and GC-MS datasets [Supplementary Figure 7A and B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. Model validation diagrams via permutation test (400 times) confirmed model robustness with no evidence of overfitting [Supplementary Figure 7C and D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. Using identical thresholds (FDR <0.05, VIP >1), we validated 53 significant metabolites by LC-MS and 30 by GC-MS [Supplementary Figure 8A and B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. Subsequent pathway enrichment analysis in cohort 2 recapitulated key dysregulations observed in cohort 1, notably in aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and phenylalanine metabolism [Supplementary Figure 8C and D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. Additional perturbations were observed in alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism pathways. In addition, S-plot analysis identified hypoxanthine, inosine, phosphate, citrate, pyruvate, choline, etc., as high-impact discriminative biomarkers of ALS, with their distribution patterns in the upper-right and lower-left quadrants, demonstrating significant contributions to group separation [Supplementary Figure 9A and B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664].

### Validation of the specificity of ALS differential metabolites through NDC

To further validate the specificity of the candidate markers, we investigated the metabolic differences between ALS and NDC. The OPLS-DA model also showed a clear metabolic discrepancy between ALS and NDC across both LC-MS and GC-MS platforms [Supplementary Figure 10, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. Consistent with prior observations, differential metabolites between ALS and NDC were primarily phospholipids, fatty acyl, and amino acids, with purines such as hypoxanthine and inosine also differentially expressed [Supplementary Figure 11A–C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. Enrichment and pathway analyses showed that aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, and phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan were the most perturbed pathways in ALS compared to NDC [Supplementary Figure 11D and E, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664], further indicating that aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and amino acid dysregulation exert important influence on the pathogenesis of ALS.

### Dysregulated amino acid metabolism in ALS

Apart from perturbations in glycerophospholipids and fatty acyl metabolism, ALS exhibits pronounced dysregulation of amino acid homeostasis, even when compared to NDC [Supplementary Figure 11C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. Integrative pathway analysis identified that aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism were the most perturbed pathways in ALS [Supplementary Figures 6B, 8C, and 11E, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. These findings collectively indicated that systemic amino acid dysregulation must be a cardinal metabolic hallmark of ALS pathogenesis. As mechanistically illustrated in Supplementary Figure 12A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664, amino acids serve as critical substrates fueling energy metabolism, regulating glucose utilization, purine cycling, and ultimately adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production. Quantification of energy-linked amino acids across independent cohorts revealed significant elevations in ALS compared to HC in two cohorts [Supplementary Figure 12B–O, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. When compared with NDC, the upregulation trend was less pronounced. This might be related to the fact that ALS shares certain similar characteristics of metabolic changes with other neurological disorders.

### Discriminant model establishment and validation

To identify robust biomarkers, 26 metabolites that exhibited significant alterations in ALS patients’ plasma compared to HC and NDC in two cohorts were selected as candidate markers [Supplementary Figure 13A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. A heatmap revealed consistent upregulation of hypoxanthine, inosine, free fatty acid (FFA) 20:4, and multiple amino acids [Supplementary Figure 13B and C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664].

ROC analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic performance of each metabolite in cohort 1. The results revealed that hypoxanthine, inosine, lyxosylamine, trigonelline, and glutamate exhibited the highest discriminatory power in cohort 1 [Table 2]. Subsequently, a binary logistic regression model was used to construct a biomarker panel. Among the five candidate metabolites, hypoxanthine, inosine, and trigonelline were selected as the most promising combination. ROC curves based on classification models using these three metabolites were generated to distinguish ALS from HC and NDC in cohorts 1 and 2, respectively [Figure 1A and B]. The model demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance, achieving an AUC of 0.982 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.968–0.997) in cohort 1, with a sensitivity of 0.970 and specificity of 0.940. In cohort 2, the model yielded an AUC of 0.934 (95% CI: 0.902–0.966), with a sensitivity of 0.942 and specificity of 0.791 [Figure 1A and B, Supplementary Table 11, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C748]. And the levels of these three metabolites significantly increased in the plasma of ALS patients, particularly notable were hypoxanthine and inosine [Figure 1C and D]. Although the biomarker combination demonstrated a high AUC, its diagnostic performance was not significantly better than that achieved by hypoxanthine alone [Table 2 and Supplementary Tables 11, 12, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C748, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C749], which underscores the value of hypoxanthine as a reliable biomarker for ALS.

## Discussion

This study implemented integrated LC-MS- and GC-MS-based metabolomics to characterize systemic metabolic disturbances and identify candidate metabolic biomarkers of ALS. Across two independent cohorts, 524 metabolites with definite structural annotations were detected. Two independent cohorts, including a total of 399 specimens, were collected and analyzed, representing a study with a relatively large number of ALS patients and comprehensive coverage of metabolites to date.

Our study revealed significant perturbations in aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and amino acid metabolism in ALS, with hypoxanthine and inosine as central nodes, which aligns with our previous report suggesting dysregulation of purine metabolism might play a critical role in the development of ALS.[18] A biomarker panel comprising hypoxanthine, inosine, and trigonelline achieved excellent diagnostic performance in distinguishing ALS from controls. Notably, the consistent diagnostic performance of hypoxanthine underscores its promise as a reliable ALS biomarker, though validation in larger multi-center cohorts is needed.

Nutritional deterioration is a key feature of ALS, with impaired glucose homeostasis contributing to disordered energy metabolism.[19–21] The hypermetabolic state accounts for weight loss in ALS, possibly through upregulated gluconeogenesis, lipolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and protein catabolism.[22,23] There are close pathophysiological interconnections between amino acid metabolism and bioenergetic regulation [Supplementary Figure 12A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C664]. In the presence of impaired glycogen degradation, enhanced gluconeogenesis is required to maintain blood glucose levels and meet the body’s energy demands.[22] Elevated circulating amino acids indicate accelerated proteolysis in ALS, with skeletal muscle serving as the main reservoir, ultimately leading to muscle wasting.[24–26]

Amino acids are essential for maintaining the central nervous system (CNS) homeostasis due to their involvement in excitatory neurotransmission, immunomodulation, and neurotransmitter synthesis.[27] Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), including leucine, isoleucine, and valine, are essential for regulating energy homeostasis.[28] Catabolism of BCAAs would generate glutamate,[29] but excessive glutamate induced by high levels of BCAAs may trigger excitotoxic neuronal damage.[30,31] We observed elevated nonessential amino acids, including aspartate, serine, and alanine. Astrocyte-derived serine functions as a major co-agonist of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors to regulate neuronal homeostasis and modulate neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity.[32]

Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, which supplies substrates for ribosomal mRNA translation, was found to be markedly dysregulated in ALS in our study.[33,34] This pathway is critically governed by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (ARSs), which ligate amino acids to tRNAs and act as metabolic sensors regulating protein synthesis to maintain cellular homeostasis.[34] Aberrant aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis can disrupt metabolome-proteome equilibrium, subsequently triggering epigenetic and genetic changes. Dominant mutations in ARSs could induce peripheral motor axon degeneration via impaired translation and cellular stress.[35] Notably, tRNA overexpression could rescue protein synthesis deficits and alleviate peripheral neuropathy by enhancing corresponding ribosomal tRNA supply,[36] implicating ARS dysfunction in neuronal degeneration and highlighting tRNA-targeted therapies as potential interventions. Critically, recent studies have demonstrated that C9orf72 repeat RNA disrupts phenylalanine-tRNA synthetase, thereby impairing phenylalanine incorporation into the proteome and depleting phenylalanine-rich proteins in ALS.[37] Importantly, evidence showed that mutant SOD1 had an aberrant interaction with lysyl-tRNA synthetase,[38,39] suggesting convergent disruption of ARS function across genetically distinct ALS subtypes. These evidences highlight the significance of further studies in aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis in ALS.

The marked elevation of hypoxanthine and inosine in ALS patients may indicate systemic dysregulation of purine turnover. Both metabolites are obligatory intermediates of the purine catabolic cascade, which ends with xanthine oxidase (XO)-mediated uric acid formation as the final step of ATP degradation.[40] Hypoxanthine accumulation, previously documented in ischemic/hypoxic conditions and aerobic exercising muscle, reflects aberrant ATP depletion,[41–43] and indicates aberrant purine breakdown in muscle.[44] Furthermore, existing data suggested that reliance on purine metabolism increases with glycogen depletion.[45] Low uric acid is linked to higher ALS risk and faster progression,[46–48] and mitochondrial dysfunction–induced ROS may impair XO activity, thereby reducing uric acid,[49] and further driving hypoxanthine/inosine accumulation in ALS.

In the CNS, elevated hypoxanthine might promote oxidative stress responses, exert toxic effects on microglia and astrocytes, and induce neuroinflammation.[50] Concomitantly, hypoxanthine accumulation disrupts mitochondrial bioenergetics by impairing tricarboxylic acid cycle flux and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, thereby triggering apoptosis.[51] Excess hypoxanthine/inosine may further compromise purine salvage pathways, leading to ATP/ adenosine diphosphate (ADP) imbalance, overactivation of adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase signaling, and dysregulation of autophagy, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial homeostasis.[52,53] Allopurinol, an inhibitor of XO, exerts antioxidant effects[54–56] and enhances hypoxanthine recycling in previous studies.[56,57] Preclinical validation in G1H-G93A transgenic mice demonstrates that allopurinol analogs delay disease onset,[57] emphasizing the crucial role of purine homeostasis in ALS progression.

The robust diagnostic power of hypoxanthine highlights the crucial roles of purine dysregulation in ALS, consistent with our previous findings that dysregulation of purine metabolism, manifested as accelerated purine nucleotide catabolism, represents a typical metabolic alteration in SOD1G93A mice.[18] Critically, purine metabolism dysregulation emerges as an incipient event in ALS pathogenesis, preceding neuropathology and further emphasizing the significance of hypoxanthine and inosine,[18] warranting further study to identify molecular triggers and downstream cellular consequences of these metabolic shifts in ALS. Unraveling these pathogenic cascades may not only help reveal novel ALS biomarkers but also provide valuable insights into ALS pathogenesis and future therapeutic strategies.

This study used integrated LC-MS and GC-MS platforms to comprehensively characterize ALS-associated metabolic reprogramming. Using complementary discovery and validation cohorts with stringent differential metabolite screening criteria, we identified conserved and stable metabolic signatures and provided potential diagnostic biomarker candidates for ALS. By leveraging large sample size and broad metabolite coverage, this work addressed the knowledge gap in clinical metabolomics of ALS and enable robust profiling of ALS-associated metabolic perturbations.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. The genetic backgrounds of participants were undefined, limiting assessment of genotype effects on metabolite profiles despite ALS complex genetic architecture. Future large-scale, multi-center studies with adequate statistical power are needed to systematically resolve genotype-specific metabolic signatures of ALS. Additionally, longitudinal studies are also required to validate the utility of hypoxanthine as preclinical biomarkers.

In summary, our findings reveal that dysregulation of aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, as well as perturbations in amino acid metabolism, might play pivotal roles in the progression of ALS. Furthermore, we emphasize the potential significance of hypoxanthine in ALS diagnosis and pathogenesis. Further investigations elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the dysregulation of aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and purine metabolism in ALS may offer valuable insights for the development of therapeutic strategies.
