# Multi-marker discovery for mild cognitive impairment in metabolomics using machine learning with a global surrogate model via partial least squares

## Abstract

### Introduction

Dementia can be prevented through early intervention; hence, there is an urgent need for biomarkers to help diagnose mild cognitive impairment (MCI).

### Objectives

We aimed to develop a multi-marker panel composed of plasma metabolites to aid in the diagnosis of MCI.

### Methods

We performed an analysis of a multi-marker panel of MCI metabolites using a random forest algorithm with variable selection methods and a global surrogate with principal component analysis and partial least squares (PLS).

### Results

By incorporating variable selection methods, we constructed a predictive model that demonstrated robust performance, with an AUC of approximately 0.85 in both cross-validation and test evaluations, using only five metabolites (methionine, quinic acid, hypoxanthine, O-acetylcarnitine, and 2-oxoglutaric acid). However, owing to the limited number of selected metabolites, it was challenging to infer the biological meaning of this multi-marker panel. To interpret this multi-marker panel biologically, we constructed a global surrogate model using PLS. By examining the PLS loadings corresponding to the scores with intergroup differences, we identified a relationship between 14 metabolites involved in neuronal energy metabolism and neurotransmission. This suggests that the multi-marker panel constructed in this study is related to abnormalities in energy metabolism and neurotransmission in patients with MCI.

### Conclusion

The method used in this study may be broadly applicable for analyzing multi-marker panels of metabolites and their biological interpretation. This study included an independent validation, and further larger-scale studies using additional external cohorts are warranted to confirm the generalizability of this approach.

## Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent cause of dementia, with amnesic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) serving as its pre-disease state. As AD remains the largest unmet medical need among all neurological diseases, there is an urgent need to detect it at an early stage of MCI (Cai et al., 2012; Citron, 2010). The pathogenesis of AD involves many biochemical processes, including amyloid precursor protein metabolism, tau protein phosphorylation, impaired energetics, oxidative stress, membrane lipid dysregulation, and inflammation (de la Monte & Tong, 2014; Procaccini et al., 2016). Accumulating evidence suggests a strong correlation between metabolic changes and neurodegeneration. Moreover, epidemiological studies have demonstrated an association between neurodegeneration and metabolic dysfunction (Hassing et al. 2009; Xu et al. 2011). Therefore, a comprehensive overview of metabolic changes in AD may provide new insights into the diagnosis and treatment of AD.

Metabolomics provides global metabolic profiling to understand the biochemical state of a disease (Kaddurah-Daouk & Krishnan, 2009; Patti et al., 2012), and facilitates network analysis approaches to identify key metabolic drivers in disease pathophysiology (Soltow et al., 2010). However, owing to the varied pathogenesis of MCI, its diagnosis is characterized by high sensitivity but low specificity, and relying on the identification of key metabolic drivers is inadequate. In addition, machine learning is often used to build models to predict outcomes from metabolome data; however, machine learning generates a black-box model that requires further interpretation. In this study, we employed an approach that uses a global surrogate model (Molnar, 2024) with principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares (PLS), frequently used methods in metabolomics to develop multi-marker panel for MCI. We identified surrogate markers using random forests and variable selection and built a highly accurate multi-marker panel with fewer metabolites and then applied a surrogate model to it. This allowed us to examine a wide range of metabolites important for interpretation and to pinpoint statistically significant ones using hypothesis testing. Thus, our method is widely applicable for the interpretation of multi-marker panel using machine learning and can be effective for biomarker development in diseases with diverse pathologies, such as MCI.

## Materials and methods

### Participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Hirosaki University Graduate School of Medicine (Hirosaki, Japan) with No. 2024-012 and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All the participants provided written informed consent.

Participants were recruited from two cohorts in Hirosaki City, Aomori Prefecture, Japan. One cohort was from the Iwaki Health Promotion Project 2017 (IHPP), which employed the same sample set as the 25 MCI and 25 control participants included in a previous study (Tatara et al., 2023). In the IHPP study, all 25 participants who were followed up as MCI cases were included in the MCI group. To minimize potential confounding, age- and sex-matched controls without a history of neurological diseases or diabetes were selected using the nearest neighbor matching method. The IHPP is a community-based study conducted since 2005 in the Iwaki district of Hirosaki City, aimed at preventing lifestyle-related diseases and improving the quality of life (Nakaji et al., 2021). There were 1,073 participants (440 males and 633 females), with an average age of 52.7 ± 15.3 (median: 53). MCI was diagnosed at Hirosaki University Hospital through a thorough examination based on the National Institute on Aging- Alzheimer’s Association criteria (Albert et al., 2011; McKhann et al., 2011) following primary screening for amnesia in the IHPP conducted from 2014 to 2017 (Nakahata et al., 2021). The second cohort was the Hirosaki Dementia cohort (Iki-Iki study), which included 15 patients with MCI and 15 control participants. In the Iki-Iki study, 15 participants with MCI were selected after excluding those with medical conditions that could act as potential confounders, including stroke, depression, Parkinson’s disease, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes. To reduce confounding effects, age- and sex-matched controls were identified using the nearest neighbor matching method to achieve comparable distributions of these variables between groups. The Iki-Iki study is a multisite, population-based, prospective cohort study on dementia. It was designed to enroll approximately 1,000 community-dwelling residents aged 65 years or older in Hirosaki City. There were 1,090 participants (404 males and 686 females), with an average age of 70.0 ± 4.3 (median: 69). The diagnosis of dementia was adjudicated by an endpoint adjudication committee using standard criteria and clinical information in accordance with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 3rd Revised Edition (Ninomiya et al., 2020).

### Blood sampling and metabolite extraction

Blood samples were collected from all participants before breakfast consumption. After centrifugation at 1,500 ×g and 4 °C for 15 min, collected plasmas were separately stored at − 80 °C. Metabolite extraction and metabolome analysis were conducted at the Human Metabolome Technologies (HMT, Tsuruoka, Yamagata, Japan) (Yamamoto et al., 2021). Briefly, 50 µL of plasma was added to 450 µL of methanol containing internal standards (Solution ID: H3304-1002, HMT, Inc., Tsuruoka, Japan) at 0 °C to inactivate enzymes. The extract solution was thoroughly mixed with 500 µL of chloroform and 200 µL of Milli-Q water and centrifuged at 2300 ×g and 4 °C for 5 min; 350 µL of the upper aqueous layer was centrifugally filtered through a Millipore 5-kDa cutoff filter to remove the proteins. The filtrate was centrifuged at 9,100 ×g and 4 °C, the pellet resuspended in 50 µL of Milli-Q water, and then used for metabolome analysis at the HMT.

### Metabolome analysis

Metabolome analysis was conducted according to HMT’s Basic Scan package using capillary electrophoresis time-of-flight mass spectrometry (CE-TOFMS), based on previously described methods (Ohashi et al., 2008; Ooga et al., 2011). Briefly, CE-TOF-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent CE capillary electrophoresis system equipped with an Agilent 6230 TOF-MS (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). The systems were controlled by Agilent MassHunter Workstation Data Acquisition (Agilent Technologies) and connected by a fused silica capillary (50 μm i.d.×80cm total length) with commercial electrophoresis buffer (H3301-1001 and I3302-1023 for cation and anion analyses, respectively, HMT) as the electrolyte. The spectrometer was scanned from m/z 50 to 1,000 and peaks were extracted using MasterHands automatic integration software (Keio University, Tsuruoka, Yamagata, Japan) to obtain peak information, including m/z, peak area, and migration time (MT) (Sugimoto et al., 2010). Signal peaks corresponding to isotopomers, adduct ions, and other ions of known metabolites were excluded, and the remaining peaks were annotated according to the HMT metabolite database based on their m/z values and MTs. The areas of the annotated peaks were normalized to internal standards and sample amounts to obtain the relative concentration of each metabolite. A total of 353 metabolites were quantified based on one-point calibration with authentic external standards. Stable isotope-labeled compounds or compound-specific internal standards were not used.

### Statistical analysis

For the analysis of multi-marker panel, we employed the random forest algorithm (Breiman, 2001) as the machine learning method. Metabolites that were detected in more than 50% of the total samples across the combined IHPP and Iki-Iki cohorts were included in the analysis, resulting in a total of 96 metabolites. Variable selection was performed using the mean decrease accuracy, mean decrease gini, area under the curve (AUC), Variable Selection Using Random Forests (VSURF) (Robin Genuer, 2015), and Boruta (Kursa, 2010).

As part of our evaluation strategy, we initially trained the model using the IHPP cohort as training data and evaluated it on the Iki-Iki cohort as a test set, in order to assess generalizability across cohorts. However, due to substantial differences in sample handling conditions and metabolite distributions between the two cohorts (see Results section), this approach yielded poor test performance. Therefore, in the subsequent analysis, we merged both cohorts and divided into 75% training and 25% test subsets for model development and evaluation. At this point, stratified random split was used to maintain the ratio of MCI and control groups. All model training, cross-validation, and variable selection steps (including VSURF and Boruta) were conducted solely within the training set. The test set was held out entirely and used only for final model evaluation, serving as an independent test set that was not involved in any model tuning or feature selection procedures.

Random forest modeling was implemented using the caret package in R. For the initial model, we set `ntree` to 500 and used the default tuning procedure to explore values of `mtry`. For the final model, we performed an extensive grid search, varying `ntree` from 1 to 500 and `mtry` from 1 to 96 (in increments of 1), to evaluate model performance across different parameter combinations. To clarify, model selection and parameter optimization (including the grid search for mtry and ntree) were conducted exclusively within the training data using cross-validation. The test set was evaluated only once using the final trained model, providing an unbiased estimate of predictive performance and ensuring its complete independence from the model tuning process.

Because the final predictive model was constructed using a small number of selected features to avoid overfitting—especially given the limited sample size—the number of metabolites directly available for biological interpretation was limited. To complement this and provide a broader overview of metabolic variation, we applied a global surrogate model using PCA and PLS. These methods allow for visualization of underlying metabolic patterns through interpretable loadings. PLS was selected not as an alternative classifier but as a well-established and widely used tool in metabolomics, suitable for exploring latent structures behind high-confidence predictions from the random forest model.

The GSM was constructed using class labels predicted by a 2-fold cross-validated random forest model. Subsequently, the GSM was applied to a subset of samples exhibiting high prediction accuracy in the model. The approximation accuracy of the surrogate model was assessed by calculating the R-squared (R2) value between the inverse Mahalanobis distance calculated from the principal component scores or PLS scores for the control and MCI groups and the prediction probabilities from the random forest mode. All calculations were performed using R ver. 4.2.2. The random forest model was implemented using the Caret package (Kuhn, 2008), and variable selection was conducted using the VSURF and Boruta packages. PCA (Yamamoto et al., 2014) and PLS (Yamamoto, 2017) were performed using the loading package, whereas all other analyses were conducted using in-house programs. For PLS analysis, we focused on the first two components (PLS1 and PLS2). Statistically significant metabolites were selected based on a significance level of 5% (p < 0.05) for GSM-PLS loading. For multiple testing correction, q-values were calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

## Results and discussion

Forty adults with MCI and controls enrolled in the IHPP 2017 and Iki-Iki studies 2017 in Hirosaki were included in this study (Table 1). Mean ages of the MCI and control groups were 74.8 ± 6.2 and 72.9 ± 5.6 years, respectively. Mean years of education were 11.6 ± 2.4 and 12.5 ± 2.6 for MCI and controls, respectively, with no significant difference. Both Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Wechsler Memory Scale-Revised (WMS-R) scores were significantly higher in the control group than in the MCI group with t-test p-values < 0.001. The major differences in MCI screening between the IHPP and Iki-Iki studies was the age of the participants: IHPP participants were aged 60 years or older, while the Iki-Iki recruited participants 65 years or older with the MMSE delayed regression scores being 2 and 4 points or less, respectively. The IHPP also determined the WMS-R score threshold for each year of education and included both subjective and objective cognitive impairment in its criteria.

We analyzed metabolome data from two cohort studies, the IHPP and the Iki-Iki study, in Hirosaki City. As a preliminary trial, the analysis aimed to train a model using data from the IHPP as the training dataset, and the Iki-Iki study was used as the test dataset, to evaluate its predictive accuracy. Using random forest, the 2-fold cross-validation AUC of the training data was calculated as 0.866 (true positives (TP) = 27, false positives (FP) = 7, false negatives (FN) = 4, true negatives (TN) = 18), whereas the AUC of the test data was 0.493 (TP = 10, FP = 10, FN = 5, TN = 5), indicating that the test data did not achieve high predictive accuracy (Fig. 1).

The low predictive accuracy of the test data may be attributed to overfitting of the training dataset and poor reproducibility of key metabolites in the model. To investigate the underlying factors, we performed a PCA, which revealed subtle differences between the IHPP and Iki-Iki studies along the first principal component (Supplementary Fig. S1). These findings suggest that certain metabolites vary between cohorts, rather than merely reflecting the differences between patients with MCI and controls. An example of a cohort-specific metabolite is adenosine diphosphate (ADP; Supplementary Fig. S2), ranking 18th in variable importance according to the mean decrease accuracy. ADP showed significant differences in values between the IHPP and Iki-Iki studies, unrelated to the distinction between the control and MCI groups, indicating low reproducibility. The large differences in ADP levels between the two studies could be attributed to various factors, such as differences in background information, including sex and age. While no significant differences in sex were found between the IHPP and Iki-Iki cohorts, a significant difference in age was observed between the MCI and control groups in the Iki-Iki study (Table 1). Several reports have indicated relationships between metabolite fluctuations and age, suggesting that age-related effects may play a contributing role (Menni et al., 2013). However, differences in ADP levels cannot be fully explained by age alone. We speculate that there were larger sources of variation rather than mere differences in the background information.

In particular, metabolites are known to fluctuate significantly depending on conditions such as sample collection method, and centrifugation and storage conditions (Kamlage et al., 2014). Therefore, to identify the metabolites that differed between the IHPP and Iki-Iki studies, we performed the Mann-Whitney test (Supplementary Table S1). A total of 47 metabolites showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05), with the top three most statistically significant metabolites being ethanolamine phosphate (p = 1.938 × 10− 13, q = 1.862 × 10− 11), aspartate (p = 4.880 × 10− 12, q = 1.774 × 10− 10), and taurine (p = 5.544 × 10− 12, q = 1.774 × 10− 10). These metabolites showed significant differences between the two studies regardless of the distinction between the control and MCI groups, suggesting that their variability may depend on the sample collection conditions. This study also revealed that several metabolites differed significantly between the two studies, highlighting the need to carefully select training and test samples as well as to filter metabolites in an analysis of multi-marker panel in metabolomics.

Because reproducibility could not be achieved when the IHPP was used as the training data and the Iki-Iki study as the test data, we reanalyzed the data by using 3/4 of both the IHPP and Iki-Iki studies as the training data and the remaining 1/4 as the test data. The random forest analysis yielded an AUC of 0.636 (TP = 26, FP = 15, FN = 10, TN = 16) for the 2-fold cross-validation of the training data and an AUC of 0.650 (TP = 5, FP = 5, FN = 5, TN = 4) for the test data. Although low, similar levels of predictive accuracy were achieved in both cases (Supplementary Fig. S3). These results suggest that the issue of significantly reduced predictive accuracy of the test data relative to the training data has been partially addressed. In addition, after performing variable selection using seven methods (mean decrease accuracy, mean decrease Gini, AUC, VSURF, Boruta, mean decrease accuracy backward elimination, and mean decrease gradient backward elimination), the highest predictive accuracy was achieved with mean decrease accuracy backward elimination. The AUC for the training and test data were 0.857 (TP = 29, FP = 3, FN = 7, TN = 28) and 0.856 (TP = 8, FP = 4, FN = 2, TN = 5), respectively (Fig. 2). The optimized parameters for the random forest model were `ntree` = 456 and `mtry` = 1. Among the various feature selection methods tested within the training set using cross-validation, the mean decrease accuracy backward elimination yielded the best performance. The metabolites used in this model were methionine, quinic acid, hypoxanthine, O-acetylcarnitine, and 2-oxoglutaric acid.

In the random forest model with variable selection, we adopted a predictive model using the five metabolites with the highest predictive accuracy among those evaluated. However, because additional metabolites related to these five metabolites may fluctuate in a similar manner, we applied global surrogate models using PCA (GSM-PCA) and PLS (GSM-PLS) to identify these metabolites from the predictive model. While interpretability methods such as SHAP (Lundberg, 2017) are widely used, we chose GSM-PLS for its compatibility with commonly used multivariate analysis techniques in metabolomics, allowing for more intuitive interpretation through loadings. When using GSM-PCA for the predictive model, no group differences were observed in the first and second principal component scores, whereas partial group differences were observed with GSM-PLS (Fig. 3). However, when examining the R-squared (R²) values, which represent the model’s approximation accuracy, GSM-PCA showed R² = 0.0265, and GSM-PLS showed R² = 0.143, indicating that neither model achieved high accuracy. Therefore, to investigate how R² changed based on the probability output of the sampled data, we calculated R² values for the 50 resampled datasets (Fig. 4).

R² showed a tendency to increase as the probability threshold increased. Therefore, we set the probability output at the value where R² first exceeded a median of 0.8 as the threshold. While GSM-PCA did not achieve a median R² above 0.8, GSM-PLS first exceeded a median R² of 0.8 at a probability output of 0.8, demonstrating excellent approximation accuracy for samples with high prediction reliability. This suggests that samples with a high probability of output are easier to classify owing to clear differences between groups, facilitating accurate approximation in the surrogate model. However, samples with a low probability of output are likely to exhibit ambiguous group differences, making it challenging to approximate using the surrogate model. This outcome was expected because the probability output from the random forest model measures the prediction confidence for each sample.

Next, we set the probability output threshold at 0.8 and applied GSM-PLS only to samples with prediction probabilities > 0.8, obtained through resampling (Fig. 5). As shown in Fig. 4, the GSM-PLS identified distinct group differences in the first PLS component. Subsequently, statistically significant metabolites were examined based on the loadings of the first PLS component. The significant metabolites (p < 0.05) were methionine, quinic acid, hypoxanthine, 2-oxoglutaric acid, alanine, phenylalanine, sarcosine, tyrosine, guanidoacetic acid, asparagine, citrulline, O-acetylcarnitine, N2-phenylacetylglutamine, and leucine (Table 2, Supplementary Table S2). Notably, all the five metabolites selected by random forest—methionine, quinic acid, hypoxanthine, O-acetylcarnitine, and 2-oxoglutaric acid—were included.

To derive biological inferences regarding these 14 metabolites, we conducted pathway enrichment analysis. Only the shikimate pathway was significantly affected (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S3). In bacteria, the shikimate pathway facilitates the conversion of quinic acid to shikimic acid (Farag et al., 2020). Although the shikimic acid pathway is expected to be affected by intestinal bacteria, it was difficult to interpret the results for the host metabolic pathway. Therefore, we focused on determining the association of individual metabolites with MCI.

Guanidoacetic acid is synthesized from glycine and arginine by glycine amidinotransferases (GATM) in the brain, kidneys, pancreas, liver, endocrine tissues, and skin. Guanidoacetic acid is delivered to the liver and converted into creatine by guanidinoacetate N-methyltransferase (GAMT). Guanidoacetic acid is neurotoxic and accumulates exclusively during GAMT deficiency. Chronically increased guanidoacetic acid could also cause downregulation of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABAA) receptors, and thus desensitization of GABAergic neurons (Gordon, 2010). Acetylcarnitine is an acetylated form of the amino acid derivative l-carnitine that assists in mitochondrial fatty acid metabolism. In human plasma and tissues, acetylcarnitine is the predominant acylated ester of carnitine, is an amino acid derivative of lysine and methionine in the kidneys, liver, and brain. It is currently under investigation as a potential treatment for numerous conditions, including neuropathy, depression, and dementia (Salvioli & Neri, 1994; Virmani & Binienda, 2004). 2-Oxoglutaric acid is an important intermediate in the tricarboxylic acid cycle. It is converted to glutamic acid and provides a substrate for GABA, aspartic acid, and alanine. 2-Oxoglutaric acid also plays a crucial role in the removal of reactive oxygen species in the tricarboxylic acid cycle. Sarcosine is a metabolic intermediate in the conversion of choline to glycine, and is generated by the metabolism of ingested choline, betaine, glycine, and methionine and is ultimately degraded to glycine. It is currently being investigated as a potential treatment for schizophrenia (Chang et al., 2020). Hypoxanthine is a reaction intermediate of adenosine metabolism and nucleic acid biogenesis via the salvage pathway. Quinic acid is a metabolite derived from the diet, as it is widely found in plants (Farag et al., 2020). Since MCI covers a wide variety of clinical conditions, it is difficult to discuss the relationship between the metabolic changes identified in this study and cognitive function. Instead, since the metabolic changes were close to central metabolism, we believe that a signature common to MCI has been captured. Therefore, the multiple-metabolite surrogate markers employed in this study represent promising strategies for predicting MCI.

In a previous study, we measured identical plasma samples of IHPP by LC-MS/MS using the AbsoluteIDQ P400 kit (Biocrates Inc.) and reported lower levels of plasma amino acids (alanine, arginine, asparagine, glutamate, histidine, isoleucine, leucine, methionine, phenylalanine, proline, threonine, tryptophan, tyrosine, and valine) in individuals with MCI compared to that in the controls, suggesting amino acid starvation in immune cells (Tatara et al., 2023). The present study used capillary electrophoresis-mass spectrometry measurements, and the resulting data demonstrated a significant correlation for every amino acid measured (Supplementary Table S3). However, the correlation coefficient for lysine was low (0.290). The low reproducibility of lysine measurements could be attributed to artifacts from the derivatization of amino acids in the LC-MS/MS measurements. A comparison of amino acid profiles from the IHPP and Iki-Iki study sample sets revealed partially consistent differences between MCI and controls, with alanine, asparagine, histidine, methionine, and threonine, consistently lower in the MCI group (Supplementary Fig. 4). One study reported that plasma lysine, methionine, and threonine levels differed among individuals with MCI (Ikeuchi et al., 2022). A common thread in these reports was that the amino acids showing differences were essential amino acids. Therefore, our surrogate biomarkers, which include essential amino acids, may reflect the metabolic status and pathophysiology of MCI. However, future detailed studies are warranted.

One limitation of this study was that the IHPP and Iki-Iki studies differed in their MCI screening methods. While primary screening by the MMSE was common, the IHPP adds assessment by the Clinical Dementia Rating, which is based on information from family members and other surroundings. This makes IHPP primarily used for amnesic MCI with memory impairments. In contrast, the Iki-Iki study did not specify MCI subtypes. Since this study involved a relatively small number of participants, validation analysis in other cohorts is necessary. In addition, unmeasured or unadjusted confounding factors—such as BMI, diet, and comorbidities—may have influenced the observed metabolic differences. Although age was matched between the MCI and control groups and gender distributions were generally balanced, further validation using larger and better-characterized cohorts will be essential to confirm the findings and assess their generalizability.

## Conclusion

In this study, we conducted metabolome analysis to identify multiple biomarkers of MCI-related metabolites. Using random forest, we constructed a highly predictive multi-marker panel using only five metabolites. To interpret this random forest model further, we employed a global surrogate model using PLS and examined the PLS loadings, which revealed associations with 14 metabolites. These metabolites were involved in critical pathways, such as neuronal energy metabolism and other aspects of neuronal function, both of which may be disrupted in patients with MCI. These findings suggest that the multi-marker panel developed in this study reflects the underlying metabolic changes in MCI, making it a promising tool for early diagnosis and monitoring of MCI progression. Moreover, the approach used in this study can be broadly applied to metabolite biomarker discovery and biological interpretation, supporting its potential for use in various metabolomics studies.
