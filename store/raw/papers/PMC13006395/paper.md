# Identification of tofersen PD-response biomarkers in VALOR clinical trial CSF via multiplexed quantitative proteomics

## Abstract

Tofersen, the first approved genetically targeted therapy for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), demonstrates significant lowering of plasma neurofilament in adults carrying mutations in the superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene; however, additional biomarkers of treatment response in ALS are lacking. Here, we analyze longitudinally collected cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples from the phase 3 VALOR clinical trial to identify candidate tofersen treatment-response biomarkers in SOD1-ALS via quantitative proteomics. We observe significant modulation from baseline abundance for 56 proteins in tofersen-treated participants relative to placebo, including CSF GPNMB, which is significantly and continuously elevated across all post-baseline timepoints. We orthogonally confirm this observation by GPNMB immunoassay in independent tofersen-treated cohorts. Taken together, these data identify pharmacodynamic-response biomarkers of tofersen treatment that can be measured as early as 4 weeks post-treatment in SOD1-ALS patients and demonstrate the utility of leveraging unbiased proteomic screening integrated with targeted validation methods to identify pharmacodynamic-response biomarkers in clinical trial patient samples.

## Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is an adult-onset neurodegenerative disease characterized by loss of motor neurons, leading to progressive muscle loss, paralysis, and death. Mutations in the superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene, the first gene identified to be causally linked to the development of ALS,1 account for approximately 2% of ALS cases. However, the mechanism(s) through which mutations in SOD1 cause ALS are not entirely understood, although there is evidence to suggest that such mutations may cause conformational changes in the protein, leading to protein instability, misfolding, aggregation, and accumulation.2,3,4 Such misfolded SOD1 can become toxic to cells, eventually leading to loss of motor neurons.5

Recently, the antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) tofersen became the first approved genetically targeted therapy in ALS based on the totality of its associated clinical data. These data included significant lowering of plasma neurofilament light (NfL), a biomarker of axonal injury and neurodegeneration, which both preceded and predicted clinical benefit over the longer term in a placebo-controlled, randomized 28-week phase 3 study (VALOR, ClinicalTrials.gov number NCT02623699) and its open-label extension (ClinicalTrials.gov number NCT03070119). NfL also played a critical role as a prognostication marker to control for imbalances in the study population at baseline, as ALS literature has well characterized that higher levels of neurofilament are associated with faster disease progression and shorter survival.6 With NfL serving as an anchor for identification and evaluation of additional prognostic and treatment response biomarkers in ALS, we sought to identify additional PD (pharmacodynamic) biomarkers of tofersen activity and treatment response using CSF samples from the VALOR study. To do so, we employed an unbiased, mass-spectrometry-based proteomics approach to identify CSF proteins altered in abundance following tofersen treatment.

## Results

### Quantitative proteomic screening of longitudinal SOD1-ALS CSF samples

Our CSF analysis included longitudinal CSF samples from 70 subjects enrolled in VALOR Part C7 with available material for all collection time points from baseline through week 16 (five time points). In line with the trial randomization (2:1 tofersen:placebo), our cohort consisted of 47 subjects in the tofersen treatment group and 23 subjects in the placebo arm (Figure 1A), which allowed us to randomly distribute subjects in a 2:1 ratio across 16-plex tandem mass tagged (TMT) sample sets. Samples were labeled with the isobaric tags at the peptide level for analysis within each TMT set. Following offline fractionation to reduce the complexity of the samples, subsequent mass-spectrometry-based analysis demonstrated quantitation of up to 1,625 proteins per analysis set. We imposed a threshold for sample analysis requiring quantification of at least 450 proteins per set; samples that were assayed in one of six TMT sets that quantified fewer than 450 proteins were excluded from downstream analyses (Figure 1B). Additionally, two baseline samples were determined to be likely outliers by principal-component analysis (PCA). These samples and their corresponding longitudinal samples were excluded from further analysis (Figure 1D, asterisks). After these exclusions, 2,476 proteins were quantified in at least one of the remaining TMT sets, and 405 proteins were quantified in all remaining TMT sets. To mitigate the impact of missing data (Figure S1B) on differential abundance analyses, proteins quantified in fewer than 50% of TMT sets were excluded from further analysis (Figure 1C), leaving 1,106 proteins. ### Identification of pharmacodynamic-response biomarker candidates based on abundance changes from baseline

We analyzed the 1,106 proteins with sufficient data across the sample set for differential changes in abundance from baseline between the placebo and tofersen-treated groups. Additionally, we observed a significant treatment effect on 17 proteins at the week-4 time point and on 56 proteins at the week-16 time point (Figure 2B; Table S1). At week 16, tofersen treatment was associated with a significant decrease in abundance in 43 of these proteins (Figure 2C; Table S1). Among these proteins, the largest treatment effect estimate was 0.387, observed for the dipeptidyl peptidase DPP2, indicating a 61.3% placebo-adjusted mean decrease in protein abundance in the tofersen arm. The smallest treatment effect estimate was 0.816, observed for the low-density lipoprotein LRP1, corresponding to an 18.4% mean decrease (Figure 2C; Table S1). Among the 13 proteins for which tofersen treatment was associated with an increase in abundance, the smallest treatment effect estimate was a fold change of 1.22 (22% increase) for the complement protein subcomponent C1R, and the largest treatment effect estimate was a fold change of 11.66 (1066% increase) for the immunoglobulin IGHM (Figure 2C; Table S1). To gain insight into potential pathways impacted by tofersen treatment in SOD1-ALS individuals, we performed a gene ontology/protein set enrichment analysis of the significantly altered-in-abundance protein subset at week 16 (56 proteins) relative to the background CSF proteome, revealing significant overrepresentation of proteins associated with lysosomal function and trafficking, regulation of cellular and axonal growth, nervous system development, phosphorylation, and sphingolipid metabolism (Figure 2D). To further assess changes in CSF protein abundance patterns with tofersen treatment over time, we also examined protein abundance changes at individual timepoints prior to week 16 and observed a significant treatment effect on the abundances of 17 proteins at week 4, 24 proteins at week 8, and 40 proteins at week 12 (Table S1). We further identified 11 proteins for which a significant change from baseline was observed at all four time points with consistent directionality of change; IGHM, GPNMB, C163A, and FCGBP were increased at all time points, while CBPE, CBPQ, DPP2, FSTL5, HEXB, LYAG, and SEM7A were consistently decreased in abundance following tofersen treatment (Table S1; Figure S2).

### Increased abundance of CSF immunoglobulins is consistent with elevation of CSF white blood cell counts in tofersen-treated individuals

Among the proteins with the largest increases in abundance post-tofersen treatment were immunoglobulins IGJ and IGHM (Figure 3A). At week 16, the estimated geometric mean fold changes from baseline were 3.28 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.00, 5.39) and 11.66 (95% CI: 5.88, 23.12), respectively. Notably, we observed that increased abundance of CSF immunoglobulins is consistent with elevation of CSF white blood cell counts (WBCCs) in tofersen-treated individuals (Figures 3B and 3C; Table S2). ### CSF GPNMB is a putative biomarker of tofersen PD response

The protein GPNMB was among the proteins with the most substantial increase in abundance in CSF following tofersen treatment in our unbiased mass-spectrometry-based screen (Figure 4A). As our proteomic screen reports only relative quantitative abundance for GPNMB, we next sought to validate the observation of elevated GPNMB levels in CSF by an orthogonal method allowing absolute protein quantitation. To this end, we utilized a commercial immunoassay to detect and quantify soluble GPNMB protein in human CSF. Consistent with the results of the TMT screen, blinded evaluation of longitudinal VALOR CSF samples via immunoassay demonstrated significant elevation in CSF GPNMB abundance over time on tofersen (Figure 4B) with an average treatment effect of 3.03 (95% CI: 2.30–3.99) at 16 weeks, exhibiting positive correlation between assays and clear separation of treatment and placebo groups (Figure 4C). To test whether CSF GPNMB concentrations were also elevated in response to tofersen treatment in an independent sample cohort, we evaluated CSF from subjects in an earlier stage of the VALOR trial, who received either 60 or 100 mg of tofersen. In both the 60 and 100 mg dose groups, we observed increases in CSF GPNMB abundance with increasing time on drug up to week 16. Notably, we also observed a decline in CSF GPNMB following dosing discontinuation (Figure 4D). For the 100 mg dose group, we report an average treatment effect on CSF GPNMB of 2.89 (95% CI: 1.82–4.60) at 16 weeks in subjects from VALOR Part B, demonstrating agreement with the treatment effect observed in Part C. ### Correlation between CSF GPNMB and plasma NfL

To interrogate the potential significance of elevated GPNMB in the context of disease progression in the clinic, we examined the relationship between baseline CSF GPNMB and plasma NfL. We report a significant positive correlation (Rs = 0.65, CI: 0.45–0.79) between CSF GPNMB and plasma NfL (Figure 4E). Interestingly, tofersen treatment had opposite impacts on these two proteins, resulting in significant lowering of plasma NfL and significant elevation of CSF GPNMB. In examining correlation in the change from baseline for CSF GPNMB and plasma NfL, we also observed significant correlation between CSF GPNMB and plasma NfL measures when treatment arms were pooled, but not within individual treatment arms (i.e., tofersen-treated only or placebo-treated only) (Figure 4F), suggesting that the observed GPNMB-NfL correlation is driven predominately by the group-level treatment effect. While these analyses did not show a clear link between NfL reduction and CSF GPNMB changes at week 16, future evaluations, at later time points or in specific subgroups, may clarify their relationship and GPNMB’s biomarker potential.

## Discussion

In this study, we screened VALOR phase 3 clinical trial samples using quantitative proteomics, resulting in the identification of 56 proteins that showed a significant tofersen-associated treatment effect following 16 weeks on drug. To further explore their capacity as candidate biomarkers of treatment response, we evaluated abundance change from baseline at earlier time points and observed a significant increase in CSF protein abundance for several proteins—including both the immunoglobulin protein IGHM and the transmembrane glycoprotein GPNMB—as early as week 4 (i.e., prior to measured NfL increases), which persisted to week 16 in tofersen-treated subjects. We went on to confirm this observed increase in CSF GPNMB abundance in an independent tofersen-treated SOD1-ALS cohort using a quantitative immunoassay.

Current literature reports conflicting evidence on the role of GPNMB in ALS. Clinical reports have suggested that high baseline levels of CSF GPNMB are associated with poorer prognosis, including shorter survival, in individuals living with ALS.8,9 In contrast, several studies—largely in non-clinical models—have suggested that extracellular GPNMB may exert neuroprotective effects,10,11,12 including in the context of SOD1-ALS.10 The biological relevance of elevated GPNMB levels following tofersen administration remains to be elucidated.

It is possible that GPNMB elevation occurs as a consequence of ASO administration and not modulation of SOD1 biology. Upon uptake, ASO molecules are trafficked through endosomes and ultimately to lysosomes for degradation, yet to engage cytosolic or nuclear targets, ASOs must ultimately escape the endosome.13 GPNMB has recently gained attention as a potential biomarker of lysosomal dysfunction in macrophages in the context of lysosomal storage diseases, accumulation of neuromelanin in the context of aging, and in activation of specific subpopulations of microglia in the context of neurodegenerative diseases.9,12,14,15 Interestingly, we also observed significantly altered abundance for multiple other proteins important for lysosomal function (ARSA, ASAH1, TPP1, CLN5, CTSL, GAA, GNS, HEXB, and MAN2B1). It is possible that changes in lysosomal-related proteins, including GPNMB, occur as a result of lysosome-mediated ASO processing or changes in the character of tissue-resident microglia or CSF-resident monocytes/macrophages in response to tofersen treatment.

To begin to interrogate a potential lysosome-driven inflammatory effect following ASO administration, we measured GPNMB protein concentrations in CSF from participants carrying C9orf72 mutations enrolled in a clinical trial (NCT04288856) evaluating safety and efficacy of BIB078, an ASO designed to lower sense C9orf72 transcript expression. Interestingly, we did not observe a comparable magnitude of rise in CSF GPNMB in CSF from BIIB078-treated participants, suggesting that the rise in GPNMB observed in our study is not merely a consequence of any ASO administration.

While the molecular mechanisms underlying elevated GPNMB in SOD1-ALS subjects dosed with tofersen and clinical significance of GPNMB remain to be elucidated and will require significant further investigation, validation of this observation using an orthogonal methodology and in an independent cohort of tofersen-treated subjects provides additional confidence in other candidate PD-response biomarkers identified in this discovery study.

In contrast to GPNMB, whose abundance showed no significant relationship to CSF WBCCs, we detected positive correlations between CSF WBCCs and several proteins whose abundances were significantly elevated at 16 weeks following tofersen treatment, including IGHM, IGJ, and EMIL2, which could reflect local inflammatory responses to tofersen administration. ASOs—and in particular ASOs possessing phosphorothioate (PS) backbone chemistries like tofersen—have been previously shown to accumulate in macrophages, promote complement activation, and stimulate pro-inflammatory responses.16,17,18,19 Myelitis and radiculitis have been observed in some participants treated with 100 mg tofersen and were accompanied by elevated CSF pleocytosis and are thought to represent clinical manifestations of neuroinflammatory response to tofersen.7,20 Further investigation into the markers of immune and inflammatory responses in response to tofersen and other ASOs may better clarify the impact of these observations.

More broadly, proteomic analysis of CSF from individuals treated with tofersen revealed widespread alterations in protein abundances associated with neuronal signaling, synaptic function, and axonal remodeling. Notably, several neuronally derived proteins showed significant decreases, suggesting potential modulation of pathways involved in axonal regeneration and neuroprotection. Proteins linked to excitatory neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity were also reduced in abundance. Markers associated with axon guidance and repair, including members of the semaphorin family, were consistently lowered, potentially reflecting reduced neuronal degeneration or enhanced regenerative signaling. Additionally, changes in proteins implicated in synaptic remodeling and neurodevelopment were observed, indicating possible long-term neuroprotective effects. Collectively, these data suggest that tofersen may exert broad effects on neuronal integrity and synaptic function, with implications for biomarker development and therapeutic monitoring in ALS.

Recently, Steffke and colleagues carried out an NULISA-based analysis in longitudinally collected CSF samples from tofersen-treated individuals as well as non-ALS controls.21 Several commonalities emerged with our analysis, including changes in neuronal pentraxin proteins, which have been proposed as biomarkers of synaptic dysfunction in neurodegenerative disease.22 Additionally, the NULISA-based analysis was able to identify changes in various cytokines that were undetectable in our dataset, highlighting the utility of diverse platforms in the identification of candidates representing disease- and/or treatment-associated molecular changes.

### Limitations of the study

Several limitations of the current study are important to consider in the interpretation of these data. Individuals with SOD1-ALS represent a small sub-population (∼2%) of an already rare disease with an annual incidence of ∼2/100,000,23 limiting any potential implications on the broader ALS population. We further limited our analysis to the subset of donors for which samples were available for multiple timepoints to aid in our evaluation of the consistency of protein abundance trajectories as the trial progressed. Moreover, the current study assessed only a limited follow-up duration—16 weeks post-treatment initiation. As a result, the sample sizes for each group are relatively small. Due to the rarity of SOD1-ALS and the subsequent constraints on the clinical study size, the number of samples available for discovery work was limited. This study aimed to identify PD biomarkers associated with tofersen treatment using CSF samples from a clinical trial. Unlike non-clinical studies where participants can be treated with a matched negative control, the placebo-treated participants in the VALOR trial were not dosed with any ASO during the lumbar puncture procedure. Therefore, changes in protein abundance in CSF from tofersen-treated participants may be associated with either (1) reduction of SOD1-encoded species, (2) response to ASO backbone, or the combined impact of (1) and (2). Studies to more comprehensively address this question by investigating CSF responses to ASO treatments and candidate biomarker profiles in samples from additional clinical trials targeting non-SOD1 species in both ALS and non-ALS patient populations will be critical to define the molecular mechanisms underlying these pharmacodynamic changes and better understand their potential therapeutic significance.

### Experimental model and study participant details

Inclusion criteria for the study population are outlined in NCT02623699 and are described by Miller et al. (https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2204705).7 In brief, adults with weakness attributable to ALS and a confirmed SOD1 mutation were eligible for inclusion in the Phase 3 clinical study and comprised the Intent to Treat (ITT) population.7 Participants were randomly assigned in a 2:1 ratio to receive either tofersen or placebo by lumbar puncture.7 Enrollment in NCT02623699 (Link: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03070119#study-overview) began 2017-03-08 and data were collected and analyzed over the course of the trial, until trial completion on 2024-08-12. Participant samples and data were collected across 30 centers, as outlined in Protocol 233AS101. The VALOR study protocols were approved by relevant ethics committees.

Exploratory analyses detailed in this present study to identify candidate biomarkers of tofersen pharmacodynamic response in CSF samples from participants who had consented to Future Scientific Research were conducted between August 2022 and December 2025. Subjects from the ITT population were included in the present study if two additional criteria were met: (1) consent granted for Future Scientific Research and (2) availability of baseline and at least two post-baseline CSF samples. Samples sizes were determined based on the availability of CSF samples from the VALOR clinical trial (NCT02623699) ITT population who had (1) provided consent to Future Scientific Research for CSF samples and (2) availability of CSF samples collected at baseline through Week 16. Sex and gender were not considered in the study design.

Written informed consent for Future Scientific Research (FSR) was provided by participants or their legal representatives who were enrolled in the VALOR (NCT02623699) clinical trial, which was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation and ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

### Method details

#### Protein isolation and digestion

CSF samples were selected from the VALOR participants for inclusion based on participant FSR (future scientific research) consent. For each sample, 250μL (∼125μg of protein) of CSF were aliquoted for protein denaturation and digestion. An equal volume of 8M Urea/50mM HEPES (pH = 8) was added to each sample along with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Samples were reduced with 2.5μL of 1M dithiothreitol (DTT) (5mM final concentration) with incubation for 30 min at 56°C, then cooled on ice for 2 min. Samples were alkylated with 15μL of 0.5M iodoacetamide (IAA) (15mM final concentration) and incubated for 20 min at room temperature protected from light. Alkylation was quenched with 2.5μL of 1M DTT (5mM final concentration) with incubation for 15 min at room temperature protected from light. Proteins were precipitated with 125μL of ice-cold trichloroacetic acid with incubation on ice for 1 h, then collected by centrifugation with two washes of 300μL ice-cold acetone (14000xg for 5 min at 4°C for all spins). Pellets were air dried to remove acetone, then resuspended in 1 mL 1M urea/50mM HEPES (pH 8) prior to enzymatic digestion. Peptides were first incubated with LysC (6.25μg/sample; 1:20) overnight at room temperature with agitation, then with trypsin (3.125μg/sample; 1:40) for 6 h at 37°C with shaking. Digestions were quenched with 50μL of 10% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). Soluble peptides were separated from undigested pellets by centrifugation (15000xg for 5 min at 4°C) and desalted over individual 50 mg C18 SepPak vacuum cartridges (Waters) using standard procedures. Peptides were eluted with 750μL 0.5% acetic acid, 40% ACN followed by 750μL 0.5% acetic acid, 80% ACN and dried overnight by vacuum centrifugation.

#### Peptide labeling with isobaric (TMT) tags

Dried peptides were resuspended in 260μL 50% ACN/1% FA, vortexed, and sonicated for 5 min. Peptide concentrations were measured using Pierce Quantitative Fluorometric Peptide Assay kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Fluorescence signals were measured using Ex/Em at 390nm/475nm. Following peptide concentration determination based on a standard curve, 31.25μg of peptides were aliquoted into separate vials and dried overnight by vacuum centrifugation. Digested peptide samples were resuspended in 50μL 200 mM HEPES (pH 8.5)/30% anhydrous ACN, vortexed, pulse centrifuged at 3000xg, and sonicated for 5 min. Individual TMT channel labels were added to each peptide solution (10μL/sample) followed by incubation for 1 h at RT. Samples from each timepoint (n = 5) for a given subject (n = 3 per set) were randomized within a given TMT set, leaving the 16th channel available to label an aliquot of a pooled CSF sample included in each 16plex set to serve as bridge between sample sets to aid in normalization (Figure S1A). Labeling reactions were quenched with 5% hydroxylamine and incubated for 15 min at RT. Labeled peptide mixtures were acidified with 50μL of 1% TFA, combined into sets of 16, and desalted over 50mg C18 SepPak Vacuum cartridges (Waters) as previously described. Desalted labeled samples were subsequently dried by vacuum centrifugation prior to offline high-pH fractionation.

#### Offline high pH fractionation

Peptide mixtures were resuspended in 550μL 5% ACN/5% formic acid, followed by brief vortex agitation, pulse centrifugation at 3000xg, and sonication for 5 min. Resuspended samples were then separated at high pH across an HPLC gradient (7–50% ACN in 0.05% ammonium hydroxide) on a Zorbax Extend-C18 analytical column (80 Å, 4.6 × 250mm, 5μm) (Agilent, ref. 770450-902) on an Agilent 1260 Infinity HPLC into 96 fractions, which were combined into 24 fractions (A1 = A1+C1+E1+G1; B1 = B1+D1+F1+H1; A2 = A2+C2+E2+G2; B2 = B2+D2+F2+H2, etc.) and vacuum centrifuged to dryness.

#### Mass spectrometry analysis

Twelve fractions (A1, B2, […], A11, B12) per sample were analyzed for each set, with the remaining 12 fractions available as back-ups. Individual fractions were resuspended in 8ul 1% formic acid and loaded onto an EASY-Spray C18 Reversed Phase HPLC Column (2um, 75um x 500mm), separated across a 180 min linear HPLC gradient (6–33% ACN in 0.1%FA), and analyzed on a Thermo Fisher Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid mass spectrometer for MS3-based reporter ion quantitation.28 Full scan MS1 data were acquired in the Orbitrap (120K resolution; maximum IT 100ms; AGC 4E05). Determined charge states between 2 and 6 were required for sequencing with a 75s dynamic exclusion window. Data-dependent MS2 scans were performed in the ion trap with collision-induced dissociation (CID) fragmentation for the Top 10 most intense precursor ions (Iso. window 1.2Th NCE 35%; maximum IT 50ms; AGC 1E04). MultiNotch MS328,29 quantification scans were detected in the Orbitrap following HCD (higher energy collision-induced dissociation) fragmentation of the Top 10 most intense MS2 ions (50K resolution; NCE 55%; maximum IT 120ms; AGC 1E05).

#### Protein identification and quantitation

Raw MS data were searched using Sequest24,25,26,30 in GFY Core Version 3.8 (licensed through Harvard University) against a reviewed UniProt/SwissProt human protein database (UP000005640_9606; Download date: 2022-08-11) appended with SOD1 mutation variants and common contaminants. Sequest searches were performed using a 50ppm precursor ion tolerance and included the following constraints: full enzymatic digestion with trypsin with 2 allowed missed cleavages; static carbamidomethyl modification of cysteine, oxidation of cysteine, and TMT modification of lysine and peptide N-termini. Peptide-level spectrum match false discovery rates (FDR) were limited to 1% via application of a target-decoy linear discriminant analysis (LDA) as previously described,31,32 with each run being filtered separately. Peptide-level posterior probabilities from the LDA were multiplied to determine protein-level probabilities. Protein-level FDR was limited to 1% using the Picked FDR method.33 Reporter ion quantitation was carried out at the MS3 level based on measurement of the most intense peak within a 0.003Da window of each theoretical reporter ion peak. Correction for isotopic impurities was included prior to calculation of signal-to-noise ratios based on manufacturer reported channel coefficients.

#### Data filtering and normalization

Individual 16-plex sets were filtered based on summed signal-to-noise ratios (SSN >200) and a minimum co-isolation specificity score of 0.75. Reverse database matches were then removed. Protein abundances were normalized within and across sets, first based on total peptide intensity (column median) per sample, and then to a bridging sample included in each set (row median). Individual 16-plex sets whose analysis resulted in <450 quantified proteins were excluded from further analysis. Following PCA-based outlier detection, two additional individual samples were removed from the analysis.

#### Gene ontology enrichment analyses

Functional enrichments represented among significantly differentially abundant proteins identified at Week 16 were identified based on category over-representation relative to a background human proteome defined by all 2486 proteins detected in the current study. Gene ontology (GO) enrichments were determined using Metascape.27

#### Quantitative immunoassay-based (ELISA) measurement of GPNMB concentrations

Soluble GPNMB protein was quantified using a RayBio Human Osteoactivin (GPNMB) ELISA kit (RayBio, Cat. No. ELH-Osteoactivin-1). Briefly, pre-coated plates with anti-human GPNMB capture antibody were used to add the standard and samples, upon which protein binding was allowed to proceed for 2.5 h at 24°C ± 2 with agitation (450rpm). Plates were then washed prior to addition of biotin-conjugated anti-human GPNMB detection antibody and incubated for 1 h (450rpm, 24°C ± 2). Plates were washed, then incubated first for 45 min (450rpm, 24°C ± 2) with streptavidin horseradish peroxidase (HRP) then for 30 min (450rpm, 24°C ± 2) with tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate. Stop solution was added and signal was detected at 450nm on a Molecular Devices SpectraMax microplate reader. GPNMB concentrations in each sample were determined based on output from a standard curve on a per-plate basis. Human CSF from non-ALS donors (PrecisionMed) was used as the endogenous quality control. To further mitigate the effects of plate-to-plate variability, samples were randomized and distributed across plates such that all samples from a given donor were evaluated on the same plate and such that each plate had samples from both placebo and treatment groups.

### Quantification and statistical analysis

Experimental statistical details including sample sizes (n) per treatment group, group means with 95% confidence interval are indicated in individual Figures and corresponding Figure Legends and results sections. Statistical tests and models are identified and described in the results, Figure Legends, and STAR Methods sections. Additional detailed statistical analysis methods are provided below in this section of the STAR Methods.

For each protein quantified in at least 50% of remaining TMT sets (i.e., at least 9 sets), an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model was fit to the Week 16 log2 fold changes from baseline. The model adjusted for baseline normalized log2 abundance, baseline log plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL) concentration, use of riluzole or edaravone, and randomized treatment arm (placebo or tofersen). The treatment effect estimate was derived from the ANCOVA model as a ratio of the geometric mean fold change from baseline estimates in the two treatment arms. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.34 Statistical significance was defined using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.1. Data at weeks 4, 8, and 12 were analyzed in the same way. For TMT experiments, we set a threshold for quantitative comparisons that required a given protein be quantified in at least 9 TMT sets. Due to the distribution of tofersen-treated samples and placebo-treated samples across groups, sample numbers per group ranged from 18 tofersen treated vs. 9 placebo for proteins quantified in at least 9 TMT sets (minimum threshold) up to 36 tofersen-treated vs. 18 placebo for proteins quantified in 18 sets.

The ANCOVA models were fit using a complete case approach to missing data: missing values introduced when a protein was not detected/quantified in a TMT set(s) were assumed to be missing completely at random. Therefore, only those subjects whose samples were included in a TMT set in which the protein was quantified were included in the model for that protein. For increased confidence in the principal findings resulting from the complete-case assessment, evaluated the missing data mechanism directly for GPNMB using the data generated in an ELISA assay. We compared concentrations measured via ELISA of those individuals who were excluded from TMT analysis due to having complete missing data and those individuals for whom we have data at all timepoints (Figure S3C). We also conducted a sensitivity analysis whereby we repeated the MMRM analysis on the ELISA data after excluding those who were excluded from the TMT analysis due to missing data. The GPNMB trajectory remains qualitatively the same, and quantitatively, the treatment effect estimates are even larger in the analysis that includes those individuals with missing data in the TMT analysis (Figure S3D). This suggests that our approach to handling missing data would not inflate the false discovery rate.

Data from the GPNMB ELISA experiments were analyzed using a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) approach. For the VALOR Part B data, the outcome was the log2 ratio of the post-baseline and baseline concentrations. The fixed effects included the log2 baseline concentration, visit, treatment, the interaction of the log2 baseline concentration and visit, the interaction of treatment and visit, and adjusted for disease progression type (“fast progressing disease” vs. “other”) as a single covariate. For the VALOR Part C data, the outcome was the log2 ratio of the post-baseline and baseline concentrations. The fixed effects included the log2 baseline concentration, visit, treatment, the interaction of the log2 baseline concentration and visit, the interaction of treatment and visit, and adjusted for baseline log plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL) concentration and use of riluzole or edaravone as covariates.

To confirm our assumption of minimal benefit of employing an MMRM (Mixed Models for Repeated Measures) over a simple ANCOVA, we re-analyzed the TMT data by MMRM. As with the original analysis, each protein was modeled separately. The outcome for each MMRM model was the log2 ratio of the post-baseline and baseline abundances. The fixed effects included the log2 baseline abundance, visit, treatment, the interaction of the log2 baseline abundance and visit, the interaction of treatment and visit, baseline log plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL) concentration, and use of riluzole or edaravone. In each MMRM model, an unstructured variance-covariance matrix was used to model within-subject variation. Week 16 treatment effect estimates and adjusted p-values for all proteins were compared across both models (Figure S4), demonstrating a lack of substantive difference between methods in identification of significantly differentially abundant proteins. Using an adjusted p-value threshold of 0.1, there were 59 significant proteins using the MMRM approach, 56 significant proteins using the ANCOVA approach, and 54 significant proteins in both approaches.
