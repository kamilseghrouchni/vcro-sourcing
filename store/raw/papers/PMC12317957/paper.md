# Multi-omics and Mendelian randomization study explores potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas

## Abstract

### Background

Meningioma is a common primary central nervous system tumor that can cause a heavy burden on patients. Despite its well-established treatment modalities, pharmacological treatments are not sufficiently abundant. Therefore, we explored potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas by integrating genomic and proteomic data.

### Methods

We integrated meningioma data from the UK Biobank and Finnish databases and subsequently explored potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas through multi-omics data using bioinformatics techniques and Mendelian randomization. These targets were finally evaluated using phenotype-wide association group analysis.

### Results

We found that BET1L, COL17A1, CFAP43, SH3PXD2A, TTC28, ZNRF3, SLK, AKR1C3, NRXN3, and RSPO3 can be potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas.

### Conclusion

This study provides evidence and explores the biological significance of BET1L, COL17A1, CFAP43, SH3PXD2A, TTC28, ZNRF3, SLK, AKR1C3, NRXN3, and RSPO3 as potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas, providing new insights into the development of targeted therapy for meningiomas.

## Introduction

Meningiomas are primary brain tumors originating from arachnoid cells and are now the most common intracranial tumors [1]. Although most meningiomas are considered grade I tumors by the World Health Organization, some are classified as grades II and III, and their treatment is relatively challenging [2]. The mainstay of treatment for meningiomas is surgical resection, and the extent of resection of some tumors is limited to providing neurovascular protection [3]. As a result, the risk of recurrence is significantly higher in these patients, and additional treatment is essential. Even though surgical techniques are now quite sophisticated, the health-related quality of life of patients with postoperative meningiomas is compromised to some extent [4]. In addition to edema of the surrounding tissues due to occupancy, meningiomas can cause different clinical manifestations depending on their location and size [5, 6]; therefore, primary and recurrent meningiomas can cause great physical and psychological stress to patients. The prognosis of meningiomas in children is even worse than in adult patients [7]. Although research on the pathogenesis of meningiomas is progressing, the pharmacological treatment of meningiomas has not considerably progressed [8]. Therefore, exploring potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas is crucial to improving patients’ quality of life.

Although plasma proteins are essential for maintaining normal physiological functions, they have also been implicated in the pathogenesis of various diseases, such as diabetes mellitus [9] and pancreatic cancer [10]. Recent research has also pointed to a potential association between plasma proteins and meningioma development. It has been shown that certain plasma proteins can be synthesized not only in the liver but also locally within neoplastic brain tissues, including meningiomas, suggesting a potential role in tumor progression through paracrine or autocrine mechanisms [11]. Additionally, TXNDC16, a meningioma-associated antigen, has been identified as a secreted and immunogenic protein, indicating that specific plasma protein-related antigens may participate in tumor immune evasion or modulation [12]. Furthermore, elevated levels of fibrinogen degradation products have been detected in the serum of patients with meningiomas [13], supporting their involvement in the disease’s pathophysiology. Age-related changes, including increased permeability of the blood–brain barrier, may facilitate the entry of plasma proteins into the cerebrospinal fluid [14], coinciding with the higher incidence of meningiomas in the elderly population [15]. Despite this, most proteomic studies to date have focused primarily on tumor tissue samples [16], which may not capture the full spectrum of circulating protein alterations. Therefore, a systematic investigation into the causal relationship between plasma proteins and meningiomas is warranted, potentially contributing to the discovery of novel molecular targets for treatment.

With the development of genome-wide association studies (GWAS), millions of genetic variants have been detected in individuals, providing a direct channel for gene–phenotype association analyses and greatly promoting the development of genetics research [17]. Among these genetic variants, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) represent the most common form of variation and play critical roles in influencing gene function, regulation, and ultimately phenotypic traits. Importantly, interactions among multiple SNPs—known as epistatic effects—can modulate the overall genetic risk of complex diseases, sometimes amplifying or mitigating individual SNP effects. Therefore, studying multiple SNPs simultaneously provides a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of disease mechanisms and prevention strategies. The abundance of GWAS presents a foundation for the study of some complex diseases, and the application of GWAS offers a new path for the discovery and utilization of drugs and the prevention and treatment of tumors [18]. The continuous updating of GWAS has pushed the Mendelian randomization (MR) method onto the stage; thus, methods for clinical and basic research have been improved. MR uses genetic variation as a tool to explore potential causal relationships between exposure factors and disease and is less susceptible to confounding factors [19, 20]. Moreover, MR has shown extraordinary value in proteomics and exploration of potential drug targets for diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease [21], multiple sclerosis [22], and other complex diseases. Meanwhile, the development and improvement of gene expression databases have provided abundant histological data for studying the factors of disease development, adding new means for a deeper understanding of diseases [23].

In this study, we included multiple sources of meningioma GWAS data for integration to identify new genetic variant determinants of meningiomas, which were subjected to relevant bioinformatics studies. This was followed by a comprehensive MR analysis of the plasma proteome and meningiomas to explore the causal relationship between the two and to assess the reliability of our results. We then performed an interaction network analysis for some proteins with strong causal relationships. Finally, we evaluated the correlation between these potential therapeutic targets and other phenotypes by conducting phenotype-wide association group studies [24]. We aimed to discover potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas and explore the possible causal relationship between plasma proteomes and meningiomas. We investigated the role of plasma proteins in the development of meningiomas to discover new therapeutic targets and provide new treatment options for patients with meningiomas.

## Methods and materials

### Study design

We first meta-analyzed the meningioma data and identified new significant genetic loci. We then obtained related genes based on these loci. We searched for two meningioma data levels from the GEO database for merging and further evaluated the genes of interest through differential analysis, correlation analysis, and immune infiltration with these genomic data. Subsequently, we performed an MR study using plasma proteins from meningiomas to explore potential protein targets. We validated the proteins we identified based on the pooled data and constructed a protein interaction network to analyze the linkages between the proteins. Finally, we evaluated the feasibility of the potential targets by performing a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) with the pooled data.

### Data sources

We used plasma proteomic data from the deCODE group (https://www.decode.com/summarydata/), which contains 4907 plasma proteins detected in 35,559 individuals [25]. The GWAS data for meningiomas were obtained from the FinnGen database (https://r10.finngen.fi/) and the UK Biobank database [26], which included a total of 1587 cases and 673,553 controls. Genomic data for meningiomas were obtained from GSE88720 and GSE43290 in the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). To ensure the accuracy of our study, we chose the gene sequencing expression profiling data for meningiomas in Homo sapiens. The data included in our study were all from publicly available databases and did not require additional ethical approval or informed consent.

### GWAS study of meningiomas

We performed a meta-analysis of meningioma GWAS data from the FinnGen and UK Biobank databases and then screened for new genetically significant loci (p < 5 × 10−8). The screened loci were then compared with the previously reported genetically significant loci distances, and loci within 500 kb were removed. Finally, we localized the new significant loci to the genes and reflected the genes to the chromosomes through the g: Profiler platform [27].

### Differentially expressed gene recognition

We investigated whether the identified genes are differentially expressed between tumors and normal tissues. If these genes were differentially expressed in the samples, then they would be preferred as candidate targets. First, we normalized the dataset from GEO and then merged the datasets before removing batch effects. We then extracted expression data of relevant genes and performed differential expression analysis of genes. Genes with P < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Finally, we calculated the correlation coefficients between differentially expressed genes.

### Analysis of immune cell infiltration

We calculated the relative immune cell content of the samples using the CIBERSORT tool [28]. Subsequently, we compared the content of immune cells in the meningioma group with that in the control group and explored the differences. Finally, we calculated the correlation between immune cells and differential genes. When P < 0.05, we considered that immune cells and differential genes were correlated.

### MR analysis

To complement our genomic analyses, we conducted a proteome-wide MR analysis using genetic instruments for circulating plasma proteins. This approach aimed to identify proteins that may causally influence meningioma risk, independent of the loci discovered through GWAS. We designed this part of the MR study according to the STROBE-MR guidelines. MR is a research method that uses single nucleotide diversity as an instrumental variable to investigate exposure and outcome, and SNPs need to meet certain conditions [29]. First, the SNPs we selected should be significantly associated with exposure. Second, the SNPs we selected cannot be associated with other confounding factors. Finally, SNPs can only act on outcomes by influencing exposure factors.

When selecting instrumental variables for Mendelian randomization analysis, we first identified SNPs that showed a strong association with plasma protein levels, applying a stringent genome-wide significance threshold of p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ to ensure robustness of the associations. To minimize the potential bias caused by linkage disequilibrium (LD) between SNPs, we performed LD clumping using a window size of 10,000 kb and an r² threshold of 0.001, thereby retaining only independent SNPs that are not in high LD with each other. Following this, we conducted harmonization procedures to align the effect alleles of the selected SNPs across the exposure and outcome datasets, checking for palindromic SNPs and strand ambiguities to avoid misalignment errors. Finally, to assess the strength of the selected instrumental variables and reduce the risk of weak instrument bias, we calculated the F-statistic for each SNP. Instrumental variables with an F-value greater than 10 were considered sufficiently strong to satisfy the relevance assumption in Mendelian randomization [30]. SNPs failing this criterion were excluded from further analysis to ensure the validity and reliability of causal inference.

During the subsequent MR analyses, we removed the groups that could not be subjected to sensitivity analyses (i.e., those that could not be extracted with more than two SNPs for the sake of the robustness of the results and did not perform subsequent analyses. In the subsequent analyses, we mainly used three methods to evaluate the results: inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, and weighted median methods. The IVW method is the most efficient when all the instrumental variables are valid; thus, we used it as the main method [31]. When the p-value of the IVW method was < 0.05, we considered the result to be significant. When there was polytropy or multiple validity, we utilized the remaining two methods for additional reference.

In the subsequent horizontal multivalence test, we conducted the Cochran Q test based on the MR-Egger and IVW methods, and when the p-value was > 0.05, we considered that the results were not heterogeneous. Simultaneously, we evaluated the horizontal multivalence of the results based on the MR-Egger intercept method, again requiring a p-value > 0.05.

### SMR analysis

SMR is a type of MR based on pooled data, which enables causal inferences to be made using data at the aggregated level [32]. After we performed the MR study, we performed SMR replication analysis using the pooled data of the plasma proteome, and when the p-value was < 0.05, the result was considered significant. Simultaneously, we performed sensitivity analyses using the HEIDI test, at which point we required a p-value > 0.05. Only proteins replicated successfully in the SMR were included in our subsequent analyses.

### Protein–protein interaction analysis

To explore protein–protein interactions, we visualized all the screened genes and proteins with correlations through the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) and constructed a protein interaction network.

### Phenome-wide association study

PheWAS is a method for phenome-wide investigation of the association between genes and phenotypes [33]. We used PheWAS to analyze all the included potential targets to investigate whether the therapeutic loci would impact other shapes, eliminating their side effects as much as possible. Gene and protein therapeutic targets without side effects became our final candidate therapeutic targets. Therefore, we conducted our PheWAS study through exome sequencing data from the UK Biobank [34].

### Statistical analysis

We performed a meta-analysis of meningioma GWAS summary data using the METAL software with an inverse-variance weighted fixed-effects model [35]. Gene expression data from the GEO database were processed using Strawberry Perl (v5.32.1.1) and analyzed in R with the limma package to identify differentially expressed genes. SMR was conducted to link gene expression to meningioma risk using public eQTL and GWAS summary statistics. For two-sample MR, we used the TwoSample MR package in R. Immune cell infiltration was estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm (1,000 permutations). PheWAS was performed to assess pleiotropic effects of candidate proteins, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple testing.

All visualizations were generated using ggplot2, ggpubr, corrplot, and reshape2. SNP clumping and data harmonization were done with plinkbinr. All statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio (version 4.3.2).

## Results

### Genome-wide association analysis results

We performed a meta-analysis of 1,587 cases and 673,553 controls of meningioma GWAS data, for which we obtained a total of 19,345,634 associations after data refinement. For visualization purposes, we randomly selected 1 million SNPs from the total 19,345,634 associations to generate the Manhattan plot (Fig. 1a). This random sampling may not include all genome-wide significant loci (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸); however, all such loci were identified. We identified a total of 41 novel loci and localized them to genes, which were CHEK2, BET1L, STN1, COL17A1, CFAP43, SH3PXD2A, TTC28, ZNRF3, and SLK. They were clustered on chromosomes 10, 11, and 22 (Fig. 1b).

### Differentially expressed genes results

We extracted six genes, CHEK2, BET1L, COL17A1, SH3PXD2A, TTC28, and SLK, which were differentially expressed in the meningioma genomic data. Among them, BET1L, SH3PXD2A, and SLK were differentially expressed in the meningioma and control groups, and BET1L and SH3PXD2A were highly expressed in the meningioma group, whereas SLK was highly expressed in the control group (Fig. 1c). For these three differentially expressed genes, we observed that they were mainly negatively correlated (Fig. 1d).

To further investigate whether the observed gene expression changes were associated with alterations in the tumor immune microenvironment, we next conducted immune cell infiltration analysis.

### Immune cell infiltration results

In the correlation analysis of immune cells, we compared the amount of each immune cell in the meningioma and control groups. We found a significant difference in the expression of M1 macrophages and resting mast cells in the two groups. When M1 macrophages were more highly expressed in the control group, resting mast cells were more highly expressed in the meningioma group (Fig. 1e). In the correlation of differentially expressed genes with immune cells, we observed that most of them were negatively correlated (Fig. 1f).

### Mendelian randomization results

At the beginning of the MR study, we selected 4144 SNPs from 4907 plasma proteomes as our instrumental variables. All of them had F-values that met our requirements; therefore, they would not interfere with our results. We only extracted more than 2 SNPs from 562 plasma proteins for subsequent analyses. To make our results more robust, we selected the top five proteins with the smallest p-values for subsequent analysis, which were AKR1C3, MAN1A2, NRXN3, RETN, and RSPO3. Among them, AKR1C3 (odds ratio [OR] 1.002; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.000–1.003) and NRXN3 (OR 1.001; 95% CI 1.001–1.002) were risk factors for meningioma. However, MAN1A2 (OR 0.999; 95% CI 0.998–1.000), RETN (OR 0.999; 95% CI 0.999–1.000), and RSPO3 (OR 0.999; 95% CI 0.998–1.000) were protective factors for meningioma (Table 1). In subsequent tests for heterogeneity and horizontal polytropy, we did not find significant heterogeneity and horizontal polytropy in our results. We used a false discovery rate correction for results that were associated, with the aim of eliminating some false-positive results [36].

### SMR results

In our SMR validation using pooled plasma proteomic data, we found that AKR1C3, MAN1A2, NRXN3, RETN, and RSPO3 all stood up to the test, and they ensured both a p-value of < 0.05 and a p-value of > 0.05 for the HEIDI test (Table 1). SMR confirmed that these plasma proteins may be causally associated with meningioma development.

### Protein–protein interaction results

We constructed a protein–protein interaction network map of meningioma-related gene proteins and conducted a visual analysis (Fig. 2). We observed that our candidate proteins were more or less connected with other proteins, such as BET1L, TTC28, and MAN1A2, which highlighted the role of the proteins in relation to each other. Notably, we found that NRXN3 (from MR analysis) interacts directly with CHEK2 (from GWAS and DEG), suggesting a potential shared biological pathway. Other MR proteins, including MAN1A2 and RETN, were integrated into sub-networks involving GWAS hits, further supporting their relevance in meningioma biology.

### PheWAS results

We utilized the PheWAS approach to evaluate whether the MR-identified candidate genes were associated with phenotypes, such as metabolic, immune, or cardiovascular traits. Genes that showed strong associations with systemic conditions were considered to carry a higher risk of off-target effects or potential adverse reactions, making them less suitable as therapeutic targets. Therefore, we prioritized proteins that demonstrated a causal association with meningioma but showed minimal involvement in unrelated physiological systems—representing potentially safer and more disease-specific targets. We excluded CHEK2, STN1, MAN1A2, and RETN after we performed a PheWAS of all candidate genes with the candidate proteins included in the study, including AKR1C3, NRXN3, RSPO3, etc. CHEK2 affects binary and continuous traits, STN1 affects continuous traits, MAN1A2 affects continuous traits, and RETN affects continuous traits (Fig. 3).

## Discussion

To our knowledge, ours is the first study to adopt a multi-omics research approach, using multiple modalities, to explore and validate potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas. To increase the generalizability of our findings, we included genome-wide association group data from approximately 670,000 people from the FinnGen and UK Biobank databases. This is the first and largest developmental study of potential targets for meningiomas based on the meningioma genome and plasma proteome. We found a causal genetic relationship between AKR1C3, NRXN3, and RSPO3 and meningiomas, as well as several related genes that may regulate the development of meningiomas. These results will help study the mechanism underlying meningioma development and improve therapeutic means.

The development of meningiomas is influenced by various factors, and previous studies have made some progress; however, the cause of meningiomas remains unclear. Chromosomal alterations are crucial for the development of meningiomas, and the development of atypical meningiomas is associated with alterations in chromosomes 1, 6, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 17, 18, 20, and 22 [37]. In contrast, most of the genetically related loci for meningiomas that we identified are located on chromosomes 10, 11, and 20, which will provide new research ideas for the study of the pathogenesis of meningiomas.

BET1L is essential for maintaining normal Golgi apparatus function [38] and has been associated with various conditions, including uterine fibroids [39], amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [40], and longevity in the Japanese population [41]. Although no direct evidence currently links BET1L to meningiomas, its involvement in vesicular transport in other diseases suggests that its role in meningioma development warrants further investigation [42]. SH3PXD2A has been implicated in the pathogenesis of nerve sheath tumors through abnormal gene fusion events [43], and since such tumors can co-occur with meningiomas in nerve sheath disease syndromes, SH3PXD2A may have an indirect influence on meningioma biology. Additionally, the MERTK/TYRO3 complex, known for its role in immune modulation, may represent a parallel mechanism relevant to both tumor types [44]. SKL has been associated with uterine leiomyomas [45], yet in our study, SKL was more highly expressed in the control group. Given the female predominance of meningioma [3], further studies could clarify the relevance of this locus.

AKR1C3 has been implicated in the regulation of estrogen and progesterone, with increased levels enhancing the expression of these hormones [46]. As meningiomas are often hormone-responsive, the oncogenic potential of AKR1C3 may be mediated through hormonal pathways [47]. NRXN3, a neuropeptide family member, is composed of isoforms including several laminin proteins and is associated with psychiatric disorders [48, 49]. Although a direct association with meningiomas has not yet been established, NRXN3 remains a plausible candidate due to its relevance in neurobiological processes. RSPO3 is involved in fat metabolism and distribution, and since obesity is a known risk factor for meningiomas, RSPO3 may affect tumor development through its influence on metabolic pathways and Wnt signaling [50, 51].

Although genes such as ZNRF3, COL17A1, CFAP43, and TTC28 were not differentially expressed between meningioma and control groups in our analysis, their potential roles in meningioma should not be excluded. ZNRF3 is a key regulator of Wnt/β-catenin signaling and has been implicated in tumors such as adrenocortical carcinoma [52] and colorectal cancer [53]. As aberrant β-catenin signaling is involved in meningioma pathogenesis [54], ZNRF3 may act through this pathway. COL17A1 has been reported in the context of pancreatic [55] and colon cancers [56] and can influence cell migration and development via the P53 pathway [57], which is commonly active in meningiomas [58]. CFAP43, while not previously linked to meningiomas, is known to affect ciliary structure and function and cause conditions such as normal pressure hydrocephalus [59]. In our protein–protein interaction network, CFAP43 showed a strong association with RSPO3 (Fig. 2), suggesting that its involvement in meningioma may be mediated indirectly. TTC28, a gene associated with maintaining chromosomal stability via autophagy [60], has been found to be dysregulated in several cancers, including esophageal [61] and ovarian cancers [62]. A case of TTC28 deletion in a patient with meningioma and craniofacial abnormalities further supports its potential relevance [63]. Furthermore, our network analysis revealed a close interaction between TTC28 and CHEK2 (Fig. 2), and co-deletion of CHEK2 and NF2 has been associated with increased chromosomal instability at chromosome 22, a known factor in meningioma biology [64]. Therefore, TTC28 may exert its effects in conjunction with CHEK2, supporting its consideration as a potential therapeutic target.

Macrophages are the most abundant type of cell in meningiomas in immune cell infiltration analysis [65]. We found that M1 macrophages, which secrete various inflammatory factors and express anti-tumor effects, were reduced in the meningioma group, but we did not find a difference in M2 macrophages, which express pro-tumor effects [66]. Thus, the reduction of M1 macrophages becomes an integral part of the pathophysiology of meningiomas, whereas the role of M2 macrophages in meningiomas is not obvious. In contrast to previous studies, we collectively found a high expression of resting mast cells in meningiomas and did not find differential expression of T and B cells [67]. The samples we used for analysis were not identical, indicating the prevalence of resting mast cells in meningiomas. The immune state associated with allergic reactions affects meningioma development [68], and resting mast cells may play an important role in this process. However, no correlation exists between the differentially expressed genes we found and differentially immune cells, and the genes that mediate differentially expressed immune cells need to be further explored. Beyond the observed macrophage and mast cell dynamics, our analysis reveals key immunomodulatory genetic alterations in meningiomas. AKR1C3 overexpression may promote immunosuppression through progesterone metabolism and prostaglandin E₂ accumulation, polarizing macrophages toward pro-tumor M₂ phenotypes [69, 70]. NRXN3 mutations could impair dendritic cell recruitment by disrupting neuro-immune interactions, a mechanism increasingly recognized in CNS disorders [71]. RSPO3 amplification likely expands tumor-associated macrophages via Wnt/β-catenin signaling and CSF1R pathway synergy [72, 73]. These genotype-immunophenotype relationships highlight actionable therapeutic targets. However, there is no direct evidence for the specific role of genetics in tumors and immunity, and more research is needed to prove this in the future.

In this study, we integrated whole-genome association sequencing data from two major databases of patients with meningioma and used a multi-method analysis with multiple validation steps to investigate potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas. GWAS combined with bioinformatics analysis and MR allowed a comprehensive investigation of potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas, and PheWAS studies were conducted to investigate the side effects of these targets to screen safer potential therapeutic targets. However, our study still has some limitations. Firstly, the population included in our study was predominantly European, which has drawbacks in terms of generalizability to all human populations. Therefore, further studies are needed when data on multi-ethnic meningiomas are abundant. Secondly, because we conducted our study based on pooled data, we were unable to stratify patients by characteristics such as grade, age, sex and types of molecular pathology. Furthermore, we only analyzed the relationship between individual proteins and disease during MR, and the effect of protein association on disease needs to be further elucidated in future studies. Finally, because the data mainly came from public databases with inevitable confounding interference, further experiments are needed to verify our findings. Despite the limitations of our study, our findings still generate testable hypotheses for future preclinical validation.

## Conclusion

In this study, we aimed to identify potential therapeutic targets for meningiomas through a comprehensive multi-omics approach. Genome-wide association and transcriptomic analyses revealed several candidate genes, including BET1L, COL17A1, CFAP43, SH3PXD2A, TTC28, ZNRF3, and SLK, among which BET1L, SH3PXD2A, and SLK showed differential expression between meningioma and normal meningeal tissues, highlighting their therapeutic relevance. In parallel, Mendelian randomization analysis of the plasma proteome identified AKR1C3, NRXN3, and RSPO3 as proteins significantly associated with meningioma risk, suggesting their potential causal role. Together, these findings prioritize a set of genes and proteins functionally involved in key biological processes, such as vesicle transport, hormonal signaling, and Wnt pathway modulation. This integrative analysis provides a foundation for future studies aimed at mechanistic validation and targeted intervention in meningioma.
