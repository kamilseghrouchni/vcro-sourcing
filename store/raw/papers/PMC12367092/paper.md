# Qualification of a electrochemiluminescence assay for the detection of human urinary neurotrophin receptor p75

## Abstract

The extracellular domain of the neurotrophin receptor p75 has been shown to be a prominent biomarker for both disease diagnosis and progression for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. This urinary analyte may serve as a valuable fluid biomarker which greatly increases the ease of sample collection in both healthy volunteers and patients. In this paper, the method development and validation for an electrochemiluminescence assay is described. This assay completely uses commercially available reagents and can be performed using common lab equipment found in most bioanalytical labs. This method shows good accuracy and precision, high sensitivity as well as good parallelism illustrating the ability of the method to detect and report on urinary concentrations of neurotrophin receptor p75. The assay can quantitate as low as 78 pg/mL of neurotrophin receptor p75 and > 98% of healthy urine samples tested fell within the dynamic range of the assay.

## Introduction

1.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is an incurable and fatal neurodegenerative disorder which usually causes death within three years of onset [1,2]. The disease affects the upper and lower motor neurons (UMN and LMN, respectively) in the brain and spinal cord, causing progressive and debilitating paralysis. Usually, ALS patients succumb to the disease due to denervation of respiratory muscles. Currently, seven treatments are Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved therapeutic treatments which have only been shown to provide a small life extension of approximately 3–6 months [3].

Due to the nature of this disease, biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis are screened for in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood. Inflammatory markers associated with glial response and axonal damage or apoptosis were included in the screenings [4]. The two major markers from these initial studies which came to prominence were the neurofilament phosphorylated heavy chain (pNfH) and the neurofilament light chain (NfL) [5].

In the fight against neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS, new biomarkers are constantly being searched for to be used as metrics for both disease diagnosis and progression. Mary-Louise Rogers and coworkers have largely pioneered the use of urinary neurotrophin receptor p75 (p75NTR) as one of the first alternative fluid-based biomarker for the diagnosis and progression of ALS [6–8]. In a developing brain, p75NTR is primarily expressed as a crucial receptor responsible for axonal pruning [9]. This 427 amino acid, 75 kDa cellular transmembrane protein contains both an intracellular domain (ICD) and an extracellular domain (ECD) [10]. Activation of p75NTR involves binding of adaptor proteins to the ICD, which in turn causes subsequent cleavage and release of the p75NTR ECD into the extracellular environment [11]. In typical, healthy patients p75NTR is weakly expressed [12]. Neurodegenerative disease states cause a significant increase in p75NTR expression, resulting in increased release of p75NTR ECD into biological fluids such as blood, CSF, and urine [13].

While initially proposed as a urinary biomarker for the diagnosis and progression of ALS, recently p75NTR has been discussed as a possible biomarker for several other neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease [14], age-related dementia [15], schizophrenia [16] and diabetic neuropathy [17]. As p75NTR becomes a more commonly used biomarker in multiple disease states, more robust and available methods will need to be developed and validated. Current studies on human p75NTR have all been performed using either home-built enzyme linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) or by commercial ELISA kits available from vendors such as R&D Systems and Biosensis [6–8,18,19]. However, several home-built ELISA methods use in-house produced capture antibodies, and commercial kits pose several issues such as but not limited to lot-to-lot reproducibility and time of acquisition.

Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) assays provide several advantages over their ELISA counter parts in terms of assay robustness, ease of operation, inter-plate reproducibility and sensitivity [20]. Herein, we present the development of an ECL assay to detect urinary human p75NTR using only commercially available materials. This assay is built upon the Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) platform and uses a commercially available SULFO-TAG (STAG) secondary detection antibody which eliminates the need for in-house antibody conjugations. We also present method validation covering the assay performance through precision and accuracy, dilution linearity, parallelism, robustness and cross-reactivity assessments using both recombinant protein quality controls and endogenous quality controls from pooled individual samples.

### ECL protocol

2.2.

A step-by-step diagram of the assay protocol is shown in Figure 1. Mouse Anti- p75NTR (Millipore Sigma, Cat. # 05–446) was used as the capture antibody. Upon reconstitution according to manufacturer’s instructions, the capture antibody was prepared in a solution of 1X PBS at a concentration of 1 ug/mL. 50 uL of the capture solution was added to all wells of an MSD High Bind plate and allowed to incubate overnight (~16 hours) at 4°C. After coating, all contents are aspirated from all wells and washed with 300 uL of PBS with 0.05% Tween for a total of three washes. Blocking was performed using 50 μL/well of SuperBlock T20 and allowed to incubate for at least 1 hour at RT with shaking at 700 RPM. After blocking, all contents are aspirated from all wells and washed with 300 uL of PBS with 0.05% Tween for a total of three washes as previously performed. Standard curve and quality controls are prepared fresh daily using the recombinant protein p75NTR (R&D Systems, Cat. # 367-NR-050/CF) in SuperBlock T20 from Thermo. This recombinant protein is a truncated protein containing only the ECD from Lys29-Asn250. Figure 1.Pictural in-well representation of assay protocol documenting each step in method. Inset shows final assay format.

Standards were prepared by spiking recombinant protein p75 into SuperBlock T20 at the following concentrations: 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, 0.625, 0.313, 0.156 & 0.078 ng/mL.

Quality controls (QCs) were prepared by spiking recombinant protein p75 into SuperBlock T20 at the following concentrations: 10.0, 7.50, 1.00, 0.250, & 0.078 ng/mL (Upper Limit of Quantitation (ULOQ), Quality Control High (QCH), Quality Control Mid (QCM), Quality Control Low (QCL) & Lower Limit of Quantitation (LLOQ), respectively).

A most reasonable dilution (MRD) of 4-fold is applied to all urine samples by dilution with SuperBlock T20. 50 uL of all standards, quality controls and samples are added to the plate and allowed to incubate for 1 hour at room temperature with shaking at 700 RPM. After sample incubation, the entire plate is washed as described above. The primary detection antibody, a goat Anti- p75NTR (R&D Systems, Cat. # AF367), is prepared in a solution of 1% BSA in 1X PBS at a concentration of 2 ug/mL. 50 uL of the primary detection antibody solution is added to the plate and allowed to incubate for 1 hour at room temperature with shaking at 700 RPM. After primary detection antibody incubation, the entire plate is washed as described above. The secondary detection antibody, a SULFO-TAG labeled anti-goat IgG antibody (MSD, Cat. # R32AG), is prepared in a solution of 1% BSA in 1X PBS concentration of 0.25 ug/mL. 50 uL of the secondary detection antibody solution is added to the plate and allowed to incubate for 1 hour at room temperature with shaking at 700 RPM. After secondary detection antibody incubation, the entire plate is washed as described above. 150 uL of MSD Gold Read Buffer A is then added to all wells of the plate and immediately read by an MSD SECTOR instrument. Here, all ECL measurements were performed using an MSD SECTOR S600 reader and data was captured using MSD Methodical Mind. SoftMax Pro Software, version 7.0.3 was used for analysis and regression.

Endogenous quality controls (eQCs) were prepared by pooling five individual pre-screened urine samples obtained from BioIVT and tested to be the following concentrations: 2.46, 0.990 & 0.326 ng/mL (eQCH, eQCM and eQCL, respectively.

### Bio-layer interferometry

2.3.

Antibody binding affinity was measured using Bio-Layer Interferometry (BLI measurements) measurements on the Sartorius Octet R8. Protein G tips were hydrated in 1X PBS for at least 10 minutes. Hydrated tips were then placed in wells containing 10 μg/mL of capture or detection antibody in 1X PBS and incubated for 5 minutes with shaking at 700 RPM. Loaded sensor tips were placed in a baseline of 1% BSA in PBS for 5 min with shaking at 700 RPM before being moved into wells containing spiked recombinant p75(ECD) in 1X PBS. Association data curves were observed at the 10, 5, 2.5, and 1 μg/mL for 5 min while shaking at 700 RPM. Sensor tips were moved back into the baseline solution for 5 min at 700rpm to observe dissociation curves. Global fits were performed and KD values were obtained using the Sartiorus Octet Kinetic Analysis Software.

## Results

3.

### Initial method development

3.1.

To start development of this method, we drew from the work of Rogers, et al. who used a polyclonal goat anti-p75NTR in several of their in-house developed ELISA methods [6–8,18]. Our first strategy was to use this commercially available antibody as a primary detection antibody paired with a SULFO-TAG labeled anti-goat IgG that is available from MSD. Knowing that this antibody binds to the ECD of p75, seven monoclonal antibodies were screened as potential capture antibodies. Each antibody pair was then screened using the following samples: two commercially available p75 (ECD) recombinant calibrators tested at three different concentrations, two individual human urine samples at two dilutions and one blank composed of assay diluent. The top performing antibody pair was assessed based on the following metrics: strength of signal to recombinant protein, ability to report signal from endogenous p75 from healthy volunteer urine, and blank signal performance. Also, signals present for both recombinant of endogenous p75(ECD) must dilute linearly. Once the antibodies were selected, an antibody titration was performed to assess the optimum capture and detection antibody concentrations. Here, the optimum concentrations were chosen via highest signal-to-background ratio. Twelve combinations of blocking solutions and assay diluents were assessed to potentially further increase the signal-to-background ratio. Furthermore, the assay was performed on standard and high-bind plates from MSD in which it was determined that the high-bind plates showed a significant reduction in nonspecific binding. Several diluent optimization steps including commercial anti-species blockers, reducing agents, salt concentrations and detergents were tested in which none improved overall assay signal or performance. With the optimized assay, the authors did not test the reversed antibody orientation as this would require an entirely different secondary detection antibody. Final assay format is shown in the inset of Figure 1.

Each individual capture antibody was coated overnight on an MSD High-Bind Plate. This method of capture coating eliminates the need to biotinylate the capture antibody as some labs do not have the capability to do so. At the time of publication, neither of the selected antibodies were available commercially in a biotinylated form. Increased sample incubation time showed no significant increase in assay signal or sensitivity. An example of the resulting standard curve using this combination of critical reagents is shown in Figure 2(a). Figure 2.(a) An example of the seven point standard curve with an example of the best fit curve performed by a 5PL fit using a 1/y^2 weighting factor. (b) Calculated concentrations versus expected concentrations of recombinant p75(ECD) from human (circle), mouse (square), rat (triangle) and rabbit (diamond).

The selected combination of capture and detection antibodies was then assessed for specificity to human p75 (ECD). Recombinant mouse, rat and rabbit p75(ECD) were measured at seven concentrations within the dynamic range of the assay. Results are shown in Figure 2(b). Mouse p75(ECD) was not detectable until 3.125 ng/mL and is reported at 2.5% cross-reactive. Both Rat and Rabbit p75)ECD were not detectable until 6.25 ng/mL and are reported at 1.4% and 1.8% cross-reactive. This data suggests that the assay described in this document is highly specific toward human p75(ECD) and should not be used for animal or preclinical non-human studies.

Bio-layer interferometry (BLI) is a powerful tool that can provide information on binding kinetics between antibody-antigen interactions [21]. Binding affinities between capture/detection molecules and their targets is an excellent metric to use for assay troubleshooting, and for lot-to-lot bridging of all CR. The binding affinity between recombinant p75(ECD) and both capture and detection antibodies was measured using the Sartorius Octet R8. BLI data is shown in Figure S1. Global fitting parameters suggest that the capture and detection antibodies bind to the recombinant p75(ECD) with affinities (KD) of 9.80x109 ±2.87x1010 M and 7.91x109 ±1.47x1010 M, respectively. When procuring reagents, assessing antibody binding affinities can be useful in gauging their binding performance in the assay. With the addition of a correction factor to the reagent’s concentration, lot-to-lot variability may be mediated without the need to perform antibody titrations.

### Parallelism

3.3.

To assess parallelism for the assay presented in this paper, six individual normal human urine samples were prepared with an MRD of 4-fold diluted in SuperBlock T20. Each prepared sample was tested at four different dilutions: 1-, 2-, 4- and 8-fold diluted in SuperBlock T20. Parallelism results are shown in Figure 4(a). The assay saw acceptable linearity across all dilutions in both signal space and calculated concentrations. Calculated concentrations were then normalized and compared at each dilution by dividing by the measured concentration at the dilution factor of 1. The acceptance criteria is that for each normalized dilution, parallelism %RE for four out of six must be 100% ± 20%. These data suggest that an MRD of 4-fold is sufficient to produce reliable results from individual urine samples. (b) Jitterplot of the calculated concentrations of p75(ECD) from 63 individual urine samples. Dotted lines represent ±1 standard deviation from the average signal.

### Stability

3.4.

Bench-top stability was tested by allowing samples to thaw and remain at room-temperature for up to 48 hours. %RE of all eQC levels are reported at < 19% which confirms that samples can remain at ambient temperature for up to at least 48 hours and still provide reliable results.

Freeze-thaw stability was assessed for up to five freeze-thaw cycles where, for each cycle, eQCs were kept at ambient condition for at least 30 minutes followed by storing again at −80°C for a minimum of 12 hours. %RE was measured to be < 12% which suggests sample data is reliable up to five freeze-thaw cycles. Data is shown in Table S3.

Coated plate stability was assessed for up to 68 hours. %RE values were < 17% for all eQC levels which suggests that plates can be used for up to at least 68 hours after initial coating. Coated plate stability data is shown in Table S4.

Long-term stability is an ongoing assessment of how long samples can be stored at −80°C. At the time of publication, long-term stability has been tested at up to 118 days with %RE values < 20%. This data suggests that samples can be stored and analyzed at 80°F for up to at least 48 days. Long-term stability data is shown in Table S5.

## Discussion

4.

A commercially available alternative to ELISA methods for the detection of a urinary biomarker of ALS, p75NTR, is currently not published or available at the time of this document. The main purpose of this paper was to develop and qualify a robust method to detect urinary p75NTR that completely uses commercially available reagents that do not need further modification and can be performed at any lab with the necessary instruments. For this purpose, we chose to use the MSD ECL platform. The MSD ECL platform has become widespread throughout the industry, and the instrumentation is widely available at most bioanalytical labs. As opposed to ELISA, ECL uses an orthogonal detection mechanism based on light emission upon application of a voltage across carbon electrodes that are printed on the reverse side of the individual wells. This electrochemiluminescence is generated from a ruthenium label conjugated to a detection antibody. ECL does not rely on less consistent substrates and uses less washing steps. It can provide higher sensitivity and broader dynamic ranges compared to ELISA [22,23]. Compared to the cost of most commercial assay kits, the in-house developed method is reasonably priced per plate. The MSD platform was chosen due to its larger universal availability and ease of customization when compared to other platforms such as the SIMOA, ELLA, and Luminex platforms. If alternative platforms are preferred for other reasons, it is the author’s suggestion that the antibody pair and recombinant calibrator combination described in this manuscript may be used as a starting point in method development.

While these results reported by this assay are an incredibly useful metric, it is also recommended that results reported for p75NTR are normalized to urine creatinine. Normalization of ng of p75NTR to mg of creatinine in urine is the standard reported value [6].

While the MSD instrumentation is widely available, it should not be assumed that all bioanalytical labs have the ability to reliably conjugate, purify and characterize a SULFO-TAG detection antibody. Therefore, in deciding on an assay format, the use of a commercially available species specific SULFO-TAG conjugated anti-IgG would need to be used. MSD provides a large catalog of such reagents. A commercially available goat IgG anti-p75NTR antibody has been used in several publications as a detection antibody, therefore it was selected as a potential primary detection antibody for the ECL method presented in this paper paired with a SULFO-TAG labeled anti-goat IgG as a secondary detection antibody which is available from the MSD website.

To demonstrate the proof of concept, the method was qualified in-house. This level of qualification is appropriate for research use, or to be adapted and validated as a means to analyze a clinical or preclinical endpoint involving p75NTR. For a Pharmacokinetic (PK) assay, the standard curve and QCs use the same analyte that is measured in patient samples. In contrast, a ligand binding biomarker assay typically uses a recombinant calibrator to create the standard curve and QCs. Measuring assay reproducibility using a recombinant calibrator does not necessarily give an accurate and consistent measurement on endogenous analytes within matrices of interest [24,25]. To account for this, the accuracy and precision of this assay was assessed using QCs prepared from the recombinant calibrator as well as eQCs prepared from pooled individual human urine.

According to ICH M10 standards, the assay shows good precision and accuracy for both QC and eQC samples [26]. While QCs are standard across all validated and qualified assays, the addition of eQC passing criteria is beneficial to the data integrity of a study. If a trial is scheduled for an extended period of time, it is inevitable that reagents lots will need to be bridged. By having eQC passing criteria, data integrity is strengthened if different patients from an individual sample over multiple timepoints across multiple reagent lots need to be directly compared.

During development and validation of a ligand binding assays, it is important to show assay parallelism. Particularly for biomarker assays, good parallelism indicates that sample dilution response curves are parallel to the standard calibrator response curve [24,27]. Good parallelism data may provide extremely strong evidence that the calibrator material is suitable for the detection and quantification of the endogenous analyte in question [24,27]. Furthermore, observing parallelism can help determine suitable surrogate matrix/assay buffer and the minimum required dilution (MRD), as well as establish an LLOQ to gain information on assay sensitivity/selectivity [24,27]. Parallelism data is satisfactory, and this data suggests that using MRD of 4-fold is sufficient to avoid any issues seen from the matrix background.

When testing a baseline of healthy human urine samples, 98% fall within the dynamic range of the assay, and fall in the bottom 10% of the overall dynamic range. The ability to quantitate changes in p75NTR concentration in normal patient samples illustrates the intended purpose of this assay. Literature values show that p75NTR values are elevated in disease state by, on average, a factor of 2 [6–8,18]. Since p75NTR levels rarely increase by an order of magnitude, this data suggests that the majority of samples, both disease and non-disease state, will fall within the dynamic range of the assay reported in this paper. Table 4 shows the mean and standard deviation of p75NTR found in individual samples grouped by sex, ethnicity, and age group for comparison. This data suggests that no bias is found toward any particular group of healthy individuals, though this comparison is largely out of the scope of this document.

As biomarker assays are commonly used as exploratory endpoints, there are no regulations mandating stability analysis to be included during qualification runs. However, it is best practice to use fit-for-purpose practice and to assess the stability measured for the desired specimen type [28]. Therefore, this assay measured the following stability parameters: freeze-thaw stability, coated plate stability, bench-top stability (short-term stability), and long-term stability. Bench-top and freeze-thaw stability are important to account for any potential mishaps during shipment and sample re-assays. For each of these experiments, all three eQC levels were measured and regressed from a freshly prepared standard curve. For this assay, plates are coated in advance and left overnight. Measuring stability for a Friday to Monday timeline can be very convenient in terms of project and data efficiency. For later phase trials, or for any reason to test samples in bulk, it is advantageous to prepare multiple plates concurrently to save time across multiple days. To be considered passing, the %RE compared to the expected concentration must be less than or equal to ± 20%.

For any form of regulated or exploratory analysis, it is the responsibility of the individual lab to author their own internal document outlining the strict protocol steps of this method. Syneos Health has authored a document allowing for contemporaneous recording of each step including documenting reagent lot numbers, time, dates and signatures. Inconsistencies in assay steps, such as inconsistent incubations times, can lead to day-to-day variability in results.

The binding affinity between each antibody and recombinant p75NTR target was also assessed by BLI. During development, assessing antibody binding affinities can be useful in gauging their binding performance in the assay. Once the assay is developed, binding affinities between capture/detection molecules and their targets is an excellent metric to use for assay troubleshooting, and for lot-to-lot bridging of all CR and can help strengthen reproducibility across the lifecycle of an assay. This characterization is crucial for long term method success as it is inevitable that various lots of critical reagents will be used across a project. New methods of lot-to-lot bridging of critical reagents using BLI are currently being studied to assess antibody binding performance rather than concentration (or degree of labeling for conjugated antibodies). With the addition of a correction factor to the reagent’s concentration, lot-to-lot variability may be mediated without the need to perform antibody titrations. Our data shows strong binding from the antibody to our targets. These measurements should be reassessed at different time periods (6 months, 12 months, etc.) to understand the performance of the critical reagents overtime.

## Conclusion

5.

In this paper, we have developed an electrochemiluminescence assay for the detection of p75NTR in human urine. This method uses commercially available reagents that show good accuracy in addition to acceptable parallelism and stability. The ability to detect p75NTR in human urine may aid in the development of new drugs combatting ALS. The assay presented in this manuscript presents an inexpensive and convenient metric for the diagnosis and progression of ALS when combined with other blood and CSF biomarkers. The intended purpose of this paper is to share this method for those interested in both pre-clinical and clinical measurements for the diagnosis and prognosis of ALS using p75NTR. At the time of this paper, ALS is the dominant indication that p75NTR is being used for. However, as the knowledge of biomarkers and the ability to detect them in new matrices constantly grows, we hope that this method will be able to be put to use in the battle against many more neurodegenerative disorders.
