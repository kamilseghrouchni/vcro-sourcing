# Astrocyte–vascular interplay in Alzheimer's disease pathology: Novel insights into stage‐specific biomarkers and therapeutic targets

## Abstract

### INTRODUCTION

We examined how neurovascular and astrocytic response alter cognition in Alzheimer's disease (AD).

### METHODS

Cognitive scores, biomarkers of AD and astrocytic activation, vascular factors, and brain structure data from 693 Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) participants were analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), multiple linear and cox regression, mediation and mixed models.

### RESULTS

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)‐C levels varied significantly across cognitive stages (normal cognition [CN], mild cognitive impairment [MCI], and AD). Vascular factors showed stage‐specific patterns with amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau pathology. Higher CSF VEGF‐C and angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) correlated with better cognition and milder brain atrophy. CSF ACE was also associated with slower decline and lower AD risk. The effect of Aβ pathology on cognition was mitigated by CSF VEGF‐C, whereas tau pathology and neurodegeneration were mitigated by CSF ACE. Independently, these CSF factors mediated the association between CSF glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and cognition. Astrocytic biomarkers mediated AD pathology–vascular links.

### DISCUSSION

Dynamic astrocyte–vascular interplay in AD pathology could influence cognition, considered as a potential therapeutic target.

## INTRODUCTION

1

More than 50 million people worldwide are living with dementia, of which 60%–70% is attributed to Alzheimer's disease (AD); this number is projected to increase to152.8 million by 2050. 1 The pathological hallmarks of AD include amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques, neurofibrillary tangles from hyperphosphorylated tau (p‐tau), neuronal loss, and synaptic damage. Growing evidence implicates blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction in AD pathogenesis, characterized by dysregulated vascular factors and elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)–to‐serum albumin ratios (Qalb). 2 , 3 As the regulatory role of the BBB in AD becomes clearer, therapeutic strategies targeting its protection and repair are emerging as a critical research focus.

A novel paradigm, the vascular–immune–neural network (VIN), emphasizes dynamic interactions between immunity and the vascular–neural axis, whereas evidence in frontotemporal dementia shows interconnected vascular, astrocytic, and glymphatic dysfunction mediating cognitive decline. 4 , 5 The neurovascular unit, including pericytes and astrocyte, is pivotal in maintaining BBB integrity and fluid circulation, both of which are disrupted in AD. 6 Astrocytes, in particular, support BBB homeostasis through their perivascular endfeet, which secrete regulatory factors and express specialized proteins. 7 For instance, mesencephalic astrocyte‐derived neurotrophic factor (MANF) demonstrates significant neuroprotective and anti‐inflammatory properties, potentially aiding BBB repair. 8 Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a key marker of astrocyte activation, is significantly upregulated during reactive gliosis. 9 Aquaporin‐4 (AQP4), localized predominantly in astrocytic endfeet, regulates cerebral water homeostasis and influences BBB permeability. 10 In AD, astrocytes undergo hyperactivation, enhancing their ensheathment of cerebral vasculature. 11 This reactive astrogliosis may exacerbate vascular dysfunction by disrupting vascular tone and impairing cerebral blood flow, potentially accelerating to disease progression. 12

Vascular‐related factors are increasingly implicated in AD pathology. Apelin and angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) regulate vascular tone and homeostasis. 13 , 14 Pathological angiogenesis, associated with cerebrovascular damage and tau pathology, involves elevated CSF levels of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway components (soluble VEGF receptor 1 [sVEGFR1], VEGF‐D), and placental growth factor (PGF), and interleukin‐8 (IL‐8) in patients with AD. 15 , 16 Furthermore, aberrant expression of urokinase‐type plasminogen activator (uPA) may modulate vascular maturation and neuroprotection. 17 These factors may accompany neurodegenerative changes, neuroinflammation, and BBB disruption, participating in these pathological processes through concentration changes that either compensate or exacerbate. Vascular‐targeted interventions hold great potential and promise as a novel therapeutic strategy for AD.

This study aimed to elucidate how AD pathology drives astrocyte dysfunction, leading to changes in vascular homeostasis and cognition, and to identify novel therapeutic targets. We hypothesized that AD pathology induces astrocyte activation, resulting in BBB disruption, vascular dysfunction, and pathological angiogenesis, ultimately contributing to cognitive decline.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

2

### Study participants

2.1

Data for this study came from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) (https://adni.loni.usc.edu), a comprehensive repository that provides access to neuroimaging, clinical, and biomarker data that are critical for rigorous and reproducible research on neurodegenerative diseases. 18

A total of 693 participants meeting the inclusion criteria were enrolled, with data on clinical conditions, cognitive function scores, brain structural measurements, CSF or plasma biomarkers of AD and astrocyte, and CSF vascular factors. All participants provided written informed consent, ensuring that they were fully informed about the study's objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits in accordance with ethical guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review boards at all participating ADNI institutions, ensuring full compliance with ethical standards and regulations for human subject protection.

### Measurements of cognitive function

2.2

Cognitive function was assessed using multiple standardized cognitive evaluation scales, including the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB). The MMSE evaluates global cognitive status, with lower scores indicating greater cognitive impairment. 19 The CDR‐SB assesses dementia severity, with higher scores reflecting increased cognitive and functional decline. 20 Cognitive assessments were conducted over a 10‐year follow‐up, with each participant completing at least one evaluation in addition to baseline.

### Measurements of CSF biomarkers

2.3

CSF biomarkers—specifically Aβ42, p‐tau181, and total tau (t‐tau)–were measured using automated Roche Elecsys and cobas e 601 immunoassay analyzer systems. 21 These data were extracted from the “ADNIMERGE‐Key ADNI Tables Merged into One Table.” All CSF biomarker assays were performed in duplicate, and the results were averaged. Longitudinal CSF assessments of AD core biomarkers were conducted over 10 years, and every participant completed at least one follow‐up beyond baseline. CSF vascular factors (VEGF‐C, VEGF‐D, PGF, angiopoietin‐2 [ANGPT‐2], sVEGFR2, sVEGFR1, IL‐8, uPA, Apelin, ACE, and MANF), and astrocytic biomarkers (GFAP and AQP4) were extracted from the “CruchagaLab CSF SOMAscan7k Protein matrix postQC” dataset, with protein levels reported as relative fluorescence unit (RFU) based on the Soma scan assay. 22 For sensitivity analyses, CSF levels of VEGF‐A (pg/mL), PGF (pg/mL), ANGPT‐2 (ng/mL), IL‐8 (pg/mL), and ACE (ng/mL) were sourced from the Biomarkers Consortium Project, “Use of Targeted Multiplex Proteomic Strategies to Identify Novel CSF Biomarkers in AD,” using the Luminex xMAP platform with MyriadRBM panels (3–24 assays per panel). 23

### Measurement of brain structure

2.4

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were acquired using Siemens Trio 3.0 T and Vision 1.5 T scanners (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). Regional volumetric measures (V) were derived with the FreeSurfer software (versions 4.3 and 5.1 for the 1.5 T and 3.0 T datasets, respectively). 24 The analyzed brain structural indices included ventricular volume (ventricular‐V), hippocampal volume (hippocampal‐V), whole‐brain volume (whole brain‐V), fusiform volume (fusiform‐V), middle temporal lobe volume (MTL‐V), and entorhinal cortex thickness (EC‐THK).

### Measurements of plasma biomarkers

2.5

Plasma samples from participants are being analyzed by the Biomarker Core in batches approximately every 4 months using the validated and fully automated Lumipulse chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay platform (G1200) for Aβ42/Aβ40 and pTau217 and the Simoa Quanterix HD‐X immunoassay platform for GFAP. 25

### Group classification

2.6

Participants were classified by ADNI clinical criteria as cognitively normal (CN, MMSE >24, CDR‐SB = 0), mild cognitive impairment (MCI; MMSE >24, CDR‐SB = 0.5), or AD (MMSE scores between 20 and 26, CDR‐SB of 0.5 or 1.0). Participants with subjective memory complaints (SMCs; MMSE 24–30) were included in the CN group based on the ADNI protocol and previous study. 26 Established thresholds for CSF biomarkers were used to define abnormal levels: Aβ42 <976.6 pg/mL (indicating amyloid plaque accumulation) and p‐tau1811 >21.8 pg/mL (indicating tau‐related neurodegeneration). Based on these thresholds, participants were categorized into four groups: (1) A–T– (normal Aβ42, normal p‐tau181), (2) A+T– (abnormal Aβ42, normal p‐tau181), (3) A–T+ (normal Aβ42, abnormal p‐tau181), and (4) A+T+ (abnormal Aβ42, abnormal p‐tau181). 27 Although CSF t‐tau has been removed from the latest ATN framework, 28 it has been linked in numerous studies to more advanced neurodegeneration, alongside neurofilament light chain (NfL). 29 Additional classifications were based on apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele status (non‐carriers or carriers), age (midlife <65 years, late‐life ≥65 years), gender (male or female), and education years (well‐educated ≥7 years, less educated <7 years).

### Statistical analyses

2.7

For the analyses of CSF biomarker measurements, extreme outliers were identified and removed using the three‐standard deviation (3‐SD) method. Fifteen participants were excluded. To achieve or approximate a normal distribution, all biomarker values and cognitive function scores were initially subjected to a log10 transformation. Following this, the data were standardized on a Z‐scale. Continuous variables were analyzed using one‐way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to assess differences among the three diagnostic groups, whereas categorical variables were evaluated using the chi‐square test. The ANCOVA was employed to compare the levels of CSF vascular factors across the AT groups. Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log‐rank test and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess the association between CSF vascular factors and the risk of AD over a 10‐year follow‐up period. Under the same covariate conditions, Spearman's partial correlation analysis and multiple linear regression were employed to examine the relationships between astrocytic biomarkers, AD pathology, and cognitive function. We employed the “mediate” package in R software to conduct mediation analysis, aiming to investigate whether astrocytic biomarkers could mediate the relationship between CSF vascular factors and AD pathology. All mediation analyses used 10,000 bootstrap replicates. For each mediation model, the following criteria must be met to establish valid mediation: For vascular factors as mediators between AD pathology and cognition, the prerequisites are: (1) AD pathology is significantly associated with vascular factors; (2) vascular factors are significantly associated with cognition; and (3) AD pathology is significantly associated with cognition. For astrocytic biomarkers as mediators between AD pathology and vascular factors, the criteria are: (1) AD pathology is significantly associated with astrocytic biomarkers; (2) astrocytic biomarkers are significantly associated with vascular factors; and (3) AD pathology is significantly associated with vascular factors. Subsequently, we employed linear mixed‐effects models to investigate the relationship between baseline CSF vascular factor levels and longitudinal changes in AD biomarkers and cognitive function. Finally, we performed interaction analyses to assess the effects of age, gender, education years, and APOE ε4 status. Based on the results of the interaction analyses, subgroup analyses were conducted. Selecting another CSF vascular factor data from the Biomarkers Consortium Project may be used to reproduce the findings of SomaScan7k project. Finally, we also used multiple linear regression to further examine the associations between CSF vascular‐related factors and plasma levels or neuroimaging data.

The covariates in all analyses included gender, age, education years, and APOE ε4 carrier status. Intracranial volume was added as a covariate when dependent variables were associated with brain structure. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. All statistical analyses and the creation of figures were performed using R Studio software (version 2024.12.0), R software (version 4.4.2).

### The dynamic changes of vascular factors across different stages of AD pathology

3.2

During the initial stage of AD pathology, we found that VEGF‐C, VEGF‐D, ANGPT‐2, sVEGFR1, ACE, GFAP, and MANF demonstrated significant decreasing trends in the A+T– group compared with the A–T– group. As tau pathology developed in the presence of pre‐existing Aβ pathology, the levels of VEGF‐C, VEGF‐D, ANGPT‐2, sVEGFR2, sVEGFR1, ACE, GFAP, and MANF increased more in the A+T+ group than in the A+T– group. However, PGF and Apelin, in contrast to other vascular factors, exhibited opposing trends during both of the above two pathological phases. Furthermore, IL‐8, uPA, and AQP4 showed no significant alterations across the progression of either Aβ or tau pathology, suggesting that their levels remained relatively stable throughout AD stages (Figure 1, Table S1).

### Associations of baseline vascular factors with AD progression

3.3

Higher levels of CSF VEGF‐C (β = 0.167, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.093 ∼ 0.241, p < 0.001) and ACE (β = 0.126, 95% CI: 0.056 ∼ 0.196, p = 0.001) were associated with increased MMSE scores, whereas elevated VEGF‐C (β = −0.092, 95% CI: −0.167 ∼ −0.017, p = 0.034) levels were also linked to reduced CDR‐SB scores (Figure 2). Regarding AD pathology, CSF ACE levels were positively correlated with CSF Aβ42 (β = 0.268, 95% CI: 0.207 ∼ 0.328, p < 0.001), t‐tau (β = 0.405, 95% CI: 0.342 ∼ 0.467, p < 0.001), and p‐tau18 (β = 0.365, 95% CI: 0.302 ∼ 0.427, p < 0.001). However, VEGF‐C only showed a significant positive correlation with CSF Aβ42 (β = 0.105, 95% CI: 0.038 ∼ 0.173, p = 0.005). CSF VEGF‐D, ANGPT‐2, sVEGFR2, and sVEGFR1 were positively correlated with Aβ42, whereas CSF PGF and Apelin showed inverse relationships. Similarly, elevated levels of CSF VEGF‐D, ANGPT‐2, sVEGFR2, sVEGFR1, and uPA were associated with higher t‐tau or p‐tau181, whereas PGF exhibited an opposite correlation with t‐tau or p‐tau181. Higher concentrations of CSF VEGF‐C, VEGF‐D, ANGPT‐2, sVEGFR2, sVEGFR1, IL‐8, uPA, and ACE were associated with increased MANF and GFAP levels, indicative of augmented astrocytic trophic support and reactive gliosis. In contrast, Apelin showed a reverse correlation with these astrocytic biomarkers, whereas PGF was negatively associated with MANF levels. In addition, CSF sVEGFR2, sVEGFR1, uPA, and Apelin correlated positively with AQP4 levels, whereas CSF VEGF‐C, VEGF‐D, PGF, and ACE demonstrated an opposing relationship. Furthermore, CSF sVEGFR1 was longitudinally associated with Aβ42 levels (β = −0.018, 95% CI: −0.029 ∼ −0.006, p = 0.003). As expected, CSF ACE also had significant associations with change of MMSE (β = 0.054, 95% CI: 0.017 ∼ 0.090, p = 0.004) and CDR‐SB (β = −0.024, 95% CI: −0.040 ∼ −0.008, p = 0.004) (Table S2). Furthermore, compared with participants in the CSF ACE tertile1 group, those in the CSF ACE tertile3 group were associated with an average of 42% decreased risk of developing AD (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.40 ∼ 0.85, p = 0.005). Subgroup analyses showed that participants <65 years of age in CSF ACE tertile2 group were associated with lower AD risk than the tertile1 group. Furthermore, participants in the CSF ACE tertile3 group who were ≥65 years (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.40 ∼ 0.91, p = 0.016), female (HR = 0.42, 95% CI = 0.23 ∼ 0.78, p = 0.006), A+ (HR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.40 ∼ 0.85, p = 0.005), and T+ (HR = 0.32, 95% CI = 0.20 ∼ 0.51, p < 0.001) had significantly lower AD risk (Figure 3).

### Mediation analyses between CSF biomarkers and cognitive function

3.4

Only elevated CSF GFAP and AQP4 levels were associated with cognitive decline. The CSF MANF/GFAP ratio exhibited a strong correlative trend with CSF Aβ42, p‐tau181, and t‐tau levels (Table S3). We further examined the linear associations between each CSF biomarker and cognitive performance, and the results were consistent with those obtained from the Spearman correlation analysis (Table S4). Considering that only CSF VEGF‐C and ACE were associated with better cognitive measurements, we further explored the influences of CSF VEGF‐C and ACE on the association of AD pathology with global cognition. Significant associations were consistently observed: CSF Aβ42 mediated its effect on MMSE scores through CSF VEGF‐C (proportion: 4.7%, p = 0.002), and CSF t‐tau/p‐tau181 mediated their effects on MMSE scores through CSF ACE (proportion: 43.3%–52.3%, both p < 0.001). Furthermore, CSF VEGF‐C (proportion: 34.5%, indirect effects, indirect effect [IE] = 0.034; p = 0.018) and ACE (proportion: 83.9%, IE = 0.080; p = 0.013) accounted for a higher proportion of mediation in the association between GFAP and cognitive performance (Figure 4, Table S5). However, no mediating effect of GFAP and CSF ACE was observed in the path from AD pathology to MMSE scores (t‐tau/p‐tau181 → GFAP → ACE → MMSE), suggesting that stage‐specific change may represent independent processes (Figure S1). Other vascular factors, although not associated with cognition, were significantly correlated with AD pathology and astrocyte activity. Results of mediation analyses showed that CSF MANF mediated the association between CSF Aβ42 and VEGF‐C, PGF, ANGPT‐2, and ACE (VEGF‐C, proportion = 28.4%, IE = 0.037; PGF, proportion = 23.6%, IE = ‐0.027; ANGPT‐2, proportion = 28.4%, IE = 0.147; ACE, proportion = 33.8%, IE = 0.125; all p < 0.05). Furthermore, higher CSF tau/p‐tau181 was similarly associated with increased CSF VEGF‐D, ANGPT‐2, sVEGFR2, and sVEGFR1 through CSF MANF and GFAP (MANF, proportion: 26.0%–51.3%, IE: 0.098–0.190; GFAP, proportion: 7.7% ∼ 49.3%, IE: 0.045 ∼ 0.163; all p’s < 0.05) (Figure 5, Table S6–S7).

### Interactions and subgroup analyses

3.5

Interaction analyses revealed that the significant effects were driven by age, such as in the associations between CSF PGF/Apelin and cognition, and between CSF ACE and AD pathology (Figure S2). The age of participants in our study was approximately normally distributed (Figure S3). Furthermore, CSF VEGF‐C showed a significant negative correlation with age, whereas VEGF‐D, PGF, ANGPT‐2, sVEGFR2, IL‐8, MANF, and GFAP exhibited positive correlations with age. MMSE and CSF Aβ42 were negatively correlated with age, whereas both t‐tau and p‐tau181 were positively correlated with age (Table S8). Next, we performed additional age‐stratified analyses and found that the above associations were more pronounced in individuals aged ≥65 years. However, only CSF ACE was still associated with cognition in the two subgroups (Figure S4–S5). Performing further mediation analyses reproduced in both subgroups, CSF MANF mediated the associations of CSF Aβ42, t‐tau, and p‐tau181 with ACE, whereas ACE mediated the relationship of CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181 with MMSE (more prominently in participants ≥65 years of age). However, only in the ≥65 year age group did GFAP mediate the association of CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181 with MMSE, ACE mediate the association between GFAP and MMSE, and VEGF‐C mediate the associations of Aβ or GFAP with MMSE (Figure S6–S7).

### Sensitivity analyses

3.6

Although the targeted multiplex proteomic method shows reduced precision compared with SOMAscan7k of CSF vascular factor measurements in 184 subjects, the sensitivity analysis revealed that the characteristics of the population were distributed similarly; only CSF VEGF is different among CN, MCI, and AD. Meanwhile, the associations of vascular factors (VEGF‐A, ANGPT‐2, and ACE) with astrocytic biomarkers, AD pathology, and cognitive function were largely consistent with the findings we described (Table S9–S10). Notably, CSF VEGF‐A levels were significantly and positively correlated with CSF p‐tau181 and t‐tau levels. In addition, CSF VEGF‐A, PGF, ANGPT‐2, and ACE demonstrated significant positive correlations with age, whereas IL‐8 showed no association with age (Table S11).

### Associations of CSF VEGF‐C and ACE with brain structure and plasma measurements

3.7

In both the overall population and the sensitivity analysis, only CSF VEGF‐C and ACE displayed stable associations with cognitive function. Accordingly, we further explored their relationships with plasma levels and neuroimaging data to elucidate their potential roles. Despite a reduction of seven individuals in the imaging cohort, CSF VEGF‐C continued to show significant differences across cognitive stages (Table S12). Regarding brain structure, ventricular volume increased with age, whereas volumes of other brain regions generally decreased. For plasma biomarkers, only plasma Aβ40 and GFAP exhibited significant positive correlations with age (Table S13). Elevated CSF VEGF‐C and ACE levels were both associated with ventricular enlargement and atrophy across multiple brain regions, with the association for ACE being more pronounced. Consistent with CSF AD biomarker results, CSF VEGF‐C was positively correlated with plasma Aβ42 (β = 0.249, 95% CI: 0.003–0.494, p = 0.048), whereas CSF ACE showed a negative correlation with plasma p‐tau217 (β = −0.268, 95% CI: −0.502 to −0.035, p = 0.025) (Table S14).

## DISCUSSION

4

Our findings provide novel insights into the neurovascular unit's role in AD pathogenesis. With AD progression, neuroinflammatory responses are enhanced, causing disrupted BBB. Astrocytes, normally critical for BBB maintenance, undergo reactive transformation with changed secretion of GFAP, MANF, and AQP4, thereby influencing angiogenic, vasoactive, and inflammatory mediators secreted by endothelial and choroid plexus epithelial cells. These changes collectively mediate the relationship between AD pathology and cognition, with CSF VEGF‐C and ACE possibly contributing to attenuating cognitive decline (Figure 6).

Decreased GFAP and MANF reflect diminished astrocytic activity and weakened neuroprotection, whereas stable IL‐8 and uPA suggest preserved glial homeostasis. In the following tau pathology and neurodegeneration stage, ACE upregulation and Apelin downregulation modulate vasomotion, and VEGF‐C/D, ANGPT‐2, and sVEGFR1/2 rise, whereas PGF declines, marking a shift from vascular repair to remodeling and neuroprotection. We observed reciprocal, stage‐specific shifts in vascular factors. Levels of VEGF‐C, VEGF‐D, ANGPT‐2, sVEGFR1, and ACE were suppressed in the A+T– stage but elevated in A+T+, a pattern reversed for PGF and Apelin. These shifts aligned with their positive and negative correlations with CSF Aβ42, respectively. These results align with mechanistic evidence. Aβ oligomers exert cytotoxicity on fibroblasts and neurons, the main sources of CSF VEGF‐C/D, leading to reduced secretion. 30 , 31 Aβ plaques also impair endothelial proliferation and function, resulting in decreased sVEGFR1 and sVEGFR2. 32 , 33 , 34 In addition, Aβ disrupts vascular stability by downregulating ANGPT‐2, while inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells (NF‐κB) activation, which enhances PGF expression. 35 , 36 Increased CSF ACE acts as a compensatory mechanism that promotes Aβ degradation and clearance, thereby sustaining higher CSF Aβ42 concentrations. 37 Apelin expression may be upregulated to counteract Aβ‐induced oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. 38 Meanwhile, higher VEGF‐D, ANGPT‐2, sVEGFR1/2, uPA, and ACE were associated with elevated t‐tau or p‐tau181, with PGF showing the opposite trend. Pathology of tau and neurodegeneration drive upregulation of angiogenic factors, including sVEGFR1/2, VEGF‐C/D, and ANGPT‐2 levels. 3 Furthermore, tau‐induced oxidative stress also activates NF‐κB, which upregulates ACE transcription. 39 VEGF‐A may also be upregulated through Hypoxia‐Inducible Factor‐1 alpha (HIF‐1α), involved in tau hyperphosphorylation, with reduced CSF PGF, suggesting impaired vascular adaptation. 40 PGF can compete with VEGF‐A for binding to VEGFR1, influencing the alternating and compensatory dynamics between VEGF‐A and VEGF‐C. 41 This finding explains why CSF VEGF‐C and VEGF‐A showed opposite patterns of association with CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181.

CSF VEGF‐C and ACE uniquely showed direct cognitive links. VEGF‐C was associated with better cognitive scores and higher Aβ42, and was established as a specific mediator of Aβ pathology on cognition. Mechanistically, Aβ pathology activates death receptor in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), inducing apoptosis and reducing VEGF‐C production. 42 , 43 Furthermore, VEGF‐C binding to VEGFR‐3 enhances meningeal lymphatic vessel growth, facilitating Aβ clearance, and it may have anti‐tau effects, from the brain and ultimately improving cognitive function in patients. 4 , 44 Second, CSF ACE was associated with higher baseline CSF and plasma tau biomarkers and better cognitive performances at baseline, with longitudinally reduced cognitive decline, and with predicted 42% lower AD risk. Although both ACE and VEGF‐C had a mediation role between AD pathology and global cognition, the effect of ACE appeared more pronounced. CSF ACE mediated the associations of tau pathology and neurodegeneration with cognition, accounting for 43.3% to 52.3% of the total effects. Pathology of tau drives ACE elevation through two complementary mechanisms: ROS generation that activates NF‐κB signaling to upregulate ACE expression, 45 , 46 and neurodegeneration‐related BBB disruption that permits peripheral ACE entry into the CSF. Higher CSF ACE may support cognition by maintaining neurovascular homeostasis through preserved vascular integrity, enhanced Aβ degradation, and modulation of neuroinflammation and cerebral perfusion. As an Aβ‐degrading enzyme, ACE reduces oligomerization and plaque formation. 47 Meanwhile, ACE expression in microglia activates spleen tyrosine kinase signaling, enhancing Aβ clearance and neuroprotection. 37 , 48

Postmortem studies showed higher ACE levels in AD brains compared to controls. 49 Both the brain and blood ACE levels showed significant associations with AD, as well as at the transcriptomic level. 50 Blocking Ang II production with ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) may diminish or eliminate anti‐inflammatory and neuroprotective responses. 51 , 52 Furthermore, it is important to note that ACEIs may simultaneously exacerbate tau phosphorylation and aggregation, potentially increasing the risk of cognitive impairment. 53 A positive association was observed of ACE with tau pathology and neurodegeneration, consistent with preliminary evidence that pre‐clinical AD increases systolic blood pressure (BP). 54 Although ACE levels are typically elevated in patients with longstanding hypertension, the resulting vascular damage and other mechanisms may exacerbate cognitive decline. Intensive BP reduction is effective in lowering the risk of all‐cause dementia in patients with hypertension. 55 A recent meta‐analysis revealed that ACEIs were less effective than angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) in reducing dementia risk. On the other hand, using non–BBB‐penetrating ACEIs was associated with a 20% higher risk of developing dementia per year, compared to other antihypertensive medications. 56 In addition, ACEI use was not linked to more amyloid deposition in cognitively healthy individuals. 57 , 58 In addition to regulating BP and preventing vascular damage, ACEIs may also provide partially neuroprotective benefits by attenuating brain‐derived neurotrophic factors decline, suppressing tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) release, and reducing oxidative–nitrosative stress. 59 , 60 It was reported that tau hyperphosphorylation was attenuated by the ACEI captopril. 48

Our findings suggest that VEGF‐C and ACE may play relatively greater mediating roles in linking GFAP to cognition. As a core component of the neurovascular unit (NVU), astrocytes indirectly regulate pathological angiogenesis through interactions with endothelial cells and pericytes upon activation. 61 Hyperactivated astrocytes further compromise BBB integrity, 7 leading to sVEGFR dysregulation associated with BBB leakage in both AD. Aβ and tau pathologies induce astrocytic hyperactivation, reflected by increased GFAP, MANF, and pro‐angiogenic factors, 62 , 63 which collectively promote vascular remodeling and pathological angiogenesis. Astrocytes also drive microglial activation and release IL‐1β, IL‐7, and TNF‐α, 64 which upregulate VEGF‐C/D and promote lymphangiogenesis. 65 Synaptic degeneration, a hallmark of AD, is accompanied by elevated Ang II from activated astrocytes. 66 ACE was also reported predominantly expressed on the surface of glutamatergic neurons and astrocytes. 50 Through interactions with endothelial cells, astrocytes also modulate angiogenesis and vascular tone, 61 with intracellular Ca2 + signaling and release of 20‐Hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (20‐HETE) driving vasoconstriction, paralleling Ang II effects generated by ACE. 67 , 68 , 69 The mediation effect of ACE between GFAP and MMSE reached 83.9%. These interwoven processes underscore the dual and stage‐dependent role of ACE, positioned at the intersection of neuroprotection and vascular dysfunction, and highlight the astrocyte–vascular factor axis in AD progression. Similar studies have also suggested that the glymphatic system is closely associated with vascular function, and that the neurodegenerative process may be influenced through the regulation of astrocytes. 4 Moreover, MANF, GFAP, and AQP4 mediated associations between AD pathology and vascular factors, emphasizing astrocyte–vascular crosstalk. Activated astrocytes secrete VEGF family factors and ANGPT‐2 to promote pathological angiogenesis, 70 while hypoxia‐inducible factor signaling further enhances VEGF expression. 71

Aging reverses PGF and Apelin roles in AD, driving PGF toward pathological angiogenesis and Apelin toward signaling loss. 13 , 72 This underscores the need for stage‐specific therapies. Age‐stratified analyses revealed that astrocytic regulation of ACE was more pronounced in participants ≥65 years of age, consistent with age‐related neuroinflammatory alterations. Aging microglia show reduced proliferation and mitochondrial dysfunction, and increased secretion of IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and IL‐6, promoting neuronal injury and tau hyperphosphorylation. 73 Although ACE levels are higher in older individuals, microglial senescence limits its protective effects, fostering an inflammatory milieu that accelerates AD progression. Senescent astrocytes activate autocrine inflammatory loops, releasing cytokines and proteases, while GFAP‐positive astrocytes increase markedly. 73 Thus, aging amplifies AD‐related astrocytic–vascular dysfunction. Meanwhile, tau pathologies have been reported to play important roles in the relationship between BP and cognition, with significant age‐ and BP‐type dependences. 74 The observation that CSF p‐tau181 and tau influence ACE through astrocytic biomarkers in older individuals (≥65 years) aligns with and reinforces the previous reports. Preventing vascular aging and reducing cardiovascular events are crucial for mitigating age‐related neurodegenerative changes and slowing the progression of AD. 55 , 75

This population‐based cohort study demonstrated several significant strengths. First, it integrates cross‐sectional and longitudinal analyses within the AT framework to reveal stage‐dependent dynamic changes in vascular factors in AD, suggesting a potential “compensation–decompensation” process that could guide stage‐specific interventions. In addition, vascular factors were identified as mediators linking AD pathology (Aβ, tau pathology, and neurodegeneration) to cognitive changes. A novel “astrocyte–vascular axis” was proposed, positioning astrocytes as critical regulators of vascular dysfunction. These findings were validated across multiple detection platforms, reinforcing their robustness and highlighting the potential of vascular factors for therapeutic targeting.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the ADNI cohort is predominantly composed of White participants, which may limit the external validity of our findings across other racial and ethnic groups. Proposed pathological mechanisms remain to be further validated through animal experiments or in vitro cell models. Although a longitudinal design was adopted, causal inferences were partially limited by insufficient follow‐up data for certain vascular factors. As dynamic contrast‐enhanced MRI data were not available for the included participants, associations between BBB integrity and AD pathology through neuroimaging evidence could not be examined. Future work will investigate the vascular–astrocytic–neuroimaging axis and integrate central and peripheral biomarkers even incorporate multimodal data for a more complete understanding of AD.

This study identifies VEGF‐C and ACE as key mediators linking AD pathology to cognition, with astrocytes serving as a bridge between AD pathology and vascular dysregulation. Our findings reveal stage‐specific vascular changes and identify the astrocyte–vascular axis as a potential therapeutic target, offering translational insights for precision neurovascular‐protective strategies in AD. Notably, caution is warranted when considering ACEI therapy in patients with AD.
