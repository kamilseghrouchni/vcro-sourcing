# Distinct CSF lipidomic profiles are associated with five proteomic subtypes in patients with Alzheimer’s disease

## Abstract

### Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is molecularly heterogeneous. In our previous cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteomic study in AD, we identified and validated five distinct molecular subtypes characterized by neuronal hyperplasticity (subtype 1), innate immune activation (subtype 2), RNA dysregulation (subtype 3), choroid plexus dysfunction (subtype 4) and blood–brain barrier impairment (subtype 5). These subtypes also differed in the CSF levels of proteins involved in lipid metabolism, suggesting that lipid dysregulation in AD might be subtype specific.

### Methods

We performed untargeted lipidomics on CSF samples from 601 individuals in the Amsterdam Dementia Cohort who were previously included in our proteomic study (n = 416 AD, 185 controls). Using the CSH-QTOF platform for complex lipids, 3,532 lipids were detected in CSF, 270 of which could be mapped to 13 different lipid classes. Lipid levels were compared between each AD subtype and controls using linear regression models adjusted for age and sex (R v4.2.1). Lipids with significantly different levels (p < 0.05) were included for pathway enrichment analysis with MetaboAnalyst6.0

### Results

We observed alterations in the levels of 1,893 lipids, with the majority associated with a single AD subtype. Subtype 3 (RNA dysregulation) exhibited the most pronounced alterations, with altered CSF levels of 669 lipids, including triglycerides and fatty acids, which were reduced compared to controls. Subtype 4 (choroid plexus dysfunction) and subtype 5 (blood-brain barrier dysfunction) both had alterations in the same set of 150 lipids, but with changes occurring in opposite directions (i.e., decreased in subtype 4, and increased in subtype 5). These lipids were associated with sphingolipid metabolism and lipid transport. Subtype 1 (neuronal hyperplasticity) and subtype 2 (innate immune activation) had less pronounced differences compared to the other subtypes. Subtype 1 had increased levels of several phospholipids, indicating neuronal membrane remodeling, and subtype 2 decreased arachidonic acid levels, a precursor of immunoregulatory oxylipins.

### Conclusion

Our findings reveal subtype-specific lipid metabolism alterations in AD. Currently, five lipid-targeting drugs are in phase 1 and 2 trials. Our results suggest that treatment efficacy may vary by subtype. Understanding these molecular differences can inform trial design and analysis, advancing the development of tailored therapies for AD.

## Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia and remains one of the most pressing challenges in healthcare and society. The pathological hallmarks of AD are the extracellular accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) peptide in plaques and the intracellular aggregation of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of phosphorylated tau (P-tau) [1–3]. However, the pathophysiology remains poorly understood and is heterogeneous at the molecular level. We recently identified five biological AD subtypes based on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteomics, which were characterized by specific genetic risk profiles and protein signatures that were associated with distinct molecular processes [4]. These include neuronal hyperplasticity (subtype 1), innate immune activation (subtype 2), RNA dysregulation (subtype 3), choroid plexus dysfunction (subtype 4), and blood‒brain barrier impairment (subtype 5) [4, 5]. Another aspect in which these subtypes differed was the proteins and/or specific AD genetic risk variants associated with distinct lipid metabolism-related processes. For example, the choroid plexus dysfunction subtype was enriched for genetic risk variants in ABCA7 and PICALM, genes involved in lipid transport. The blood–brain barrier subtype, on the other hand, had an overrepresentation of carriers with the APOE e4 genotype, which is an important regulator and transporter of lipids and cholesterol [6–8] and is associated with a higher prevalence of amyloid abnormalities at younger ages [9]. Therapies targeting lipid metabolism are currently under development [10]. Possibly, only specific AD subgroups can respond to certain treatments, making it important to understand the extent to which subtypes differ in their CSF lipid profiles.

Developments in analytical techniques have enabled large-scale, untargeted lipidomic studies in CSF. CSF is in close contact with the brain and contains lipids in addition to proteins, the levels of which may reflect ongoing lipid-related processes. Recent CSF lipidomics studies detected between 245 and 1,070 lipids, with both targeted and untargeted approaches being used [11–13]. In these studies, CSF levels of ceramide (Cer) and cholesterol ester (CE) levels were consistently reported to be increased in AD patients, whereas sphingomyelins (SM) and omega-3 fatty acid (FA) levels were decreased [14, 15]. Findings concerning phospholipid levels are conflicting [11–13]. Such inconsistencies may stem from the molecular heterogeneity in AD, emphasizing the need for large-scale cohort studies to provide a deeper understanding of specific lipid pattern alterations and the underlying biological mechanisms in AD. Given our interest in capturing a broader range of lipids than previously reported, we employed an untargeted charged-surface hybrid quadrupole time-of-flight (CSH-QTOF) platform, which provides comprehensive coverage of lipids in CSF. While untargeted lipidomics enables the detection of thousands of compounds current spectral libraries and reference databases still represent only a fraction of the lipidome. As lipidomics resources continue to expand rapidly, the annotation and interpretation of these signals will likely improve in future studies.

In this study, we aimed to investigate whether CSF lipid levels were associated with the proteomic AD subtypes by measuring untargeted CSF lipidomics in the same 601 samples from our previous proteomic study.

## Methods

### Participants

A subset of 601 individuals (age 65.5 ± 9.46 years, 44.6% females), from our previous proteomic study had sufficient CSF volume available for untargeted lipidomics analysis and were included in the present study. Briefly, these individuals were selected from several studies conducted at the Alzheimer Center Amsterdam, including the Amsterdam Dementia Cohort (ACD) [16], the 90 + study [17], the Twin study [18] and the European Prevention of Alzheimer’s Dementia Consortium (EPAD) [19]. Further cohort details and characteristics can be found in our previous publication [4].

We defined AD based on the presence of an abnormal amyloid marker in CSF or on PET (n = 415) and included all clinical stages: 105 with normal cognition, 102 with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and 208 with AD-type dementia [20]. Controls (n = 186) had normal cognition and normal amyloid markers [21, 22]. CSF amyloid-beta 42 (Aβ42), total tau (t-tau) and tau hyperphosphorylated at threonine 181 (p-tau) were routinely measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) (Innotest: Fujirebio, Ghent, Belgium) [23]. The ADC cohort’s CSF Aβ42 levels were adjusted for drift over time, as previously described [24]. Biomarker abnormality cut-offs for the ADC cohort are CSF Aβ42 < 813 pg/ml, CSF t-tau > 375 pg/ml, and the t-tau/Aβ42 ratio > 0.52.55 [25].

Individuals were labelled according to their AD proteomic subtype as found in our previous study. Briefly, in that study we measured CSF proteomics using untargeted tandem-mass tag (TMT) mass spectrometry in 419 individuals with abnormal amyloid across the clinical spectrum (intact cognition n = 107; MCI n = 103; dementia n = 209) and in 187 controls (i.e., intact cognition and normal CSF amyloid and tau). This resulted in the detection of 3,863 proteins. Next, proteins that had observations across all individuals, as well as altered levels in AD compared to controls, were selected for further analysis. This resulted in 1,058 proteins that were then used for data driven subtyping with non-negative matrix factorisation (NMF). NMF is a dual-clustering algorithm that simultaneously clusters individuals and proteins, thereby identifying both patient subgroups and their defining proteomic signatures. Further methodological details are available in the original publication [4]. These analyses resulted in the identification of 5 biological AD subtypes: the first one was characterised by increased neuronal hyperplasticity (subtype 1, n = 137), the second by innate immune activation (subtype 2, n = 123), the third by RNA dysregulation (subtype 3, n = 23), the fourth by choroid plexus dysfunction (subtype 4, n = 76) and the fifth by blood–brain barrier dysfunction (subtype 5, n = 56). All participants provided written informed consent for the use of their data and biospecimens for research purposes. The study protocols were approved by the ethics committee of the Amsterdam UMC (location VUMC).

## CSF collection

CSF samples were collected via lumbar puncture at the L3/L4, L4/L5, or L5/S1 intervertebral space via a 25-gauge needle and syringe and stored in polypropylene tubes [26]. The samples were processed and stored in the Alzheimer Center biobank in accordance with international consensus criteria [26]. For this study, samples were randomly distributed across seven 96-well plates, each containing 100 µl of CSF, and preserved at −80 °C. During the measurement, the researchers were blinded to the diagnosis.

## CSF untargeted lipidomics

Lipid levels in CSF were measured following the protocol published by Matyash et al. (2008) [27]. The dried lipid extracts were resuspended in 110 μL of solvent (resuspension volume), and 10 μL of the resuspended solution was injected for analysis (injection volume). All CSF samples were measured using liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry (MS) (i.e., charged surface hybrid [CSH]-quadrupole time-of-flight [QTOF] MS/MS) at the West Coast Metabolomics Center at UC Davis (Home | West Coast Metabolomics Center (ucdavis.edu)). An Agilent 6530a™ QTOF was used for positive mode (scan range of m/z 120–1200 Da, mass resolution of 10,000 ESI +), and an Agilent 6550™ was used for negative mode (scan range of m/z 60–1200 Da, mass resolution of 20,000 ESI-). Raw data were processed using mass spectrometry-data independent analysis (MS-DIAL) with parameters optimized for the instrument, followed by data refinement using mass spectral feature list optimizer (MS-FLO), to remove potential duplicates and isotopes [24, 25]. Metabolite signal intensities were normalized using Systematic Error Removal using Random Forest (SERRF), which utilizes pooled quality control samples to adjust for batch effects and technical variability [28]. Each detected feature was given a unique identifier based on its retention time (rt) and mass-to-charge (m/z) ratio. Compound annotation was carried out using the in-house m/z–rt databases at the West Coast Metabolomics Center (LipidBlast spectral library for CSF-QTOF) and further verified using MassHunter Quant based on peak shape, reproducibility, and retention time consistency. Additional curation and targeted MS/MS analyses were performed to confirm annotations in both positive and negative ionization modes [29]. Lipids with more than 20% missing data (39 lipids) were removed. For the remaining lipids, missing values were imputed to half the minimum value for the corresponding lipid across the cohorts, as these lipids are likely present at levels too low to be detected [30].

### Statistical and pathway enrichment analyses

First, demographic characteristics were compared between controls and AD subtypes using the Kruskal‒Wallis test and chi‒square test where applicable. Next, differences in CSF lipid levels (outcomes) were tested across all five biological subtypes for AD patients and controls (independent variable, 6-level factor) via linear regression models adjusted for age and sex. No correction for multiple testing was applied, as this study was exploratory and aimed to identify potential lipidomic patterns associated with previously defined proteomic subtypes. We therefore used a liberal significance threshold of p < 0.05 to capture broader biological trends for subsequent pathway-level interpretation rather than to establish definitive associations. Please refer to Supplementary Table 5 for false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p-values if individual lipid levels are of interest, as the use of a liberal p-value threshold increases the risk of false positives, thus, results at the individual-lipid level should be interpreted with caution. Importantly, all downstream pathway enrichment analyses employed FDR correction. After the models were fit, the R package emmeans (v1.5.4) was used to estimate the marginal means for each group. As APOE is a lipid transporter, and the APOE e4 genotype may affect processes related to lipid levels in CSF, we performed an additional analysis to test if CSF lipid alterations related to AD subtypes were dependent on APOE genotype. So we repeated the linear models with an interaction term between APOE genotype (e4 carrier vs non-carrier) X proteomic subtypes on CSF lipid levels (outcome), adjusting for age and sex. We considered interaction terms with Pinteraction < 0.05 to be indicative that CSF lipid levels altered in subtypes were APOE-dependent. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1.

For lipids whose levels significantly increased or decreased compared to those of the controls (p < 0.05) and could be mapped to a known class, pathway enrichment analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 [31]. MetaboAnalyst queries the RaMP-DB, a list that integrates pathway information from KEGG via HMDB, Reactome, and WikiPathways, covering 3,694 pathways associated with metabolites and lipids. Multiple-testing correction was applied using the FDR method. To focus on robus findings, only pathways with FDR ≤ 0.01 and containing at least five mapped lipids were reported.

## Results

### Participant characteristics

A total of 415 individuals were assigned to specific AD subtypes based on untargeted proteomics, along with 186 controls, were included in this study. Supplementary Table 1 provides an overview of the baseline characteristics of these groups, and Supplementary Figures show their distributions across subtypes and significant group differences. The subtype with innate immune activation had the highest mean age (69.4 years), and the subtype with RNA dysregulation had the lowest age (63.9 years). Additionally, the subtype with blood–brain barrier dysfunction had the highest proportion of males (73.2%).

### CSF lipidomic profiling

In total, 3,532 lipids were detected in at least 80% of the individuals using CSH-QTOF. We compared the CSF lipid levels for each of the five AD proteomic subtypes with those of controls. Altered levels of 1,893 lipids (53.6%) were observed in at least one AD subtype compared to controls (p < 0.05), with most changes being specific to individual subtypes (Fig. 1). Among these lipids, 270 (7.6%) could be mapped to known lipid classes. The annotated CSF lipids represented 13 different classes, including 116 phospholipids (27.8%, 35 ether lipids, 9 lysolipids, 82 phosphatidylcholines, 26 phosphatidylethanolamines, and 8 phosphatidylinositols, 43 triglycerides (15.9%), 2 diglycerides, 40 sphingomyelins (14.8%), 18 ceramides, 7 glucosylceramides, cholesterol and 3 cholesterol esters, 37 fatty acids (13.7%), and 2 fatty acid carnitine esters (Fig. 2). The detailed statistical results of all the lipid comparisons can be found in Supplementary Table 2. The levels of many lipids were altered in one subtype only, as shown in Fig. 1, highlighting the heterogeneity of the subtypes. The most pronounced lipid alterations were observed in the subtype with RNA metabolism dysregulation (subtype 3: 669 lipids, 18.9%, 74 were mapped to known classes), followed by the blood–brain barrier impairment subtype (subtype 5: 404 lipids, 11.4%, 156 were mapped to known classes), the choroid plexus dysfunction subtype (subtype 4: 372 lipids, 10.5%, 164 were mapped to known classes), the neuronal hyperplasticity subtype (subtype 1: 243 lipids, 6.9%, 34 were mapped to known classes) and the innate immune activation subtype (subtype 2: 205 lipids, 5.8%, 32 were mapped to known classes) (Fig. 2B). Subtype-specific lipid signatures and pathway enrichment results are summarized below; detailed enrichment analysis results are available in Supplementary Table 3.

Among the five subtypes, the RNA dysregulation subtype (subtype 3) had the largest number of significant CSF lipid alterations, affecting 669 lipids. Among these lipids, 549 (82.1%) had decreased levels, and 73 could be mapped to known classes, including triacylglycerols (TGs) (range min–max of β estimates and p values: βrange = [0.33, 0.58], Prange = [0.002, 0.04]) and fatty acids (FAs) (βrange = [0.34, 0.63], Prange = [0.0005, 0.04]). Among these 73 lipids, oleic acid (β = 0.58, P = 0.009), palmitic acid (β = 0.44, P = 0.04), linoleic acid (β = 0.56, P = 0.001), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) (β = 0.61, P = 0.003), and the triacylglycerol TG (16:0_32:1)/TAG (48:1) (β = 0.33, P = 0.04) were commonly identified across the pathways enriched for this subtype, such as synthesis, secretion, and inactivation of glucagon-like-peptide-1 (GLP-1) (Padjusted = 6.42 × 10−8), incretin synthesis, secretion, and inactivation (Padjusted = 6.42 × 10−8), and transport of fatty acids (Padjusted = 7.14 × 10−8) (Figs. 3C and 5). Triacylglycerols, which are important for cellular energy storage, may also be involved in regulating the formation of stress granules, which are known to form when RNA metabolism is disrupted, as observed in this subtype [32–34]. Among the lipids with decreased levels in this subtype, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), an n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid that is abundant in neuronal membranes and essential for cell survival, was detected [35].

Next, we found that the subtypes associated with blood‒brain barrier dysfunction and choroid plexus dysfunction had the second highest number of CSF lipid alterations. The blood–brain barrier subtype had mostly increased levels of 302 (74.8%) lipids, mostly sphingomyelins (βrange = [−1.03, −0.25], Prange = [1.96 × 10–13, 0.049]), phospholipids (βrange = [−1.13, −0.3], Prange = [1.61 × 10–14, 0.03]), and triglycerides (βrange = [−0.51, −0.23], Prange = [1 × 10−4, 0.046]). Notably, five lipids, cholesterol (β = −0.3, P = 0.02), linolenic acid (β = −0.25, P = 0.02), eicosapentaenoic acid (β = −0.4, P = 0.006), TG (16:0_32:1)/TAG (48:1) (β = −0.25, P = 0.02), and arachidonic acid (β = −0.61, P = 3.25 × 10–05), were consistently associated with pathways related to heme signalling (Padjusted = 3.48 × 10–06), the selenium micronutrient network (Padjusted = 2.15 × 10–04), cellular responses to stress (Padjusted = 0.001), and external stimuli (Padjusted = 0.001) (Figs. 3E and 5). The lipid patterns and associated pathways seem to align with the proteomic signature of this subtype, particularly those involved in lipid transport. These proteins include fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs), phospholipases (PLAs), and lipoprotein and scavenger receptors, indicating the upregulation of lipid transport mechanisms [4], which might reflect a compromised BBB.

In the choroid plexus subtype, many of the same lipids that were increased in the BBB subtype, were now decreased alongside 12 additional lipids, increasing the total number of decreased lipids to 314 (84.4%) (Fig. 4). The majority of the lipids decreased in the choroid plexus subtype were mapped to sphingomyelins (βrange = [0.35, 0.95], Prange = [2.75 × 10–15, 0.003]) and phospholipids (βrange = [0.29, 1.09], Prange = [2.39 × 10–19, 0.03]). Sphingomyelins stand out because of their involvement in the sphingolipid metabolism pathway, which was identified as significantly enriched in our analysis (Padjusted = 0.002) (Figs. 3D and 5). We previously reported that the choroid plexus subtype was associated with a specific increase in proteins involved in sphingomyelin catabolism, such as ASAH1, a lysosomal acid ceramidase that catalyzes the degradation of ceramide into sphingosine and free fatty acids [36]. In this study, we observed that lipids related to sphingomyelins are particularly decreased in this subtype.

Next, in the neuronal hyperplasticity subtype (subtype 1), 243 (6,9%) lipids had altered CSF levels, of which 172 (70.8%) lipids had higher CSF levels than did the controls. Most of these altered lipids were phospholipids (βrange = [−0.36, −0.2], Prange = [0.0006, 0.037]), which are key components of cell membranes. Phospholipids form a lipid bilayer that acts as a protective barrier for cells; supports membrane proteins, receptors, and ion channels; and serves as a reservoir for lipid mediators that regulate cellular processes such as neural homeostasis, the immune response, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation [38]. The 71 (29.2%) lipids with lower CSF levels than those of the controls included triacylglycerols (βrange = [0.18, 0.24], Prange = [0.006, 0.04]) and sphingomyelins (βrange = [0.22, 0.36], Prange = [0.0001, 0.04]). No enrichment was detected for lipids whose levels were altered in subtype 1 (Fig. 3A).

In the innate immune activation subtype (subtype 2), 205 lipids had altered CSF levels, with 132 (63.4%) having increased levels, mainly phospholipids (βrange = [−0.34, −0.21], Prange = [0.001, 0.048]), and 73 (35.6%) having decreased levels compared with those in controls, including sphingomyelins (βrange = [0.21, 0.22], Prange = [0.035, 0.048]) and triacylglycerols (βrange = [0.18, 0.24], Prange = [0.008, 0.04]) (Fig. 3B). Among the increased lipids, the glycerophospholipid PC (32:0) (β = 0.1, P = 0.01) and sphingomyelin SM (d18:1/18:0) (β = −0.2, P = 0.048) were detected. Arachidonic acid (β = 0.34, P = 0.003), which is a lipid involved in neuroinflammation, was specifically decreased in this subtype. However, the lipids associated with this subtype were not enriched for any molecular processes.

Finally, we repeated our analyses including an interaction term of APOE e4 genotype with subtypes on lipid levels, as APOE is a lipid transporter and may influence the CSF lipid levels. We found that the majority of CSF lipid alterations associated with subtype (3,306 lipids, 93.6%) were independent of APOE genotype (Pinteraction > = 0.05). Only 6.4% (226 lipids) showed a significant interaction, with 22 annotated to known classes, and these included PCs, SMs, FAs, TGs, PE and GLcCer (see supplementary Table 4 for all detailed results). The APOE-dependent effects were most evident in subtypes 2 and 3 (36 and 49 respectively out of the 226). However, among the annotated lipids, APOE-dependent effects were primarily observed in subtypes 1 and 2, whereas subtype 3 showed fewer APOE-dependent lipids with known identities. Briefly, in subtype 1 SMs, GlcCer, and several PCs were increased compared to controls only in APOE e4 carriers. In subtype 2, we found that for multiple SMs and PCs the levels were lower than controls in non-carriers, and higher than controls in carriers. Subtypes 3–5 had most significant annotated lipids observed in noncarriers, particularly FAs and TGs, whereas carriers exhibited selective increases in SMs and PCs.

## Discussion

In this study, we investigated differences in CSF lipid profiles across distinct molecular subtypes of AD and reported large-scale and mostly subtype-specific lipid alterations in 1,893 out of 3,532 lipids. The most striking finding was the large decrease in lipids observed in the RNA dysregulation subtype, which was also the largest number of altered lipids compared with the other subtypes. The BBB impairment and choroid plexus dysfunction subtypes also had extensive lipids alterations in the CSF, with a large group of the same lipids altered in both subtypes but in opposite directions. These findings suggest that CSF lipid changes associated with these brain barriers may involve different pathophysiological processes. Future studies should further investigate the extent to which lipid metabolism is altered specifically in response to dysfunction in these brain barriers, or whether such alterations might occur upstream of these processes. Additionally, the neuronal hyperplasticity subtype was associated with an increase in CSF phospholipid levels, possibly reflecting neuronal structure remodeling, as phospholipids are important components of neuronal membranes. The innate immune activation subtype also presented an increase in phospholipids, along with a decrease in arachidonic acid, a lipid involved in neuroinflammation. In summary, our findings suggest that AD patients exhibit heterogeneity in lipidomic profiles, aligning with the proteomic alterations that define these subtypes and indicating that distinct mechanisms contribute to lipid metabolism in AD.

Three subtypes stood out: First, the RNA dysregulation subtype (subtype 3), had the most pronounced decrease in the levels of lipids, particularly fatty acids, such as docosahexaenoic acid, and triglycerides. Byeon et al. also reported reductions in CSF concentrations of fatty acids, including DHA, an ω−3 fatty acid critical for cell survival and known for its neuroprotective role, in AD patients [11]. As a precursor to signalling molecules and a potent activator of a number of gene transcription factors (e.g., peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors), DHA might contribute to its protective effects against neuronal loss in AD [35, 37]. The lower levels observed in this subtype could coincide with RNA metabolism dysregulation. Reduced DHA levels have also been reported in postmortem AD brains, along with a trend toward elevated triglycerides levels. However these findings did not reach statistical significance [11]. More studies including both tissue and CSF from the same individuals are needed to further study if the contrast in increases or decreases reflect matrix specific effects and/or subtype specific alterations. Triglycerides are essential energy reservoirs that hydrolyse into glycerol and free fatty acids to support ATP production via β-oxidation and gluconeogenesis[38, 39]. Their reduction might be associated with exacerbated energy deficits in neurons, impairing ATP-dependent RNA processes such as transcription, splicing, and transport and thus disrupting RNA homeostasis [40, 41]. RNA destabilization under oxidative stress may reflect a cellular environment that favours stress-granule formation [42]. In support of this, our previous proteomic study characterized this subtype by specifically increasing CSF levels of heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) (e.g., HNRNPA1, HNRNPA2B1, and HNRNPC), which can aggregate in stress granules during cellular stress [4]. These hnRNPs regulate alternative splicing, mRNA trafficking, and axonal transport [43–45]. Stress granules formed under these conditions influence mRNA translation and degradation, highlighting the role of RBPs in posttranscriptional regulation in AD [46], which in turn may explain the decreased levels of these lipids. Future research should investigate whether the subtype-specific alterations observed in CSF are also present in brain tissue and whether these changes are localized to specific brain regions or cell types. This could provide a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms linking these lipid changes to RNA dysregulation in AD.

Another AD subtype with prominent lipid changes is the subtype with blood‒brain barrier impairment. In the BBB dysfunction subtype, 302 lipids, particularly sphingomyelins, phospholipids, and triglycerides, were increased. Byeon et al. similarly reported increased CSF levels of triglycerides and ceramides in AD [11]. However, they reported reductions in sphingomyelins and phospholipids in patients with MCI, whereas our BBB subtype had mostly increased levels of lipids from these classes. Instead, the ChP subtype had lower levels of sphingomyelins and phospholipids. Since the previous study did not look into subtypes, the possibility that the observed decreases were driven by the inclusion of different subsets of patients or subtypes cannot be excluded, as we specifically observed such reductions in sphingomyelins and phospholipids in the choroid plexus subtype [11]. The BBB consists of endothelial cells connected by tight junctions that regulate the exchange of substances between the brain and the periphery [47]. BBB impairment and leakage may disrupt lipid transport. This subtype had the highest proportion of APOE e4 carriers. The ε4 isoform is linked to altered lipid metabolism and impaired BBB function, partly through reduced endothelial cell proliferation and disrupted tight junctions [48]. Most of the CSF lipid alterations in BBB did not depend on APOE e4 genotype, suggesting that these lipids alterations for this subtype may not be driven by this genotype, and/or that other factor may have a similar effect on BBB dysfunction and CSF lipid levels. The lipid alterations in the BBB dysfunction subtype are enriched in pathways involving heme signalling, the selenium micronutrient network, and cellular responses to stress and external stimuli. Our previous proteomic subtyping study revealed increased expression of fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs), phospholipases (PLAs), lipoproteins and scavenger receptors, indicating the upregulation of lipid transport mechanisms [4]. Conversely, proteins involved in lipid breakdown (catabolism) were decreased. This lipid signature may reflect leakage of such lipids into the brain as a result of BBB dysfunction. Dietary fatty acids can cross the BBB via chylomicrons and very low-density lipoproteins (VLDLs), where they are stored or metabolized in astrocytes for energy [49]. However, it has been suggested that pericyte dysfunction is associated with increased BBB permeability, and possibly this may contribute to lipid accumulation and inflammatory responses. [50]. Elevated triglycerides could further exacerbate this dysfunction by promoting the formation of chylomicrons, which are large lipoprotein particles that normally do not cross the BBB. Under these conditions, chylomicrons may cross the impaired BBB, potentially allowing amyloid-beta (Aβ) to penetrate the brain and trigger neuroinflammation, neuronal degeneration, and cognitive impairment [51, 52]. In addition to these lipid abnormalities, a key link to the heme signalling pathway is elevated heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) levels in neurons and glial cells, which contribute to iron accumulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis [53–55]. The role of cholesterol in AD pathophysiology is worth highlighting. In general, the upregulation of cholesterol has been linked to the promotion of Αβ formation and increased ability of APP to bind to β- and γ-secretase [56, 57]. Additionally, an association has been reported between high cholesterol and Αβ-induced oxidative stress in mitochondria, which hinders the fusion of dysfunctional mitochondria with lysosomes, potentially leading to an accumulation of damaged mitochondria and, ultimately, cell death [56]. The elevated cholesterol we observed may be related to accumulating Aβ and might as such contribute to oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in BBB endothelial cells.

The choroid plexus subtype had decreased levels of most of the same lipids that were increased in the BBB dysfunction subtype, suggesting that dysfunction of each barrier has a distinct association with lipid levels in CSF. We previously reported that the choroid plexus dysfunction subtype was associated with genetic risk variants in ABCA7 and PICALM, which are lipid transporters [58, 59]. It is possible that disruptions in lipid transport explain the lower levels of lipids observed in this study. Furthermore, our previous proteomic study revealed the upregulation of lipid-catabolizing proteins, such as ABCA2, ApoD, ASAH1 (a lysosomal hydrolase), and various lipases, whereas proteins related to lipid transport, such as APOE, APOC1, LCAT, and CETP, were downregulated [4]. These findings are consistent with the lipid changes observed in this subtype, which are indicative of increased lipid catabolism. This subtype was particularly enriched for sphingolipid metabolism, and its disruption might contribute to abnormal amyloid metabolism. Sphingolipids are fundamental components of cell membranes and play important roles in signalling and membrane dynamics [60]. Disruptions could impair beta-secretase (BACE1) and gamma-secretase activity, which are crucial for the amyloidogenic processing of amyloid precursor protein (APP) [61]. Sphingolipids, which are specialized membrane domains that facilitate APP processing and Aβ aggregation, are also key to lipid raft formation [61, 62]. Given that the choroid plexus is a brain interface with many vesicles trafficked through lipid rafts, disruption of these processes may contribute to structural and functional alterations in the choroid plexus [59, 63, 64]. Thus, future studies focusing on the lipid and protein composition in individuals with choroid plexus dysfunction would be of interest.

The lipid signatures associated with neuronal hyperplasticity (subtype 1) and innate immune activation (subtype 2) remain undefined in terms of their specific biological implications. The observed increase in phospholipids, along with a decrease in sphingomyelins, fatty acids and triglycerides, may reflect membrane or neurite remodeling, potentially influencing cellular integrity, migration and response to stimuli or immune activation. Arachidonic acid, a key lipid signalling molecule, plays a role in microglial activation [65].

Previous large-scale lipidomics studies have consistently reported extensive lipid dysregulation in AD, though the direction and magnitude of these alterations often vary across studies and sample types. For instance, previous plasma lipidomics studies from ADNI and AIBL identified both higher and lower levels of ceramide and sphingomyelin species in AD compared to controls, while PE and TG species had higher levels in AD [66]. Postmortem brain tissue metabolomic and lipidomic analyses from the Knight-ADRC and DIAN cohorts further indicated lower levels of sphingomyelins in autosomal dominant AD compared to controls, but there were both higher and lower levels of these lipids effects in sporadic AD [67], which may reflect disease heterogeneity. On the other hand, tissue lipidomic data from ROSMAP indicated predominantly reduced sphingomyelin levels in AD brains compared to controls [67]. Thus, the lipid classes we observed to be altered in CSF of specific AD subtypes also appear to be altered in plasma and in tissue. It would be of interest for future studies to compare these biomatrices from the same individuals, and to test if AD subtype alterations may explain part of the heterogeneity in previous studies. Furthermore, Post hoc analyses indicated that some of these lipid alterations for subtype 1 and 2 may be depenent on APOE genotype. In subtype 1, significant associations for SMs, GlcCer, and several PCs emerged exclusively in carriers. In subtype 2, multiple SMs and PCs levels were lower than controls in non-carriers, and higher than controls in carriers. This is in line with previous research in mice, which also reported increased phospholipid levels associated with APOE4 expression [68]. Still, it has to be noted that the majority of lipid alterations with AD subtype were independent of APOE genotype, suggesting that other mechanisms than APOE genotype explain these lipid patterns, which is an observation that aligns with previous lipidomic studies in animal models that report minimal APOE genotype-specific differences in overall brain lipid composition [69, 70].

### Study limitations and strengths

In this study, we used an untargeted approach to measure lipids in the CSF, which allows for the discovery of lipids alterations associated with AD subtypes. However as the majority of detected lipids could not be mapped to known classes, a limitation of our approach is that the specific role of these lipids in AD pathogenesis remain unclear. Still, we anticipate that with ongoing advancements in lipidomics, it will be possible to classify some of these lipids in the future. Also, for the lipids that were mapped to known classes, we observed that those altered in specific AD subtypes largely corresponded to particular aspects of processes dysregulated in each of the subtypes based on proteomics. This provides another piece of information that enhances our understanding of the different molecular mechanisms that underlie the development of AD. Another limitation is the relatively small sample size of the RNA dysregulation subtype (n = 23), which may have limited the statistical power to detect alterations within this subtype. Nonetheless, this subtype exhibited the most pronounced CSF lipid alterations, suggesting that its underlying mechanisms might strongly regulate the affected lipid classes.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides further evidence that lipid metabolism is dysregulated in AD, with specific aspects of lipid metabolism being uniquely altered depending on the molecular AD subtype. Currently, treatments targeting lipid metabolism in AD are being tested [10], and our findings indicate that these treatments may be effective for certain AD subtypes only.
