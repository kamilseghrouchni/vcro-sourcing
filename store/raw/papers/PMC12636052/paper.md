# Metabolomic and Immunological Signatures of Asthma Severity in Children

## Abstract

### Background.

Multiple risk factors for asthma severity have been identified by epidemiological studies. Yet, the pathophysiological mechanisms driving the severity of clinical asthma manifestations remain incompletely understood. In asthmatic children, Notch4 expression on circulating Treg cells and levels of circulating GDF15 have been shown to be increased as a function of disease severity, suggesting a contribution of Treg dysfunction to disease phenotype.

### Methods.

126 children with asthma (intermittent = 40; mild persistent = 43; moderate persistent = 29, and severe persistent = 14) and 83 non-asthmatic controls were recruited in the Allergy clinic at Boston Children’s Hospital and from asthma cohorts. Untargeted metabolomic analysis and cytokine profiling were performed in plasma and results correlated with disease severity, Notch4 expression, and presence of other atopic comorbidities.

### Results.

Children with moderate/severe asthma had higher levels of select lipids (triglycerides, ceramides) and carboxylic acids (lactic acid, aconitic acid) and lower levels of amino acids (sarcosine and arginine) and of IFNλ 2/3 compared to children with intermittent/mild asthma. Treg Notch4 expression and GDF15 levels, which increase with disease severity, correlated positively with lactic acid and xanthine levels and inversely with sarcosine and arginine. The concomitant presence of food allergy was associated with alterations in microbiome-related metabolites and allergic rhinitis with marked triglyceride dysregulation.

### Conclusions.

Untargeted metabolomic profiling identified both shared and unique pathways associated with known asthma severity contributors, Notch4 dysregulation and GDF15 elevation, suggesting that different mechanisms may both converge or independently contribute to determine clinical manifestations of asthma severity in asthmatic children.

## Introduction

Asthma is the most common chronic respiratory disease of childhood [1–4], and its clinical presentation is rather heterogeneous [5, 6]. While a large proportion of children manifest intermittent or mild symptoms that are controlled with either rescue or low dose daily medications, a subset of patients experiences persistent symptoms and frequent exacerbations that may be difficult to control despite the use of multiple therapies . Severe and difficult-to-control asthma accounts only for 2–10% of children, yet it results in significant disruption of daily life and in adverse health effects [7, 8].

Many factors have been implicated in affecting asthma severity, including genetic traits, environmental factors (such as exposure to irritants like smoke, air pollution, chemicals), comorbidities (such as obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, other atopic disorders), lifestyle characteristics and psychosocial factors (such as incomplete adherence to medication, poor nutrition, stress etc) [7]. The contribution of these factors - either alone, but most often in combination - has been the subject of extensive investigations. Nonetheless, the biological basis of asthma severity in children remains largely elusive, as reflected in the challenges still encountered by clinicians in the management of children with moderate to severe asthma.

We have previously identified T regulatory (Treg) cell dysfunction as a key pathogenic player in childhood asthma. Specifically, asthmatic children have increased Notch4 expression on circulating T regulatory (Treg) cells as a function of disease severity and IL-6 levels [9, 10]. Notch4 upregulation, in turn, subverts Treg cells into mixed Th2/Th17 effector T cells by Wnt and Hippo pathway-dependent mechanisms resulting in induction of GDF15 and ILC2 cells [10].

Untargeted metabolomic profiling has emerged as a powerful tool in dissecting the pathogenesis of complex diseases, as it can detect the influences exerted not only by the genome, transcriptome, and proteome, but also by the microbiome and environmental factors. Metabolomics has been extensively applied to the exploration of different aspects of asthma in children and has helped elucidating candidate mechanistic pathways [11, 12].

Here we employed untargeted metabolomic profiling to investigate asthma severity in children in relation to Notch4 expression and GDF15 elevation as well as presence of other atopic comorbidities, with the goal to explore mechanisms underlying the previously observed contribution of these factors to disease severity and to increase the current understanding of Treg dysfunction in childhood asthma that may be leveraged in the future for targeted interventions to improve asthma management.

## Methods

### Patient population.

209 subjects ages 18 years and younger were enrolled in this study, including 126 children with asthma and 83 non-asthmatic controls. Participants were recruited in the Allergy clinic at Boston Children’s Hospital and from the EASY (Environmental Assessment of Sleep in Youth) cohort, a NIH-funded study of the home environment and sleep outcomes in children (PI, Dr Phipatanakul). The study was approved by the Boston Children’s Hospital IRB (IRB-P00021163, PI Dr Crestani). Informed consent was obtained from the children’s parents/legal guardians and children’s assent was obtained from age-appropriate participants.

Patients with physician diagnosis of asthma and no evidence of acute exacerbation were included in the study. Asthma diagnosis was made according to EPA 2 guidelines and disease severity was determined either by treating physician during a clinic visit (based on frequency of symptoms) or inferred by investigators based on the medication regimen needed to control symptoms. Information on other IgE mediated conditions (food allergy, atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis) were obtained from chart review. Children were screened for symptoms of viral infections present within the 7 days prior to enrollment. Exclusion criteria included history of prematurity, history of other chronic and/or pulmonary diseases, genetic conditions – including inborn errors of immunity -, use of immunosuppressants (including systemic steroids) or biologic agents, h/o viral and/or bacterial infection within the previous week and use of antibiotics within the previous 6 weeks. Asthma therapies included SABA (albuterol), inhaled steroids (i.e. fluticasone, mometasone etc), LABA (i.e. salmeterol etc), LTRA (i.e montelukast) and anticholinergic (i.e. ipratropium), either alone or in combination. Samples were obtained from patients when they were clinically stable, with no evidence of acute asthma exacerbation. After collection, blood samples were spun, and serum stored at −80C.

### Notch4 expression measurement.

Antibodies against the following human antigens were used: CD3 (HIT3a 1:300), CD4 (RPA-T4, 1:300), Foxp3 (236A/E7, 1:200), Helios (22F6, 1:200), Notch4 (HMN4–2 1:50), CD25 (BC96, 1:200) , CD127 (A019D5, 1:200) (Biolegend) The specificity and optimal dilution of each antibody was validated by testing on appropriate negative and positive controls or otherwise provided on the manufacturer’s website. Dead cells were routinely excluded from the analysis based on the staining of eFluor 780 Fixable Viability Dye (1:1000 dilution) (eBioscience). Stained cells were analyzed on a BD LSR Fortessa cell analyzer (BD Biosciences) and data were processed using Flowjo (Tree Star Inc.).

### Cytokine profiling.

IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IFNα, IFNβ, IFNγ, IFNλ 2/3, Ip10, CXCL10 and TNFα were measured using Legendplex (Biolegend) per manufacturer’s protocol.

### GDF15 ELISA.

Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid plasma from control subjects, and patients with mild, moderate and severe persistent asthma were used to measure GDF15 with ELISA (R&D Systems, Cat#: DGD150 ) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

### Metabolomic profiling.

Non-targeted global metabolomic profiles were generated through Duke core, utilizing ultra-performance liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry. Metabolites were identified by their m/z retention time, and through a comparison to library entities of purified known standards.

### Statistical analyses.

Following log-transformation and imputation of missing values, if any, with the minimum observed value for each compound, each biochemical in the original scale was rescaled to set the median equal to 1 (scaled values). Pairwise comparisons were used to identify biochemicals that differed significantly between experimental groups. Student T-test, Welch T-test for unequal variances and Mann-Whitney tests were employed based on group size, metabolite distribution and proportion of missing/undetectable data. Metabolites with more than 1/3 of missing/undetectable data were excluded from the analyses. Logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the association between selected metabolites and predictors with asthma severity (mod/severe vs. intermittent/mild) as a dependent variable, adjusting for characteristics such as age, gender, ethnicity, sex and age-specific BMI percentile, medication use (inhaled steroids vs others), and presence of other atopic conditions. Categories of 10% increase in Notch4 expression and 50 pg/mL in GDF levels were utilized in logistic regression models. Multiple comparison adjustment was not applied due to the pilot and exploratory nature of the study. Pairwise correlation was used to assess association between continuous variables. Random Forest Analysis was performed using Metaboanalyst [13] to provide an importance rank ordering of biochemicals that discriminate between groups. Statistical analyses were carried out using Graphpad Prism version 7a, SPSS version 27 and Metaboanalyst.

## Results

### Characteristics of the study population

1.

Population demographics and disease attributes are summarized in Table 1. A slightly higher, though not statistically significant, proportion of male participants was present in all groups. Children with moderate/severe asthma were significantly older than controls. A significantly higher proportion of asthmatic children – regardless of disease severity - carried a diagnosis of allergic rhinitis as compared to controls, while atopic dermatitis was more frequent among patients with moderate/severe asthma. No significant differences were noted among study groups in regard to gender, sex and age-specific BMI percentile, ethnicity distribution and prevalence of food allergy. With regard to asthma characteristics, 83 children were categorized as having either intermittent or mild persistent asthma, while 43 were categorized as having moderate or severe persistent asthma.

### Cytokine profiling

2.

Plasma cytokine levels were measured in 120 asthmatic patients and compared between asthma severity groups. No plasma was available for 6 asthmatic patients for the purpose of cytokine profiling. Children with moderate to severe asthma showed higher levels of GDF15 and lower levels of IFNλ 2/3 as compared to those with intermittent/mild persistent asthma (Fig. 1A). Since the presence of food allergy and allergic rhinitis are known risk factors for increased asthma morbidity, we proceeded to compare cytokine levels between asthmatic patients with or without these atopic comorbidities. As shown in Fig. 1B, asthmatic children with concomitant food allergies (n=47) manifested higher levels of GDF15, and lower levels of IFNα, IFNλ 2/3 and Ip10 as compared to those with no food allergies (n=79). Of note, we observed a significant trend present when including patients with FA alone (and no asthma), with the overlap of food allergy and asthma manifesting intermediate cytokine levels in comparison to each individual atopic condition alone (Suppl Fig.1A). Higher levels of GDF15 were detected in asthmatic children with allergic rhinitis (n=102) or atopic dermatitis (n= 53) as compared to children without these comorbidities (Fig. 1C). No other significant differences in plasma cytokine levels were detected in the comparison of asthmatic children with and without concomitant allergic rhinitis or eczema.

### Human Metabolomic profiling

3.

#### Metabolomic alterations associated with asthma severity.

When comparing children with intermittent to mild persistent asthma to non-asthmatic controls, 32 metabolites were found to be dysregulated, with 17 being increased in asthmatic children. The most common alterations involved higher levels of bile acids, glycophospholipids, N-methylglycine (sarcosine), the indole indoxyl sulfate and lower levels of triglycerides (Fig 2A, Suppl. Table 1). In the comparison of children with moderate to severe asthma and controls, 46 metabolites were differentially expressed (including acylcarnitines, glycerophospholipids, triglycerides, amino acids and carboxylic acids) with 30 metabolites being increased (such as glycerophospholipids and lactic acid) and 16 decreased (such as arginine, sarcosine) in children with asthma (Fig 2B, Suppl. Table 2). The direct comparison of asthmatic patients of different disease severity revealed alterations in 33 metabolites, with 22 increased and 11 decreased in children with moderate to severe asthma as compared to those with milder disease. More specifically, children with higher asthma severity showed elevated levels of triglycerides, glycerophospholipids, acylcarnitines, lactic acid, and lower levels of the indole derivative 3-indolepropionic acid and of the aminoacid arginine when compared with children with mild disease severity (Fig 2C, Suppl. Table 3).

#### Metabolomic profiling associated with Notch4 expression.

Notch4 expression was measured by flowcytometry on circulating Treg cells of 101 asthmatic patients, including 64 with intermittent/mild and 37 with moderate/severe asthma. As we have previously reported, Notch4 levels were significantly higher in children with moderate/severe as compared to those with mild asthma (Suppl. Fig 1B) [9, 10]. Median Notch4 expression level was 19.4% (range 1.13% - 55.6%) in the mild asthma group and 29.5% (range 6.54% - 44.3%) in the moderate/severe asthma group. Pairwise correlation was applied to identify cytokines and metabolites that strongly associated with Notch4 expression among asthmatic children. As shown in Fig. 3A–B and Suppl Table 4, GDF15, IL-1b, IL-6 and IL-12, and were found to be positively associated with Notch4 expression at the p<0.01 level, together with 3 additional metabolites (lactic acid, hypoxanthine and xanthine), while 9 metabolites (one acylcarnitine species, two diacylglycerols, two sphingolipids, and the amino-acid related arginine, cysteine, betaine, and sarcosine) showed a strong inverse relation to Notch4 levels. The strongest association was detected between GDF15 and Notch4 levels.

#### Metabolomic profiling associated with GDF15 levels.

GDF15 levels were measured by ELISA in plasma of 119 asthmatics and were significantly higher among the group of 38 children with moderate to severe asthma (median 202.3 pg/mL, range 0 – 754.2) compared to the group of 81 children with intermittent/mild disease and controls (median 71.7 pg/mL, range 0 – 553.2) (Fig. 1A). In pairwise correlations, Notch4 expression was strongly associated with GDF15 levels, as described above. Among measured cytokines, IFNλ 2/3 was negatively correlated to plasma GDF15 levels. When looking at untargeted metabolomic profiling, levels of lactic acid, xanthine and triglyceride species manifested a strong positive correlation with plasma GDF15, while one acylcarnitine, sphingolipid species and sarcosine were found to have a significant inverse relationship with GDF15 levels (Fig. 3C and Suppl. Table 5).

#### Metabolomic profiling of asthma in relation to other atopic diseases.

Having observed significant differences in cytokine expression between children with asthma alone and those with both asthma and food allergy – a known risk factor for increased asthma severity -, we proceeded to compare the metabolomic profiling of asthmatic children based on their food allergy status. A total of 99 metabolites were dysregulated in asthmatic children with and without food allergy. Specifically, 27 metabolites were decreased (including the aromatic aminoacids tyrosine and tryptophan, cholesterol esters, glycerophospholipid, sphingolipids, glycosylceramides) and 72 (including triglycerides, dihydroceramides, the biogenic amines serotonin, GABA, putrescine, the amino acids glutamate and aspartate) were increased in asthmatic subjects with food allergies (Suppl. Table 6). Fig. 4A shows the top 15 differentiating biochemicals identified by Random Forrest Analysis and pairwise comparison of selected metabolites.

Allergic rhinitis also plays a significant role in asthma severity, as patients with both allergic rhinitis and asthma experience worse respiratory symptoms and are more prone to asthma exacerbations, especially if their asthma is severe [14]. When we compared asthmatic children with and without allergic rhinitis we observed differences in 136 metabolites; of these, 132 (mostly triglycerides as well as few amino acids) were increased and 4 (cholic acid, one cholesteryl ester, two phosphatidylcholines) were decreased in patients with both atopic conditions (Suppl. Table 7). Fig. 4B shows the top 15 differentiating biochemicals identified by Random Forrest Analysis and pairwise comparison of selected metabolites.

Finally, we looked at metabolomic profiles based on the presence or absence of atopic dermatitis, which has also been associated with increased risk of asthma exacerbations and hospitalizations [15]. Interestingly, only 2 metabolites were significantly different between these two groups, the biogenic amine gamma aminobutyric acid being increased and the triglyceride TG 18:2_31:0 being decreased in children with both asthma and eczema.

#### Metabolomic predictors of asthma severity.

As a last step, we applied multivariate logistic regression to identify predictors of disease severity adjusting for multiple factors, using a subset of 80 asthmatic children for which complete data was available. Those metabolites that showed the strongest association with presence of moderate/severe asthma in univariate analyses (p<0.005) were included in a multivariate logistic regression model, together with age, gender, ethnicity, BMI, presence or absence of food allergy, presence or absence of allergic rhinitis, and presence or absence of atopic dermatitis. As shown in Table 2, when adjusting for multiple factors, each 10% increase in Notch4 expression, each 50 pg/mL increase in GDF15 levels, higher levels of the diacylglycerol species DG(14:0_14:0), as well as lower levels of arginine were associated with increased risk of asthma severity. Notch4 expression, GDF15 and DG(14:0_14:0) levels remained significant when gender, age, ethnicity and BMI were forced in the model, as shown in Suppl. Table 8.

## Discussion:

In this study, untargeted metabolomic profiling was applied to investigate candidate pathways associated with both asthma severity and with known severity risk factors in a population of Th2-high asthmatic children. We identified both shared and unique metabolic pathways associated with distinct asthma features, suggesting that these pathways may both interact and converge to determine clinical manifestations of asthma. In comparison to children with milder disease, those with moderate to severe asthma manifest dysregulation of numerous lipid pathways (triglycerides, acylcarnitines, and glycerophospholipids), as well changes in carboxylic acids (lactic acid, aconitic acid) and amino acids (including arginine and sarcosine). Many of these same metabolites showed strong correlation with Notch4 expression on Tregs and with circulating GDF15 levels, which we have previously reported as markers if immune dysregulation driving asthma pathogenesis, and were confirmed as strongly associated with asthma severity and with each other in this study [9, 10]. These results suggest that some of the metabolic alterations associated with increased asthma severity may act at least in part through the Treg compartment to exert their effect on asthma clinical phenotype. Other metabolic pathways may contribute to asthma severity by influencing alternative immune players, such as sarcosine, which can affect dendritic cell trafficking [16] and possibly modulate neuronal vagal activity, as it is an intermediate in choline metabolism and can act on the NMDA receptor [17, 18]. Additionally, we observed that the concomitant presence of allergic rhinitis resulted in accentuated triglyceride dysregulation that can contribute to airway inflammation, while the presence of food allergy imparted microbiome related features to the metabolomic profile of asthmatic children suggesting a candidate role for dysbiosis in asthmatic children with concomitant food allergy.

Metabolomics has been extensively applied to the study of asthma, both in urine and in peripheral blood [19–21]. Limited studies, though, have looked at disease severity in children [22]. Our findings both confirm previous observations of candidate severity pathways, extending their validity to pediatric asthma, but also offer new insights into the mechanistic contribution of different risk factors to disease severity.

In our study, dysregulation of several lipid classes was associated with presence of moderate/severe asthma, including triglycerides, glycerophospholipids, diacylglycerols and acylcarnitines. Higher blood triglyceride levels have been previously described in adult obese patients with severe asthma [23] and have been associated with aeroallergen sensitization and with airway inflammation in children, though not with disease severity [24]. In our study, higher levels of triglycerides were observed in moderate to severe asthmatic children independent of BMI. Glycerophospholipids have been postulated to play a significant role in the pathogenesis of asthma. Serum levels of lysophosphatidylglycerol 18:0 are elevated in adult Chinese asthmatics and correlate with asthma severity and control level [25, 26]. Here, we observed a significant increase in glycerophosholipids in pediatric patients with higher asthma severity, thus supporting a broader contribution of glycerophospholipids to disease severity across different age categories and ethnic backgrounds. In our study, moderate to severe asthma was also associated with lower levels of diacylglycerols. Inhibition of diacylglycerol kinases in mouse models of allergic asthma increases diacylglycerol signaling and reduces both airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness, highlighting diacylglycerol signaling as a promising target for future asthma therapies [27, 28]. Our findings provide further support to the role of diacylglycerol metabolism as a mechanism of disease severity and as a candidate therapeutic target in asthma management.

In regard to amino acid metabolism, moderate to severe asthma was associated with decreased levels of arginine, citrulline, sarcosine, and betaine. Arginine metabolism has been extensively implicated in asthma pathophysiology [29–32]. L-citrulline supplementation has been shown to increase nitric oxide levels and to improve control in adult obese asthmatics [33], and arginine supplementation has been postulated as an additional treatment in adults with severe asthma [29, 34]. Alterations in the arginine pathway have been reported also in children with exacerbation-prone asthma [32]. Our findings of lower levels of arginine and citrulline in children further support a role for the arginine-citrulline pathway in pediatric asthma and a potential target for therapeutic intervention. Sarcosine is an intermediate in the metabolism of choline to glycine previously reported as a potential metabolomic biomarker in a model of asthma exacerbation [35]. Our findings of significantly lower levels of sarcosine in moderate to severe disease expand these previous observations to pediatric asthma as well.

Finally, we observed that children with increased asthma severity displayed significant alterations in carboxylic acids. Increased aerobic glycolysis and lactate production in T cells from asthmatic patients may contribute to airway inflammation and elevated serum lactate levels inversely correlate with FEV1 [36] in stable asthma. Here we observed an increase in lactic acid in association with children’s asthma severity. Interestingly, lactic acid showed a strong association with Notch4 expression on Tregs and with GDF15 levels, suggesting a new possible role as a marker of Treg compartment disruption. With regard to aconitic acid, in activated macrophages aconitic acid is converted to itaconate, which has been identified as a key immunomodulatory metabolite in allergic airway diseases like asthma [37, 38]. Our results provide further evidence for a role of the aconitate-itaconate pathway in modulating asthma inflammation and severity, including in children.

Atopic comorbidities, such as food allergy and allergic rhinitis, are known to affect asthma manifestations. Children with food allergies have a higher risk of asthma morbidity, including more frequent daytime symptoms, hospital admissions, and lower lung function values [39–41]. We have also previously demonstrated that the metabolomic profiles of children with food allergy and asthma are strongly skewed towards those observed in food allergy [42]. The mechanisms through which food allergy exacts its effects on asthma morbidity remain unknown. In this cohort, children with concomitant food allergy and asthma showed unique changes in microbiome-related metabolites, including bile acids, polyamines, aromatic aminoacids, in addition to overall changes in lipids. Gut dysbiosis has been postulated as a key component of food allergy pathogenesis [43, 44], and a prominent dysregulation of microbiome-related pathways is present in young food-allergic children [42, 45]. The unique changes observed in the metabolomic profiles of asthmatic and food allergic children in this cohort lead us to hypothesize that food allergy may at least impact asthma morbidity through its associated changes in the gut microbiome. Moreover, children with concomitant asthma and food allergy displayed lower levels of IFNα and IFNλ 2/3, similarly suggesting a possible impaired response to viral triggers as modifier of asthma severity related to the presence of food allergy.

Finally, patients with concomitant allergic rhinitis and asthma experience more severe asthma symptoms and impaired control [46]. We observed a marked increase in lipid metabolites, and especially triglycerides, in asthmatic children with allergic rhinitis, in line with previous observations of a potential link between high triglyceride levels and the severity of allergic rhinitis symptoms [47, 48], which can in turn worsen airway inflammation and affect asthma severity.

Strengths in our study include the recruitment of well phenotyped subjects with different degree of asthma severity and the availability of detailed information regarding concomitant allergic diseases, which allowed us to evaluate contribution of different factors to asthma severity.

Limitations of our study include its cross-sectional and exploratory nature and small sample size, which caused insufficient power to proceed with multiple comparison adjustment. A focus on pathways with multiple dysregulated components - rather than on individual metabolites – was applied to strengthen the validity of our observations. The fact that numerous pathways previously associated with asthma severity in adults were detected in this pediatric cohort supports the validity of our findings despite the limited sample size. Still, our study only provides preliminary evidence role for a role of Notch4, GDF15 and other metabolites as biomarkers of disease severity, whose application to clinical practice will require rigorous validation in larger and independent patient populations. Asthma is a chronic and fluctuating clinical condition, and the cross-sectional nature of our study does not allow to fully assess if and how individual metabolomic profiles change within the same individuals over time in relation to changes in asthma severity and control and in response to therapy. Our study Longitudinal studies that follow asthmatic patients along their disease trajectory will be key in addressing this limitation and in allowing to establish biomarkers of asthma severity for clinical use.
