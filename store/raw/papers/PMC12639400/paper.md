# Plasma lipid metabolites as biomarkers of early white matter degeneration in Alzheimer's disease

## Abstract

### INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by progressive white matter (WM) degeneration. Circulating lipid metabolites may serve as early indicators of WM microstructural changes. In this study, we investigated the associations between plasma lipid metabolites and WM integrity across the cognitive continuum.

### METHODS

We included 173 participants from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (51 cognitively normal [CN], 88 mild cognitive impairment [MCI], 34 AD) database. Plasma metabolites were quantified using targeted lipidomics, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics were derived from 52 predefined WM regions.

### RESULTS

Regression analyses revealed widespread metabolite–DTI associations in MCI, particularly within the corpus callosum. The callosal body and splenium showed significant inverse associations with phosphatidylcholines (PCs) and multiple lysophosphatidylcholines (lysoPCs) species. In AD group, inverse relationships between PCs and the internal capsule were observed.

### DISCUSSION

Circulating lipid metabolites are linked to WM microstructure in both prodromal and clinical AD, supporting their potential as sensitive biomarkers of early vulnerability and disease progression.

## INTRODUCTION

1

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder classically defined by the accumulation of extracellular amyloid‐β plaques and intracellular tau tangles, which drive neuronal loss and cortical atrophy. 1 , 2 , 3 Although AD is often viewed as a gray‐matter disease, emerging evidence indicates that white matter (WM) is also affected. Neuroimaging and neuropathological studies have revealed widespread axonal injury, demyelination, and microstructural WM abnormalities in AD brains, suggesting that WM degeneration is an important, albeit understudied, component of AD pathology. 1 Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) investigations consistently demonstrate pronounced reductions in WM microstructural integrity in both AD and its prodromal phase, mild cognitive impairment (MCI). 3

dMRI, particularly diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), provides a sensitive, noninvasive method to probe WM microstructure in vivo. 4 In DTI, water diffusion is modeled in three dimensions to yield key metrics of tissue integrity. Fractional anisotropy (FA) quantifies the directionality of diffusion (high FA implies coherent, intact fibers), while mean diffusivity (MD) is the overall magnitude of diffusion irrespective of direction. 5 DTI also yields axial diffusivity (AxD) and radial diffusivity (RD); AD measures diffusion parallel to the local fiber orientation, and RD measures diffusion perpendicular to the fibers. 5 This advanced imaging modality has been widely applied in AD research and is capable of detecting subtle WM microstructural changes before overt atrophy, highlighting its utility for studying WM degeneration in disease states. 4

Recent investigations have identified circulating metabolites as novel biomarkers of neurodegeneration in AD. 6 , 7 , 8 In particular, lysophosphatidylcholines (LPCs), phosphatidylcholines (PCs), and acylcarnitines are critically involved in key physiological functions, including membrane turnover, inflammatory signaling, lipid homeostasis, and mitochondrial integrity. 9 , 10 , 11 Elevations in specific LysoPC species, notably LysoPC C18:1 and C18:2, have been correlated with AD progression, suggesting ongoing membrane degradation and heightened neuroinflammatory activity. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Moreover, ether‐linked PCs such as PC ae C44:5 and C42:4 have been associated with tau hyperphosphorylation and increased amyloid deposition, implicating peroxisomal dysfunction and oxidative stress in AD pathogenesis . 16 , 17 , 18 Short‐chain acylcarnitines, particularly propionylcarnitine (C3), have emerged as pivotal mediators of mitochondrial energetics and cognitive reserve. Collectively, multiple studies demonstrate the prognostic utility of these metabolites in forecasting cognitive decline and delineating AD stages, supporting their role as systemic indicators of underlying cerebral pathology. 18 , 19 , 20

In this study, we investigated whether circulating levels of PCs, LPCs, and propionylcarnitine are associated with white matter microstructural integrity, as measured by diffusion MRI, across the AD spectrum. Our aim was to test whether blood‐based lipid metabolites reflect tract‐specific white matter degeneration and could serve as non‐invasive biomarkers of Alzheimer's disease–related pathology. By linking peripheral lipid metabolism to central white matter changes, we sought to provide mechanistic insights into neurodegeneration and to inform the development of biomarkers for early detection and disease monitoring.

RESEARCH IN CONTEX Systematic Review: We searched PubMed, Embase, and meeting abstracts for studies examining associations between circulating phosphatidylcholines (PC), lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC), and acylcarnitines with white‐matter microstructure measured by diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in aging, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and Alzheimer's disease (AD). Prior research supports links between lipid metabolites and established AD biomarkers; however, tract‐specific metabolite–diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) studies remain scarce. Interpretation: In a cross‐sectional analysis of 173 ADNI participants, select PCs, LPCs, and propionylcarnitine were associated with region‐specific DTI indices in cognitively normal and MCI participants but not in AD. These findings suggest that peripheral lipid signatures may reflect early white‐matter vulnerability prior to overt dementia. Future Directions: Longitudinal studies should test whether baseline lipid profiles predict white‐matter decline and clinical progression, clarify underlying mechanisms such as myelin disruption and mitochondrial dysfunction, and evaluate whether lipid‐targeted interventions preserve white‐matter integrity.

## METHODS

2

### Participants and Study Design

2.1

This cross‐sectional study utilized data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI; http://adni.loni.usc.edu/), a multicenter, longitudinal cohort study launched in 2003 under the leadership of Dr Michael W. Weiner. ADNI's primary objective is to evaluate whether serial MRI, positron emission tomography (PET), fluid biomarkers, and comprehensive neuropsychological and clinical assessments can reliably track the progression of MCI and early AD.

Participants aged 55 to 90 years underwent multimodal evaluations, including structural neuroimaging, lumbar puncture, and repeated clinical assessments. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria have been described previously. 21 Key exclusion criteria included a Hachinski Ischemic Score > 4, use of non‐approved medications, recent modifications to allowed treatments, a Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) score ≥6, and fewer than 6 years of formal education or equivalent occupational experience.

For the current analysis, only individuals diagnosed as cognitively normal (CN), MCI, or AD were included. Diagnostic classifications were based on at least one of the following: (1) Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score between 24 and 30 for MCI and 20–24 for AD; (2) CDR global score of 0.5 with a memory box score ≥0.5 for MCI, or a CDR score of 0.5 or 1 for AD; (3) a Logical Memory II subscale score from the Wechsler Memory Scale below the age‐ and education‐adjusted threshold. All participants met the diagnostic criteria outlined by the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke–Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS–ADRDA). 22

Inclusion for this analysis required baseline data availability on DTI metrics, plasma biomarker concentrations, and cognitive performance. Outliers were identified using a ± 2 standard deviation (SD) threshold from the group mean for each variable and subsequently excluded. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, detailed quality control procedures were implemented, including visual inspection of histograms and scatter plots, verification of measurement ranges, and documentation of all exclusions. Following quality control, the final analytic sample consisted of 173 individuals: 51 CN, 88 with MCI, and 34 with AD (Figure 1). A full description of participant selection and cohort characteristics has been reported previously. 21

### Cognitive Assessment

2.2

The MMSE is a well‐established and widely adopted instrument for assessing cognitive function in older adults. Since its development in 1975, the MMSE has undergone extensive validation and remains a cornerstone in the early identification of cognitive decline, evaluation of learning capacity, and monitoring of treatment efficacy. The assessment comprises 11 items that evaluate core cognitive domains, including orientation, registration, attention, calculation, recall, and language, yielding a maximum score of 30. Its brevity and ease of administration—typically requiring 5 to 10 min—make it suitable for use across clinical, institutional, and community settings. A total score of 24 or above is indicative of normal cognitive functioning. Scores below this threshold suggest cognitive impairment and are commonly categorized as mild, 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 moderate, 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 or severe (≤9). 23 , 24 , 25

### Plasma lipid metabolites

2.3

Plasma samples were prepared using the AbsoluteIDQ p180 kit (Biocrates, Innsbruck, Austria) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Internal standards (10 µL) and plasma samples (10 µL) were added to 96‐well plates, dried under nitrogen, derivatized with phenyl isothiocyanate, and eluted with 5 mM ammonium acetate in methanol. Samples were diluted either with 40% methanol in water (15:1) for ultra‐performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) or a proprietary solvent (20:1) for flow injection analysis (FIA).

A pooled quality control sample (Study Pool QC), generated from the first 76 samples, was analyzed in duplicate on every plate to monitor assay performance and batch effects. Two samples (A‐203, A‐254) were excluded due to insufficient volume.

Amino acids and biogenic amines were separated by UPLC (Waters Acquity BEH C18 column) using a formic acid gradient over 7 min, while acylcarnitines and lipids were measured by FIA (3 min per sample). Detection employed electrospray ionization in positive mode on a Xevo TQ‐S mass spectrometer in multiple reaction monitoring mode. Data processing utilized Waters TargetLynx and Biocrates MetIDQ software.

Principal components analysis (PCA) was performed to assess variability, batch effects, and outliers across analyte classes using JMP Pro. Analytes detected in over 50% of samples were included; missing values were imputed at the detection limit. PCA plots of Study Pool QC and study samples indicated minimal batch effects, with most analytes showing a coefficient of variation below 20%. One sample (A‐067) was identified as an outlier due to atypical creatinine and amine levels.

Batch correction may be applied selectively on an analyte‐by‐analyte basis if needed, using Study Pool QC or full‐plate averages for normalization. Overall, batch effects were minor and unlikely to bias study findings.

### DTI Processing and Analysis

2.4

DTI was used to assess white matter microstructure by deriving regional FA and MD measures from a standardized DTI template co‐registered with a white matter tract atlas. Nonlinear registration to each subject's brain was visually inspected to ensure accuracy.

Raw diffusion‐weighted images were corrected for head motion and eddy currents using FSL's eddy_correct tool. T1‐weighted anatomical scans underwent skull stripping and intensity normalization with ROBEX, FreeSurfer, and MNI's nu_correct tool, and brain extraction from diffusion images was performed using FSL's BET. Anatomical images were linearly registered to a modified Colin27 brain template, adjusted for diffusion resolution. To correct susceptibility‐induced distortions, skull‐stripped b0 images were elastically registered to their anatomical scans, with deformation fields applied to all diffusion volumes. 26 , 27

### Statistical analysis

2.5

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.11), incorporating the following libraries: pandas (v2.1.4) for data handling, statsmodels (v0.14.0) and pingouin (v0.5.4) for statistical modeling, SciPy (v1.12.0) for hypothesis testing, and numpy (v1.26.4) for numerical operations. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous data are reported as mean ± SD, while categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non‐normally distributed variables. Differences in categorical variables were evaluated using chi‐square tests Associations between plasma biomarkers and DTI metrics were investigated using multivariable linear regression models.

Models were adjusted for age, gender, education, cardiovascular risk factors (using Framingham score), and relevant medications to minimize potential confounding. Model assumptions were systematically evaluated by examining residual Q– plots and Shapiro–Wilk tests to assess normality, plotting residuals against fitted values to test for homoscedasticity, and calculating variance inflation factors (VIFs) to identify multicollinearity.

To correct for multiple comparisons, p‐values derived from the regression analyses were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). The total number of statistical tests performed was 5439, and the FDR threshold (alpha) was explicitly set at 0.05.

Post hoc power analyses were performed for each regression using Cohen's f2 to estimate effect sizes, with calculations accounting for sample size and number of predictors. This approach ensured that the study was adequately powered, particularly for groups with negative findings, such as the AD cohort.

Several LPC exhibited group differences, particularly in MCI. Notably, lysoPC a C24:0 and lysoPC a C26:0 were significantly lower in MCI compared with CN (p = 0.012 and p = 0.003, respectively), while lysoPC a C28:0 and C28:1 were also decreased (p < 0.001 and p = 0.004, respectively). These differences were less pronounced or non‐significant in the AD group, suggesting early perturbations in lipid metabolism during the MCI stage.

Among phosphatidylcholines (PC aa and PC ae species), several were altered in MCI. For instance, PC aa C34:4, C36:1, and C36:6 showed significant reductions in MCI versus CN (p = 0.014, 0.010, and 0.029, respectively). Similarly, PC aa C36:0, C36:1, and C36:4 demonstrated trends toward reduction in MCI, highlighting selective disruption of medium‐ and long‐chain PC species. For ether‐linked PCs (PC ae), PC ae C44:4 was significantly lower in MCI compared with CN (p = 0.017).

Sphingomyelins (SM) also displayed significant alterations in MCI. Specifically, SM (OH) C22:1, C22:2, and C24:1, as well as SM C24:0 and C26:0, were significantly reduced in MCI relative to CN (p = 0.024, 0.071, 0.005, 0.001, and 0.003, respectively), while some long‐chain SMs (C26:1) showed significant reductions in AD (p = 0.004), suggesting a progressive disruption of sphingolipid metabolism across the disease continuum.

Figures S1‐S4 illustrate group differences in FA across major white matter tracts. Significant alterations were observed in the right external capsule, where MCI and AD participants showed higher FA values compared with CN (0.29 ± 0.03 and 0.29 ± 0.03 vs. 0.27 ± 0.02; p = 0.010). Post hoc tests indicated that the increase was significant for CN versus MCI (p = 0.009) and CN versus AD (p = 0.030), while no difference was detected between MCI and AD (p = 0.961). In addition, the right inferior fronto‐occipital fasciculus showed a significant difference between CN and MCI (p = 0.045).

Linear regression analyses revealed multiple significant associations between DTI metrics and Plasma metabolites across diagnostic groups (Table 4). In the CN group, reductions in AxD of the right inferior fronto‐occipital fasciculus were significantly associated with lower levels of PC acyl‐alkyl (PC ae) species, including C42:4 (β = –0.59, t = –4.29, FDR‐adjusted p = 0.011), C42:5 (β = –0.50, t = –3.52, p = 0.040), and C44:5 (β = –0.50, t = –3.51, p = 0.040). Similarly, AxD of the right medial lemniscus was inversely associated with lysoPC a C18:2 (β = –0.58, t = –4.21, p = 0.016) and PC ae C34:2 (β = –0.50, t = –3.68, p = 0.039). Additional significant associations were observed across posterior corona radiata, posterior limb of the internal capsule, and superior fronto‐occipital fasciculus, particularly with carnitines and lysoPCs. Notably, higher propionylcarnitine levels were positively associated with increased AxD and MD across several tracts, whereas lysoPC species (C16:0, C17:0, C18:0) consistently correlated with reduced diffusivity.

In individuals with MCI, widespread associations were detected, particularly within the corpus callosum and its subdivisions. AxD of the left body of the corpus callosum was inversely associated with PC aa C36:0 (β = –0.39, t = –3.74, p = 0.040), while AxD of the splenium (bilateral and unilateral) demonstrated strong associations with lysoPC a C17:0, lysoPC a C18:0, lysoPC a C20:3, and PC ae C40:1 (all FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05). Additionally, higher lysoPC a C18:1 was robustly associated with reduced AxD, MD, and RD across corpus callosum tracts (all p < 0.05). FA measures, in contrast, demonstrated positive associations with lysoPC a C18:0 and lysoPC a C18:1 across multiple corpus callosum regions, suggesting preserved microstructural integrity in relation to these lipid species.

Among participants with AD, significant associations were comparatively fewer but demonstrated stronger effect sizes. AxD of the left anterior limb of the internal capsule showed a robust inverse association with PC aa C36:0 (β = –0.59, t = –4.03, FDR‐adjusted p = 0.023). Moreover, AxD of the right inferior cerebellar peduncle was positively correlated with PC aa C36:1 and C36:3, while MD of the left uncinate fasciculus and RD of the left uncinate fasciculus demonstrated significant positive associations with PC ae C34:0 and lysoPC a C17:0 (all FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05). Figures S5‐S8 present scatter plots illustrating the observed significant associations.

Overall, these findings highlight distinct and group‐specific relationships between white matter microstructure and circulating PCs, sphingomyelins, and carnitines. In particular, consistent associations with corpus callosum microstructure in the MCI group suggest that disruption of lipid metabolism may be linked to early WM vulnerability during the Alzheimer's continuum.

## DISCUSSION

4

In this study, we demonstrate, for the first time, stage‐specific associations between circulating plasma metabolites and tract‐specific white matter microstructural integrity across the AD continuum. Using DTI metrics and a targeted lipidomic panel, we observed that reductions in AxD and MD in CN individuals were significantly linked to lower levels of ether‐linked PC ae and lysoPCs, whereas carnitine species were positively associated with diffusivity measures. In MCI, widespread associations within the corpus callosum highlight the vulnerability of highly myelinated tracts to lipid dysregulation, while in symptomatic AD, fewer but stronger associations were detected in selective tracts, suggesting stage‐dependent metabolic influences on white matter integrity. These findings provide novel evidence that plasma lipid metabolites serve as tract‐specific indicators of early and progressive white matter alterations, offering potential biomarkers for preclinical detection and mechanistic insights into AD‐related neurodegeneration.

In CN individuals, reductions in AxD within the right inferior fronto‐occipital fasciculus and medial lemniscus were significantly associated with lower levels of PC ae species (C42:4, C42:5, C44:5) and lysoPCs (C18:2, C34:2), suggesting that subtle lipid dysregulation may underlie early axonal vulnerability. 30 , 31 PC ae species are critical membrane phospholipids involved in maintaining axonal membrane integrity and facilitating phosphatidylcholine remodeling; 32 their reduction could therefore reflect incipient axonal membrane instability, potentially predisposing tracts to subsequent degeneration. 33 Similarly, lysoPCs are bioactive lipids implicated in modulating inflammation and endothelial function, 34 and their inverse association with AxD may indicate early microstructural compromise even in the absence of clinical symptoms. Interestingly, propionylcarnitine and related acylcarnitines were positively correlated with AxD and MD across several tracts, consistent with a potential compensatory role in supporting mitochondrial β‐oxidation and energy metabolism under early metabolic stress. 35 , 36 These findings align with emerging evidence linking plasma phospholipids and carnitines to preclinical cognitive decline, 36 , 37 while providing novel tract‐specific insights into the early metabolic determinants of white matter microstructure.

In individuals with MCI, we observed widespread associations between plasma metabolites and microstructural integrity, particularly within the corpus callosum and its subdivisions. The corpus callosum, as the largest highly myelinated white matter tract, 38 is especially vulnerable to early AD‐related pathology, and alterations in AxD and FA within this region may reflect incipient demyelination or compensatory remodeling. Notably, lysoPC species (C17:0, C18:0, C18:1, C20:3) were robustly associated with both AxD reductions and FA increases across callosal tracts, suggesting that specific lipid alterations may modulate axonal integrity and myelin dynamics during the early stages of cognitive decline. 39 Similarly, changes in PC aa and PC ae species may indicate a combination of systemic lipid dysregulation and localized neurodegenerative processes. 40 These findings are consistent with prior reports of corpus callosum atrophy and DTI abnormalities in MCI, 41 and align with studies linking plasma phospholipid profiles to cognitive impairment progression. 42 Importantly, our tract‐specific analysis provides novel evidence that specific lysoPC and PC species are intimately connected with early white matter vulnerability, highlighting potential mechanistic pathways through which lipid metabolism contributes to microstructural degeneration in prodromal AD.

Among participants with symptomatic AD, significant associations between plasma metabolites and WM microstructure were fewer but demonstrated stronger effect sizes, suggesting selective tract vulnerability and potential ceiling effects in advanced disease. AxD reductions in the left anterior limb of the internal capsule and positive associations of AxD and MD with PC aa species in the right inferior cerebellar peduncle indicate region‐specific degeneration, consistent with known patterns of AD‐related WM loss. 43 Similarly, positive correlations between MD and RD of the uncinate fasciculus with lysoPC and PC ae species may reflect ongoing axonal and myelin compromise in limbic–frontal circuits. 44 These results align with prior DTI studies showing reduced AxD and increased MD/RD in comparable tracts in symptomatic AD, 45 as well as reports of disrupted plasma phospholipid profiles in clinically manifest disease. 46 Importantly, our findings underscore stage‐dependent metabolic–WM relationships, demonstrating that circulating lipid species remain informative markers of microstructural integrity even in advanced AD, thereby extending previous observations from preclinical and MCI cohorts to the symptomatic stage.

Collectively, our findings indicate that circulating lipid dysregulation—including PCs, lysoPCs, and carnitines—may drive early axonal and myelin vulnerability in a tract‐specific manner across the AD continuum. In CN and MCI individuals, subtle alterations in plasma lipid species appear to reflect preclinical metabolic vulnerability, potentially predisposing highly myelinated tracts such as the corpus callosum to microstructural compromise. 44 In contrast, in symptomatic AD, fewer but stronger associations likely reflect advanced structural degeneration with selective tract involvement. These stage‐dependent patterns suggest that plasma lipid metabolites could serve as accessible, non‐invasive biomarkers for early detection, patient stratification, and monitoring therapeutic response. Future studies should validate these associations in longitudinal cohorts, integrate plasma and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, and employ mechanistic animal or cellular models to establish causality and explore potential intervention strategies.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference and limits assessment of temporal relationships between plasma metabolites, WM integrity, and clinical progression. The modest sample size, particularly in the AD group, may have reduced power to detect subtle associations amid clinical heterogeneity. While targeted metabolomics offers mechanistic specificity, it may overlook additional relevant metabolic pathways, suggesting the need for untargeted or integrative omics approaches. Residual confounding from unrecorded factors, such as medication use, cannot be excluded, and as our cohort was research‐based, generalizability to broader populations may be limited.

## CONCLUSION

5

In summary, our study demonstrates that specific circulating lipid metabolites, including PCs, lysoPCs, and carnitines, exhibit tract‐ and stage‐specific associations with white matter microstructural integrity across the AD continuum. These findings provide novel evidence that lipid dysregulation may underlie early axonal and myelin vulnerability, highlighting their potential as accessible, non‐invasive biomarkers for preclinical detection, disease monitoring, and therapeutic stratification.
