# Alterations of amino acids in older adults with Alzheimer’s Disease and Vascular Dementia

## Abstract

### Supplementary Information

The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00726-024-03442-1.

## Introduction

Dementia is caused by brain disorders or injuries that manifests as the deterioration of a person’s cognitive abilities, most commonly with difficulties in memory (Arvanitakis et al. 2019). Currently, there are more than 550,000 people in the world with dementia and nearly 100,000 new cases occur every year, with China hosting nearly 1/5 of the global population. The number of people with dementia in China accounts for approximately 25% of the entire dementia cases worldwide (Jia et al. 2020). Dementia patients consume lots of social resources, particularly medical resources, resulting in considerable medical and economic burden. Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) and Vascular Dementia (VD) are the most common diseases in dementia (Scheltens et al. 2021; Bir et al. 2021). There are relatively several pathogenic hypotheses about these two diseases, such as the amyloid cascade hypothesis and vascular disease (Iadecola et al. 2019; Hardy et al. 1992; Frisoni et al. 2022; Karran et al. 2011). Unfortunately, rare effective treatment or prevention has been found on the basis of these hypotheses (Tolar et al. 2020; Livingston et al. 2020).

In the past, the definitive diagnosis of AD was confirmed by histopathological evidence of amyloid plaques and hyperphosphorylated tau tangles in the post-mortem brain (Khan et al. 2020). Nowadays, several strategies have applied to diagnose AD including examination of biomarkers in blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), as well as positron emission tomography (PET) scans with specific probes (He et al. 2020). However, CSF extracting and PET scanning are either invasive or expensive (Morgan et al. 2019; Lin et al. 2019), which make blood biomarkers much more suitable for clinical application. According to Alzheimer’s Disease Diagnostic and Treatment Centers, the diagnosis of VD typically involves lab tests, neurological exams, brain imaging, and neuropsychological tests, yet novel biomarker assisting its diagnosis also needs further investigation. Research into biomarkers for AD and VD has been challenging, yet crucial for differential diagnosis, with previous studies yielding limited progress (Martinez et al. 1993). Given the comorbidity of AD and VD was very common in clinic, it is important to identify specific and sensitive blood biomarkers that are suitable for differential diagnosis of AD and VD. As easily detectable substances, amino acids widely distribute in the blood and are involved in a variety of life activities, including protein synthesis and delegation. Amino acid levels in blood are closely related to the pathogenesis, prevention, and treatment of diseases, making them potential biomarkers for dementia diagnosis and treatment (Molinuevo et al. 2018; Planche et al. 2023).

As a systematic and powerful method for amino acids detection, metabolomics analyzes, identifies, and quantifies metabolites (Patti et al. 2012; Johnson et al. 2016) containing biochemical markers related to cell phenotype (Hu et al. 2019). Metabolomics has the advantage of revealing multiple metabolite disorders, helping researchers identify and observe disease progression (Sriwichaiin et al. 2021). Moreover, targeted metabolomics can provide quantitative analysis of selected metabolites (amino acids as the example) (Zhang et al. 2016; Qian et al. 2023). Recent studies have applied metabolomics to examine alterations in blood metabolite profiles in AD (Qian et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2022); such studies have the potential to discover peripheral biomarkers and identify key metabolic pathways intrinsic to AD pathogenesis (Qian et al. 2023; Varma et al. 2018; Tian et al. 2023). Furthermore, abundant studies identified changes in the blood amino acids profile of AD patients as compared to the control group (Corso et al. 2017; Ravaglia et al. 2004; Ozaki et al. 2022). Notably, few studies involved the comparison between AD and VD (Martinez et al. 1993), suggesting further investigation.

Here, targeted metabolomic analysis was conducted focusing on serum amino acids in Chinese cohort, including normal subjects, AD, and VD patients. Comorbidities with High Blood Pressure (HBP) and Diabetes Mellitus (DM) were also considered, and the patients were then subtype-grouped for further analysis.

## Materials and methods

### Chemicals and reagents

The formic acid used in the experiment was purchased from Fluka (Seelze, Germany). Acetonitrile and methanol were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). All standards and isotopic standards for amino acids and their derivatives were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, USA).

### Participants

The serum samples were collected from 98 participants in China, including 79 from the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University and 19 from Hefei Hospital Affiliated to Anhui Medical-University. The current guidelines for the diagnosis of AD follow either the National Institute on Aging and the Alzheimer’s Association (McKhann et al. 2011) or the International Working group (IWG)-2 criteria (Dubois et al. 2014). For the diagnosis of VD, the guidelines adhere to the criteria set by the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke-Association International (Roman et al. 1993). The exclusion criteria for all participants were as follows: (1) metabolic conditions, such as hypothyroidism or folic acid deficiencies; (2) central nervous system diseases that could cause cognitive decline, such as Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, multiple sclerosis and so on; and(3)patients with mixed dementia of AD and VD; (4) with MRI scanning contraindications. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University, Hefei Hospital Affiliated to Anhui Medical-University and First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their relatives prior to their participation in the study.

### Sample preparation

Serum was isolated from blood specimens, adopted after 12 h fasting and coagulate for 30 min at least, followed centrifugation at 3,000× g for 10 min. Serum was decanted and stored at -80 °C until use. Before analysis, the serum samples were taken from − 80 °C and slowly dissolved at 4 °C. Then, 100 µL of samples from each group and 400 µL pre-cooled methanol acetonitrile solution (1:1, v/v) were added to the centrifuge tube and vortexed for 60 s. After placing at -20 °C for 1 h, a low-temperature high-speed centrifuge (Eppendorf, 5430R, Hamburg, Germany) was used to centrifuge at 14,000 rcf and 4 °C for 20 min. Finally, the supernatant was freeze-dried and stored at -80 °C.

### LC-MS analysis

The Ultra Performance LC System (Agilent 1290 Infinity LC) was used for sample separation with the mobile phase: 25 mM ammonium formate + 0.08% formic acid (FA) aqueous solution in liquid A and 0.1% FA acetonitrile in B solution, using a Zic-HILIC column (3.5 μm, 2.1 mm×150 mm). Samples were placed in a 4 °C autosampler, with a column temperature set at 40 °C, a flow rate of 250 µL/min, and an injection volume of 1 µL. The relevant liquid phase gradient is as follows: 0–12 min, B liquid changes linearly from 90 to 70%; 12–18 min, B liquid changed linearly from 70 to 50%; 18–25 min, B liquid changed linearly from 50 to 40%; 30–30.1 min, B liquid changed linearly from 40 to 90%; 30.1–37 min, B liquid remained at 90%. The same volume of samples was mixed to prepare a QC sample. A QC sample was set for every certain number of experimental samples in the sample cohort to test and evaluate the stability and repeatability of the system. The 5500 QTRAP mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX) was used for mass spectrometry in positive ion mode. The 5500 QTRAP ESI source specifications are as follows: source temperature 500 °C, ion Source Gas1 (Gas1) set at 40, ion Source Gas2 (Gas2) set at 40, curtain gas (CUR) set at 30, Ion Sapary Voltage Floating (ISVF) set at 5500 V. MRM mode was used to detect the ion pairs. All the LC-MS analyses were performed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co. Ltd.

### Statistical analysis

Peak area and retention time were extracted using Multiquant software. Standards of amino acids were employed for retention time adjustment and metabolites identification.

The concentration of all amino acids and their derivatives was analyzed using R version 4.21 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with RStudio and GraphPad Prism version 9.4.1(GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Normality and Lognormality tests were used to examine whether the data conformed to a normal distribution. If yes, the Ordinary one-way ANOVA was used to compare continuous variables between three groups or the Unpaired t test was used between two groups. If not, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables between three groups or the Mann-Whitney test was used between two groups. In addition, the false discovery rate (FDR) was used to measure the proportion of false discoveries based on all p-values. Besides, correlation analysis was used for analysis the relation between amino acids levels and ages or mini-mental state examination (MMSE) variables.

Logistic regression model was used to compute the area under the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) to distinguish clinical states. Besides, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to determine the clinical utility of nomogram based on the net benefit of different threshold probabilities. All the above data analyses were performed using R version 4.21.

The visualization of heatmap and volcano plot was performed using R version 4.21 with RStudio, and the z-score in the heatmap was calculated as follows: z = (x - µ) / σ. We illustrated statistically significant up/down regulation of amino acids via the visualization of volcano plots. All differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

## Results

### Baseline characteristics of study population

The baseline characteristics of 98 participants from China who were involved in serum amino acids analysis were summarized in Table 1. The cohort was divided into three groups: 19 cognitively healthy controls (11 males, 8 females; mean age ± SD: 87.79 ± 5.07 years; mean body mass index (BMI) ± SD: 22.26 ± 1.43), 49 AD patients (14 males, 35 females; mean age ± SD: 84.59 ± 8.43 years; mean BMI ± SD: 24.07 ± 2.69), and 30 VD patients (8 males, 22 females; mean age ± SD: 84.47 ± 6.72 years; mean BMI ± SD: 23.76 ± 2.52). There were 19 (100.0%) HBP patients and 6 (31.6%) DM patients in healthy controls, 33 (67.3%) HBP patients and 10 (20.4%) DM patients in AD participants, 23 (76.7%) HBP patients and 9 (30.0%) DM patients in VD participants.

Firstly, the concentrations of amino acids in different genders were compared. In healthy controls, significantly elevated levels of creatine and taurine in female group than in male group were detected (creatine, P = 0.0155; taurine, P = 0.0241), while levels of choline and citrulline decreased (choline, P = 0.0314; citrulline, P = 0.0458) (Fig. S4B). However, all these changes disappeared in AD participants (Fig. S4D). Among VD participants, the upregulated amino acids in the female group were the same as those in the control group (creatine, P = 0.0002; taurine, P = 0.0439), but for creatine, the statistical sexual difference was much more significant than in control group (control: P < 0.05; VD: P < 0.001). Additionally, creatinine and aminoadipic acid levels decreased significantly in female group compared to the male group in those who had VD (creatine, P = 0.0002; aminoadipic acid, P = 0.0318) (Fig. S4F). The FDR was used to measure the proportion of false discoveries based on the p-value of the three groups (Table S2). In the healthy controls, the FDR of creatine (FDR = 0.0250), taurine (FDR = 0.0269), choline (FDR = 0.0325) and citrulline (FDR = 0.0664) was less than 0.1. While in the VD group, the FDR of creatine (FDR = 0.0003), taurine (FDR = 0.0490), creatinine (FDR = 0.0272) and aminoadipic acid (FDR = 0.0318) was less than 0.1.

### Differences of serum amino acids between control, AD, and VD

Quality control were applied to evaluate the stability and reproducibility of the experimental system. The relative standard deviation values of amino acids in all quality control samples were less than 30% (Supplementary Fig. S1), proving that the data obtained by this system were reliable.

The concentrations of 29 amino acids in serum were examined, and the comparisons between three groups were presented in Table 2. However, there was no significant difference in any amino acid between healthy controls and VD (Fig. 1A, C). The FDR of tyrosine (FDR = 0.0171), and ornithine (FDR = 0.0389) and cystine (FDR = 0.0021) was less than 0.1 when compared the concentration of amino acids between AD group and VD group.

### Differences of serum amino acids in comorbidity

To further illustrate the differences of serum amino acids in participants with HBP or DM (Table 1), these 98 serum samples was regrouped and analyzed. The concentration of aminoadipic acid, glutamate, and isoleucine in HBP patients exceeded over the non-HBP people (aminoadipic acid, P = 0.0075; glutamate, P = 0.0268; isoleucine, P = 0.0318) (Fig. S2A-E), while the creatine, spermidine and threonine levels decreased (creatine, P = 0.005; spermidine, P = 0.0402; threonine, P = 0.0455) (Fig. S2F-H). In patients with DM, the aminoadipic acid and isoleucine levels were higher than non-DM people (aminoadipic acid, P = 0.0283; isoleucine, P = 0.0360) (Fig. S3A-D), while the cystine, threonine and tyrosine concentrations were lower (cystine, P = 0.0123, threonine, P = 0.0266; tyrosine, P = 0.0481) (Fig. S3E-G). The FDR was used to measure the proportion of false discoveries based on the p-value of the three groups (Table S2). Compared with non-HBP people, all amino acids with P < 0.05 in patients with HBP have the FDR < 0.1 (aminoadipic acid, FDR = 0.0075; isoleucine, FDR = 0.0838; creatine, FDR = 0.0081; spermidine, FDR = 0.0614; threonine, FDR = 0.0880), except of glutamate (FDR = 0.7772). In DM patients, compared with the non-DM people, all amino acids with P < 0.05 have the FDR < 0.1 (aminoadipic acid, FDR = 0.0283; isoleucine, FDR = 0.0949; cystine, FDR = 0.0132; threonine, FDR = 0.0514), except of tyrosine (FDR = 0.1550).

Considering comorbidity of dementia with hypertension or diabetes, all participants were sub-grouped and analyzed. It was found that patients with both HBP and AD (AD+HBP+) have distinctly lower tyrosine and histidine than those with HBP alone (AD/VD−HBP+) (tyrosine, P = 0.0043, histidine, P = 0.0475) (Fig. 2A, B, H, I). Interestingly, the tyrosine levels also had a large decline in AD+HBP+ group than in VD+HBP+ group (tyrosine, P = 0.0226) (Fig. 2G, H). Moreover, the results indicated that the lysine and taurine concentrations in patients with both AD and DM (AD+DM+) were lower than those who only had DM (AD/VD−DM+) (Fig. 3D, L, M). In AD patients, when developing DM (AD+DM+), cystine had a significant decrease compared to those who did not have DM (AD+DM−) (cystine, P = 0.045) (Fig. 3F, K). In patients without DM, cystine level in AD patients was significantly higher than in VD patients, corresponding to the overall population (Fig. 3H, K). But this phenomenon disappeared in DM patients (Fig. 3I, K). The FDR was used to measure the proportion of false discoveries based on the p-value of some groups above (Table S3). And in AD+HBP+ group, the FDR of tyrosine was 0.0139 and histidine was 0.1148 compared with the AD/VD−HBP+ group. The FDR of tyrosine was 0.0728 in AD+HBP+ group while compared with VD+HBP+ group. In addition, while compared the AD+DM+ group with AD/VD−DM+ group, the FDR of lysine was 0.2272 and taurine was 0.0100. Compared with the AD+DM− group, the FDR of cystine was 0.0483 in AD+DM+ group. And in AD+DM− group, the FDR of cystine was 0.0117 when compared with VD+DM− group.

### Metabolic pathway of several amino acids

Furthermore, the metabolic pathway was used to display the correlation among some amino acids (Fig. S5). Arginine can be metabolized into ornithine, which further generates putrescine and the ODC1 plays an important role in this step. Spermidine is synthased from its metabolic precursor putrescine and can combines with glutathione to form glutathionyl-spermidine. By this step, spermidine links the arginine metabolism and the glutathione metabolism. Glutathione can further form L-glutamate and L-cysteinyl-glycine. R-S-glutamine is formed by the glutathione and RX, and can be broken down into L-glutamic acid and R-S- cysteinyl-glycine. And L- cysteinyl-glycine can be converted into glycine and L-cysteine with a series of enzymes. The glutathione metabolism connects to the cysteine metabolism via L-cysteine. In addition, arginine can also generate creatine.

### Performance of potential amino acids biomarkers

Based on results described above, some amino acids were identified as candidate biomarkers of dementia. Multivariate ROC curve analysis showed that four biomarkers (creatine, cystine, spermidine, and tyrosine) reached an optimal power in discriminating dementia potentially. Among them, creatine and spermidine showed optimal power in distinguishing between AD patients and healthy controls (creatine: AUC = 0.7154; spermidine: AUC = 0.6756) (Fig. 4A, B). When comorbidity was considered, among those people who do not have DM, cystine was found to have optimal power to discriminate AD (AD+DM−) and VD (VD+DM−) patients (AUC = 0.7546) (Fig. 4C). Tyrosine was found to have the capacity, in HBP patients, to discriminate AD (AD+HBP+) both from those with HBP alone (AD/VD−HBP+) (AUC = 0.7273) and VD (VD+HBP+) (AUC = 0.7246) (Fig. 4D, E). And the creatine and spermidine are combined and showed a higher ability to discriminate AD patients from healthy controls (AUC = 0.7476).

When DCA was used to estimate the differential diagnosis capability of amino acids, the results exhibited that the threshold probabilities of creatine, spermidine, cystine, and tyrosine were 46.9–88.9% (except 52.5–53.5%) (Fig. 5A), 53.6–79.8% (Fig. 5B), 19.3–100% (Fig. 5C), 10.7–76.5% (Fig. 5D), and 33.4–100% (Fig. 5E), respectively, indicating that the model had good application value.

### Correlation between age and amino acids concentration

To investigate the relationships between amino acids and age, participants were divided into healthy controls, AD and VD groups and the correlation were analyzed. The correlation coefficient (r value) was visualized in the form of a histogram (Fig. 6). The results indicated that the levels of 16 amino acids in control group, including tryptophan, isoleucine, valine, spermidine, leucine, phenylalanine, cystine, glycine, asparagine, alanine, histidine, taurine, tyrosine, lysine, serine and creatine declined with age and showed different degrees of weak negative correlation (-0.8 < r < -0.3). Meanwhile, the levels of three amino acids—citrulline, hydroxyproline, and glutamate—exhibited a weak positive correlation with age (0.3 < r < 0.8). Interestingly, this kind of correlation between amino acid levels and age disappeared in AD group (r < 0.3). In VD group, the correlation of glycine and alanine with age (0.3 < r < 0.8) was reversed compared to control group (-0.3 < r < -0.8).

### Correlation between MMSE and amino acids concentration

To investigate the relationship between amino acids concentration and cognitive function, the correlation between serum amino acids levels and MMSE value were analyzed. The results suggested that all the amino acids in control group exhibited positive correlations with MMSE value (0.3 < r < 0.8). But in VD patients, arginine, aspartate, creatine and citrulline showed a negative relationship with MMSE (-0.8 < r < -0.3). Moreover, six amino acids (tyrosine, ornithine, proline, isoleucine, alanine and glutamine) were negatively related to MMSE and opposite to control group in who had AD (-0.8 < r < -0.3). Besides, only spermidine, taurine, and asparagine were found to show a strong correlation with MMSE in patients of AD (spermidine, r = 0.8353; taurine, r = 0.8609; asparagine, r = 0.9300) (Fig. 7).

## Discussion

Here, 98 serum samples were detected, including AD, VD patients and age-matched healthy control subjects, by targeted metabolomics. There were significant differences of several serum amino acids in AD patients, while no difference was observed in VD patients compared to age-matched healthy participants. When considering comorbidity, the result changed in the comparison between AD and VD patients due to the exitance of HBP/DM or not. Furthermore, all the amino acids in control group were positively related with MMSE values and some of them were negatively correlated to MMSE in AD & VD groups. Interestingly, no correlation between amino acid levels and age was observed in AD (r < 0.3).

Previous studies have reported differences between AD patients and healthy controls. Yin C, et al. had reviewed 24 articles on CSF, plasma, saliva, and brain tissue samples from human subjects with AD in the past five years (Yin et al. 2023). However, metabolomic studies on VD have received little attention (Martinez et al. 1993). Our results observed multiple differences in serum amino acid levels between AD patients and healthy controls, including significantly elevated creatine and spermidine, and significantly decreased aminoadipic acid, tyrosine, histidine, creatinine and ornithine. Notably, there was no difference in concentration of serum amino acids between healthy controls and VD patients. This phenomenon indicated a different pathogenesis pathway between AD and VD, suggesting the amino acid metabolism might be a potential target for diagnosis and intervention of dementia.

Among these amino acids, creatine was well-investigated previously. Creatine had been studied primarily as a synergistic aid for training and exercise. In recent years, its effects on brain health and cognitive function have received wide attentions (Dolan et al. 2019; Roschel et al. 2021; Forbes et al. 2022; Avgerinos et al. 2018). Studies have shown that creatine can exert neuroprotective effects by reducing protein aggregation, thereby protecting AD patients from damage and improve cognition or memory (McMorris et al. 2007; Marques et al. 2019). Serum creatine level was significantly elevated in AD patients in our study, which may be due to the leakage from cells. This further reiterates that the exact role of creatine in AD is complicated and needs further investigation.

Previous reports have highlighted the important function of spermidine to reduce amyloid-β plaques burden by triggering the autophagy process, as well as to reduce AD-associated neuro-inflammation and ameliorate AD-related proteomic disorder (Freitag Kiara et al. 2022a, b). Beyond this, some other previous viewpoints also supported that spermidine has a positive effect on the central nervous system (Pekar et al. 2020, 2021; Lumkwana et al. 2022). In our study, serum spermidine levels increased significantly in AD patients as compared with health controls, which is consistent with previous report (Sternberg et al. 2022). However, some researchers reported that spermidine decreased in AD patients (Pekar et al. 2020). Remarkably, a significant decrease of serum ornithine level was observed in AD patients compared to healthy controls, corresponding to previous report that illustrated lower cognitive function is associated with decreased ornithine (van der Lee et al. 2018). The metabolic pathway shows that spermidine is a synthetic product of ornithine. But the concentration of spermidine increased evidently in AD group compared to control group and the underlying mechanism of this disorder is still poorly known (Madeo et al. 2018). It may indicate the ascend activity of ODC1 (Madeo et al. 2018), a key enzyme in the process of converting ornithine to spermidine in AD patients. Interestingly, the correlation between age and spermidine level disappeared in AD patients (r = -0.08725), as compared with that in healthy controls group (r = -0.6049), suggesting that AD incidence may interfere the effect of age on spermidine level.

A negative correlation between tyrosine and the incidence of dementia has been reported, suggesting the possible cognitive benefits of tyrosine (Hase et al. 2015; Cui et al. 2020). Increasing the intake of tyrosine can ameliorate cognitive decline (Hase et al. 2015; McCann et al. 2021). Our study showed that the concentration of tyrosine decreased significantly in AD group compared with control group (control group: 90.43 ± 23.09 µmol/L; AD group: 72.49 ± 14.76 µmol/L; P = 0.0155), which is consistent with previous study (control group: 103.30 ± 18.30 µmol/L; AD group: 91.25 ± 17.01 µmol/L; P = 0.013) (Corso et al. 2017). And the results in the comorbidity cohorts showed that patients with both AD and HBP (AD+HBP+) had lower tyrosine levels than patients with HBP only (AD/VD−HBP+) and with both VD and HBP (VD+HBP+), which was consistent with the results of overall population in Fig. 1K. However, this phenomenon disappeared in the cohort without HBP. Therefore, our results indicate that the HBP factor might be considered in further research about the impact of tyrosine on dementia.

AD have been called “type 3 diabetes mellitus” because it shares the same pathogenesis with diabetes mellitus (Kandimalla et al. 2017). The insulin receptor (IR) is widely expressed in neurons and glia, especially in the hippocampus and hypothalamus (Pomytkin et al. 2018). The damage of insulin signaling pathway will greatly affect cognitive function (Akhtar et al. 2020; Arvanitakis et al. 2020). In addition, insulin-degrading enzyme deficiency in DM not only leads to a decrease in glucose metabolism in the body, but also reduces enzymatic degradation of β-amyloid plagues and leads to mitochondrial dysfunction (Burillo et al. 2021; Farris et al. 2003), which increases the risk of AD in diabetics. Our research found serum aminoadipic acid levels significantly increased in DM patients. Besides, 2-aminoadipic acid was identified as biomarker for DM risk and a positive correlation between aminoadipic acid and DM risk had been demonstrated (Long et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2013). Studies have shown that increased level of 2-aminoadipic acid in the brain can lead to a decrease of neuroprotective astrocytes, and a reducing synaptic connectivity in AD models is reduced (Davis et al. 2021). However, the results of Fig. 1 revealed that serum aminoadipic acid concentration, compared with healthy controls, decreased significantly in AD patients. Although the underline mechanism has not been clarified clearly, our results can provide new ideas for future study, which will provide more evidence to link AD and DM in terms of amino acids.

Oxidative stress is well established as a hypothesis of pathogenesis of AD (Chen et al. 2014; Cheignon et al. 2018; Butterfield et al. 2018; Bai et al. 2022). Several investigators have reported that glutathione (GSH) acts as a major intracellular antioxidant that protects neuronal cells from oxidative stress, thereby reducing the occurrence of dementia, especially in AD (Aoyama 2021). GSH levels were regulated primarily by the excitatory amino acid carrier 1 (EAAC1) transporting cystine into the cell or the cystine/glutamate reverse transporter (System Xc−) uptakes of cystine and excretes of glutamate (Lee et al. 2020; Bannai 1986). System Xc− is closely related to the pathogenesis of AD (Qin et al. 2006). Additionally, as a metabolite of cystine, homocysteine can induce the expression of Herp (Sai et al. 2003). On the other hand, the high expression levels of Herp could contribute to Aβ accumulation, including the formation of senile plaques or vascular Aβ deposits, in patients with AD, which may lead to the increase phenomenon in AD patients compared with VD patients (Sai et al. 2002). Moreover, the homocysteine may cause the neurotoxicity by the auto-oxidation that leads to the formation of ROS, which leads to the neuroinflammation and apoptosis (Skovierova et al. 2016). In patients without DM, cystine levels in AD patients was significantly higher than in VD patients, corresponding to overall population (Fig. 3H, K). According to the metabolic pathway, the increase of cystine in AD patients, compared with VD patients, may due to the excessive breakdown of GSH, which might weaken the protective effect of GSH on neuronal cells. However, compared with VD group, the higher concentration of cystine in AD group disappeared in DM patients, which may be related to System Xc− abnormalities and disorder of GSH synthesis. Therefore, our results indicate that the factor of DM should be considered in relative research about the impact of cystine on dementia.

As one of the branched-chain amino acids, isoleucine can be easily transported through the blood-brain barrier into the brain parenchyma (Smith et al. 1987). And even in the presence of glucose, isoleucine is rapidly degraded and is considered as valuable fuel molecules in brain energy metabolism (Murin et al. 2009). Besides that, the metabolic pathway of isoleucine is relatively short, allowing it to be rapidly converted into propionyl-CoA, which is further converted into acetyl-CoA, thereby providing energy (Adeva-Andany et al. 2017). Therefore, isoleucine is consumed to maintain the brain’ s energy metabolism. These viewpoints correspond with the results of our study in control group, which exhibited positive correlation with MMSE. A similar trend was also observed in VD group, which may be due to vascular lesions in VD patients that leading to insufficient brain energy supply. However, in the AD group, isoleucine was negatively correlated with MMSE. This may be attributed to the influence of serotonin on AD patients. The neurotransmitter serotonin is a candidate for reducing amyloid plaque load and for enhancing neurogenesis (Cho et al. 2007). It has been observed that the activation of serotonin receptors decreases Aβ production (Pimenova et al. 2014) and the AD patients have decreased serotonin levels (Marcosa et al. 2008). The brain serotonin concentration is affected by its precursor tryptophan and there is a competence between tryptophan and isoleucine when crossing the blood-brain barrier (Virag et al. 2020). As a result, the increased isoleucine may affect the serotonin levels and increase the risk of AD.

As the most significant pathological changes in AD, amyloid and tau have been reported to be related to the amino acids investigated in our study. Elevated amyloid peptides and total tau in cerebrospinal fluid have been previously revealed in individuals with creatine transporter deficiency (Rahhal et al. 2023). Treatment of the amyloid prone AD-like APPPS1 mice with spermidine reduced neurotoxic soluble Aβ and decreased AD-associated neuroinflammation (Freitag et al. 2022a, b). Additionally, spermidine was reported to rescue bioenergetic and mitophagy deficits induced by tau protein (Fairley et al. 2023). Cystine exchange was found to be crucial after the activation of microglia by secreted amyloid precursor protein (Barger et al. 2001). Tyrosine was also found to modulate amyloid formation (Hsieh et al. 2022). Phosphorylation of tyrosine 310 regulates the structure, aggregation, and microtubule- and lipid-binding properties of tau (Ait-Bouziad et al. 2020). These studies align with our study, indicating the potential of creatine, spermidine, cystine, and tyrosine for the differential diagnosis of AD and VD. However, it’s important to note that multiple factors impact the metabolic levels of amino acids, such as food, drugs, hormone levels, and other diseases. These factors are variable and not negligible, interfering with the diagnosis processes. Thus, a larger amount of research data and the exclusion of various confounding factors were also crucial.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, our study highlights the potential of metabolomics for novel biomarkers in AD and VD; obvious differences were discovered in serum amino acids between these two diseases. These findings and the potential pathogenesis need to be replicated in other populations. Our results shed light on the underlying mechanisms of dementia and potential clinical applications.
