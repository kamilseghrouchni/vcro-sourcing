# Diagnostic potential of cryptic exon-derived peptides in serum extracellular vesicles for sporadic amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

## Abstract

### Supplementary Information

The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41232-026-00404-w.

## Background

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive loss of upper and lower motor neurons, with 90% of its cases classified as sporadic ALS (SALS) [1]. The median survival from diagnosis of ALS to death is approximately 20 to 48 months and has improved by only 10% over the past two decades [2, 3]. Thus, the development of biomarkers reflecting the molecular pathology of SALS is warranted to decrease misdiagnosis, shorten diagnostic delay, and enable early interventions [4, 5].

Recently, the inclusion of cryptic exons (CE) due to the mislocalization of RNA binding protein TAR DNA-binding protein of 43 kDa (TDP-43, encoded by TARDBP) [6, 7] has drawn attention both in mechanistic research of ALS neuropathology [8, 9] and in biomarker development and assessment of therapeutic effects of interventions [10, 11]. In short, TDP-43 cytosolic mislocalization and depletion from the nucleus is present in up to 97% of SALS cases [6] and leads to aberrant RNA splicing such as the CE inclusion [7]. This aberrant RNA splicing results in the loss of function of genes critical for motor neuron survival (e.g., STMN2 [8]) or neuronal activity (e.g., UNC13A [9]), thereby driving pathogenic changes associated with ALS. Notably, some CEs could be translated into cryptic peptides [12], which have been reported to be elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with SALS and blood of presymptomatic carriers of the C9orf72 hexanucleotide repeat expansion, a major genetic cause of ALS that drives TDP-43 pathology [11]. While these studies suggest that cryptic peptides could be candidate biomarkers for SALS, some important questions remain to be answered for potential clinical application: could multiple cryptic peptides be detectable in blood in SALS and, more importantly, allow identification of SALS from, at least, healthy controls (HC)?

In this study, we aimed to evaluate whether cryptic peptides reflecting TDP-43 pathology were detectable in the blood of patients with SALS and could differentiate them from HC. Particularly, we aimed to detect cryptic peptides in serum extracellular vesicles (sEVs), considering that sEVs preserve the molecular signatures of their parent cells, including motor neurons, and have been studied as candidate biomarkers for SALS [13–15]. We hypothesized that multiple cryptic peptides could be detected in the sEVs of patients with SALS, certain cryptic peptides would be more frequently detected in SALS than in HC, and integrating information from multiple cryptic peptides could effectively distinguish SALS from HC.

### Primary analysis

We detected cryptic peptides from four proteins (RANBP1, IGLON5, ACTN1, ALPK2) in sEVs from more than one participant (see Supplementary Table S1 for the peptide sequences). The IGLON5 cryptic peptide was detected more frequently in patients with SALS (50%) than in HC (0%) (adjusted P = 0.044), while no significant group differences were observed for the other three cryptic peptides (Table 2). Consistently, the number of detected cryptic peptides was significantly higher in patients with SALS (2.35 ± 0.82) than in HC (1.30 ± 0.67) (P = 0.002) (Fig. 1a), yielding an area under the curve of 0.818 (95% confidence interval (CI), 0.687–0.948) for receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis in the classification of HC and patients with SALS (Fig. 1b). The ratio of bulbar vs spinal onset of ALS was the same among patients with and without detectable IGLON5 CP (n = 4 (40%) in both groups). Similarly, patients with and without detectable IGLON5 CP did not have significant group differences in ALSFRS-R score (P = 0.599) or duration of illness (P = 0.670). ## Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively searched for cryptic peptides in sEVs based on proteomics in HC and patients with SALS. We found that the IGLON5 cryptic peptide was detected significantly more frequently in patients with SALS than HC, and the detection counts of cryptic peptides effectively distinguished between HC and patients with SALS. We did not observe clinical differences in patients with or without IGLON5 CP.

Our study presents several strengths. First, we comprehensively searched for cryptic peptides based on candidate sequences. The candidate peptide sequences were acquired based on the TDP-43 knockdown experiment and thus could be considered to accurately reflect TDP-43 pathology. This also enabled us to combine the data of multiple cryptic peptides. Second, this study utilized serum data. Since blood sampling is minimally invasive and widely accepted in the clinical screening procedure of SALS, our results could indicate potential clinical applicability and relevance.

Our results expanded the previous evidence on the cryptic peptides in SALS [11, 12, 16] and showed that cryptic peptides in sEVs could distinguish HC and patients with SALS, thus paving the way for biomarker development of cryptic peptides. Despite the dichotomization of cryptic peptide detection, the number of detected cryptic peptides yielded an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI, 0.69–0.95), with the lower bound above 0.50, indicating above-chance performance. While this suggests acceptable potential for clinical utility [17] and is numerically comparable to the diagnostic performance of serum neurofilament light chain (AUC ≈ 0.86) [18], the small sample size warrants treating this point estimate as preliminary. Future studies with larger cohorts—ideally leveraging highly sensitive proteomic assays (e.g., aptamer-based platforms [19]) and machine-learning approaches—are needed to refine and validate these findings. Of note, the CPs of ALPK2 and RANBP1 were also detected in HC. Considering the absence of TDP-43 pathologies in HC, this could be partly due to the fact that proteomic analysis does not have complete accuracy, and cryptic splicing events, as observed in motor neurons of patients with sporadic ALS, could occur in healthy tissues under oxidative stress [20].

Moreover, our results could provide another insight into the accumulating evidence for the implications of IGLON5 in SALS. Previous reports on the association between motor neuron symptoms and IGLON5 have been centered on anti-IGLON5 disease, a rare autoimmune disease caused by anti-IGLON5 antibodies. Some cases with anti-IGLON5 disease are known to mimic clinical manifestations of ALS [21–23]. Additionally, in ALS, IGLON5 cryptic peptides could be loaded onto major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class 1 and presented as neoantigens [16]. This evidence suggests that the IGLON5 cryptic peptide, which we detected in 50% in SALS (10 of 20 patients), could lead to autoimmune responses, possibly including antibodies to IGLON5 cryptic peptides, or altered IGLON5 functions. At the same time, we failed to detect group differences in the detection of the other three cryptic peptides (RANBP1, ACTN1, ALPK2). However, it is still unclear whether translations to cryptic peptides are selective to certain CEs. At the same time, considering the possible autoimmune response caused by IGLON5 CP, future studies are warranted to test the possible interaction between HLA alleles and IGLON5 CP in the pathophysiology of SALS.

## Limitations

We should note several limitations of this study. First, the study lacked datasets of patients with clinically relevant differential diagnoses of SALS (e.g., other motor neuron diseases and TDP-43 proteinopathies) and was limited to comparing HC with patients with SALS. Second, this study utilizes the baseline data of the ROPALS trial [24]. Thus, the sample size and characteristics were not optimized for the outcomes of this study, and the study population could not fully represent the clinical population of SALS [25]. In our ROPALS trial, patients with severe symptoms, long duration (over 60 months after onset), rapid progression, or superoxide dismutase-1 mutation were not included. Moreover, the resulting sample size was relatively small (10 HC and 20 patients with SALS). This limitation of sample size was most prominent in our exploratory analysis, which compared clinical characteristics between patients with and without detectable IGLON5 CP (n = 10 in each group). Partly due to this limitation, our finding on the clinical difference between patients with and without detectable IGLON5 CP should be considered preliminary. In addition, the HC group in our study was significantly younger than the SALS group, which could affect gene expression [26]. These participant characteristics could collectively limit the statistical design and power of this study and the generalizability of our results to the whole clinical population of SALS. Third, this study failed to quantify cryptic peptides in a substantial proportion of samples and thus used the proteomic data in binary representation of detection. This is likely to be related to the depth of MS-based proteomics and the substantial dynamic range of the proteome [27]. Considering that the inclusion of CEs could lead to a decline in gene expression due to nonsense-mediated decay [7, 8], MS-based proteomics could have failed to detect some cryptic peptides with potential diagnostic values. Notably, our candidate sequence library consisted only of possible CPs, which could have increased the possibility of false positives of CPs that had similar sequences to normal peptides. At the same time, CPs of ALPK2 and RANBP1 were detected in substantial proportions of HC. While this result does not mean that TDP-43 pathology exists in HC, the dichotomization of quantitative proteomic data could make the analysis more prone to possible false positives in cryptic peptide detection and ALS-unrelated cryptic splicing events. Fourth, we used the number of detected cryptic peptides as a candidate biomarker for molecular diagnosis of SALS. While this metric was readily interpretable and yielded acceptable performance, it may not provide the highest discriminative accuracy; the application of machine-learning models could improve diagnostic performance. Fifth, this study used sEV data, which is not neuron-specific. Therefore, our results may not reflect SALS-associated neuronal pathology, and our results could be affected by sEV characteristics unrelated to the SALS pathophysiology. Isolation of neuron-specific EVs (e.g., L1CAM + EVs (Gomes et al., 2022) [28] and ATP1A3 + EVs [29] could provide further insight into the CPs in SALS. Lastly, while our results are closely related to the possible autoimmune pathology of SALS, this study failed to test the relationships between CPs and autoimmune pathophysiology. This is mainly due to the limited sample size (n = 20 for the whole SALS samples and n = 10 with detectable IGLON5 CP) and lack of HLA allele data. Identification of HLA alleles from conventional short-read whole genome sequencing is still a technical challenge (Thuesen et al., 2022) [30], but future studies are warranted to test the possible interactions between IGLON5 CP and HLA alleles in the pathophysiology of SALS. Also, considering that a subgroup of SALS and anti-IgLON5 disease shares the bulbar onset of symptoms, future studies are warranted to explore humoral autoimmunity caused by CPs in SALS.

## Conclusions

Cryptic peptides in serum extracellular vesicles, which reflect TDP-43 pathology, could distinguish HC and patients with SALS, thus paving the way for future development of sEV-based liquid markers of TDP-43 pathology. The detection of IGLON5 cryptic peptides could be a pathophysiological feature of SALS.

## Methods

This study was a secondary analysis of the baseline data and sEV proteomic data [31] of the ROPALS trial [24, 32] conducted in Keio University Hospital, Tokyo, Japan. The graphical description of study procedures is shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Graphical description of this study. In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic value of cryptic peptides in serum EVs. The detection of cryptic peptides is thought to reflect the inclusion of cryptic exons caused by TDP-43 mislocalization. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Keio University Hospital (approval number D18-01) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent for this study before enrollment.

### Participant information

The procedures of this study (participant recruitment, sample acquisition, and proteomic analysis) were detailed in our previous report [31]. In short, we included patients with SALS (n = 20) who participated in the ROPALS trial and HC (n = 10) from the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry (NCNP) Biobank, a member of the National Center Biobank Network [33]. Notably, the sample size and the criteria for inclusion and exclusion for patients were based on the ROPALS trial protocols [32]. Blood samples were collected from all participants.

### Data processing

The serum samples of patients with SALS and HC were stored at − 80 °C freezers before sEV isolation. EVs released into serum were collected using EV Second L70 columns (GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Proteins in EVs were reduced with 10 mM TCEP at 100 °C for 10 min, alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 45 min, and digested on beads with Trypsin/Lys-C Mix (Promega) at 37 °C for 12 h. The resulting peptides were analyzed with Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) and UltiMate 3000 RSLC nano-flow HPLC system (Thermo Scientific). Peptides were enriched using a μ-Precolumn (0.3 mm i.d. × 5 mm, 5 μm, Thermo Scientific) and separated on an AURORA column (0.075 mm i.d. × 250 mm, 1.6 μm, Ion Opticks Pty Ltd., Australia) using a two-step gradient: 2–40% acetonitrile for 110 min, followed by 40–95% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid for 5 min. The analytical parameters of the Orbitrap Fusion Lumos were as follows: full scan resolution, 50,000; scan range (m/z), 350–1500; full scan maximum injection time, 50 ms; full scan AGC target, 4 × 105; dynamic exclusion duration, 30 s; data-dependent MS/MS acquisition cycle time, 2 s; activation type, HCD; MS/MS detector, ion trap; maximum MS/MS injection time, 35 ms; AGC target of MS/MS, 1 × 104. As a quality check of sEV purity, we confirmed the presence of typical EV markers (e.g., CD9 and Alix) in all samples in our previous work which used the same sEV samples [31]. As an independent validation of the sEV isolation procedure, we extracted sEV from a commercially available human serum sample using the identical protocol applied to the clinical specimens. The isolated vesicles were characterized by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight, 488-nm laser), which revealed a size distribution consistent with sEV (diluted 1:10 in 0.9% NaCl; analyzed using NTA v3.4.003) (see Supplementary file 1). In addition, transmission electron microscopy confirmed the presence of vesicles displaying the typical morphology of sEV (see Supplementary file 2). RNA-seq data of human motor neurons derived from the HUES3 Hb9::GFP embryonic stem cell line with TARDBP (TDP-43) knockdown or control were obtained from the GEO dataset (GSE121569) [8]. Raw paired-end FASTQ files were subjected to quality control and adapter trimming using fastp v0.23.2 [34] with the following key parameters: minimum base quality = 15, maximum number of unqualified bases = 5, minimum read length = 15, and 16 processing threads. Reads passing QC were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using STAR v2.7.11b [35] with two-pass mapping enabled (–twopassMode Basic), intron motif annotation (–outSAMstrandField intronMotif), and default alignment settings. Output BAM files were subsequently sorted and indexed using samtools v1.9 [36]. For splicing junction detection, junctions were extracted from the sorted BAM files using regtools v1.0.0 (junctions extract -a 6 -m 50 -M 500000 -s XS) [37]. The resulting junction files were analyzed with LeafCutter v0.2.9 [38] using recommended parameters. Differential splicing analysis was performed to compare TDP-43 knockdown and control samples with nominal significance of P < 0.05. The identified differential splicing events were classified into known or cryptic splicing events using GRCh38 exon list. Cryptic splicing events were defined as splicing events in which more than one of the splicing did not end in known exons. The cryptic exons were then identified using the list of cryptic splicing events. After in-silico translation, we acquired a comprehensive list of possible cryptic peptides. To evaluate potential effects on protein coding, in silico translation was performed to predict peptide sequences in three possible reading frames (+ 0, + 1, + 2). These predicted peptides were compiled into a database of candidate CP sequences for downstream analyses.

The MS/MS spectra were searched against the cryptic peptide sequence database using Proteome Discoverer 3.1 software (Thermo Scientific), in which peptide identification filters were set at “false discovery rate < 1%”. Label-free relative quantification analysis for proteins was performed with the default parameters of Minora Feature Detector node, Feature Mapper node, and Precursor Ions Quantifier node in Proteome Discoverer 3.1 software.

### Statistical analysis

We selected CP considering the following criteria: the peptide was in the database of cryptic peptides as described above, and the peptide was detected in more than one participant. All CPs which met these criteria were included in the statistical analysis. We converted cryptic peptide data for each gene into binary representation (i.e., detected/undetected). The frequency of detection of each cryptic peptide was compared between SALS and HC using Fisher’s exact tests. Multiple comparisons were performed using Bonferroni’s method. The number of detected cryptic peptides was also compared between SALS and HC using a Mann–Whitney U test. The performance of the number of detected cryptic peptides for the classification of HC and patients with SALS was evaluated with ROC curve, with AUC employed as the metric. The 95% CI of AUC was calculated using DeLong’s method [39]. Lastly, we explored the differences in the onset type of SALS (i.e., bulbar or spinal onset), duration of illness, and severity assessed with the revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS-R) among patients with or without detectable CPs which were detected significantly more frequently in patients with SALS than HC. The methods for calculating ALSFRS-R score were detailed in our previous publication (Morimoto et al., 2023) [24]. The significance level was set to P = 0.05 for all analyses.
