# Retinal Imaging and Tissue Analysis for Frontotemporal Degeneration: Recent Advances and Challenges for Biomarker Development

## Abstract

Frontotemporal degeneration (FTD) is a group of neurodegenerative disorders affecting behavior, language, and executive functions. FTD is a common cause of early-onset dementia, but there are no FDA-approved treatments or established biomarkers for diagnosing and tracking these conditions, making early and accurate diagnosis challenging during life. Recent advances in retinal imaging, particularly through technologies like optical coherence tomography, have emerged as promising tools for identifying potential biomarkers for FTD and related neurodegenerative diseases. The retina, being an accessible extension of the central nervous system, has shown abnormalities that might serve as indicators of forms of FTD. Retinal imaging has revealed changes such as thinning of specific retinal layers that could correlate with molecular forms of FTD, Alzheimer’s disease, and other neurodegenerative diseases. These advances highlight the potential of retinal imaging to not only aid in diagnosis but also differentiate between various neurodegenerative conditions. Emerging data on retinal tissue analysis with immunohistochemistry and other techniques further support the potential of retinal biomarkers, though further studies are required to validate and refine these findings. Future advancements in retinal imaging technologies, along with longitudinal and autopsy-validated studies, are crucial for enhancing diagnostic capabilities and understanding FTD-related pathologies within the retina.

## Introduction

Frontotemporal degeneration (FTD), is a heterogenous spectrum of neurodegenerative disorders and common cause of early-onset dementia. FTD leads to marked changes in behavioral, language, executive, and/or motor functions; it corresponds to neurodegeneration in frontal and temporal cortices. The neuropathology of FTD is referred to as FTLD (frontotemporal lobar degeneration) and develops from two main sources of pathology characterized by either tau (FTLD-Tau) or TDP-43 proteinopathy (FTLD-TDP).1 There are no FDA approved therapies for FTD or established biomarkers to identify and track FTLD proteinopathies, making antemortem diagnosis challenging. As mechanism-based therapies targeting tau or TDP-43 specific pathophysiology emerge, these are largely limited to the subset of ~15–20% of patients with familial disease and known pathology.2 Thus, disease-specific biomarkers are critical for FTD therapeutic development.3

Since the retina is an accessible extension of central nervous system tissue, retinal imaging has been increasingly studied as a potential clinical tool for neurologists. In recent years, detailed retinal imaging has identified abnormalities that have potential as biomarkers for molecular forms of FTLD, as well as Alzheimer’s disease (AD).4, 5 These abnormalities include the thickness of retinal layers from imaging data or presence of pathologic disease-specific features seen in emerging retinal tissue data from FTD patients.6–8 Beyond aiding diagnosis, retinal imaging may help monitor neurological changes over time and differentiate diseases, with future technological advances expected to enhance its capabilities.9, 10

Here, we present the current advances in retina biomarker development for FTD and related neurodegenerative diseases, including both retinal imaging data and retinal tissue analyses that support this line of research. While covering ongoing advancements and their potential impact on clinical care, we also discuss the challenges in retina biomarker development for FTD.

## Overview of Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration

The main neuropathologic subtypes of FTLD (Figure 1) are FTLD-Tau and FTLD-TDP-43; FTLD-FET is a less common subtype. FTLD-Tau and FTLD-TDP each compose almost half of FTLD pathology, while FTLD-FET is only about 5–10% of FTLD.1, 11, 12 FTLD-Tau is characterized by abnormal aggregations of tau, a microtubule binding protein with 6 isoforms in the human brain characterized by variable N terminal domain sequences and inclusion or exclusion of a microtubule binding domain coded by exon 10.2 FTLD-Tau has several pathological subtypes which can be classified by the main isoform type of tau contained within neuronal and glial cytoplasmic inclusions. Based on the presence of 3 or 4 repeats of the microtubule binding domain, these subtypes include 3R tauopathy (Pick’s disease) and 4R tauopathies (Progressive supranuclear palsy, PSP; corticobasal degeneration CBD; globular glial tauopathy GGT).11, 13, 14 Familial forms of FTLD-Tau are caused by pathogenic mutations in the tau gene (MAPT) with various isoform types including 3R, 4R and 3R/4R containing inclusions.15 In contrast to the structural role of tau in the cytoskeleton, TDP-43 (Transactive response DNA-binding Protein of 43 kDa) is a nuclear protein involved in transcriptional regulation and RNA metabolism.2 FTLD-TDP is characterized by the presence of TDP-43 cytoplasmic inclusions in neurons and glia, as well as depletion of healthy TDP-43 from the nucleus of degenerating neurons as the inclusion formation sequesters TDP-43 to the cytoplasm.16 FTLD-TDP can be further subtyped based on the morphology and laminar distribution of inclusions histopathologically.17 The most common familial forms of FTLD-TDP include hexanucleotide expansion of the C9ORF72 gene (also the most common familial form of ALS, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis)18 and mutations in GRN resulting in haploinsufficiency of progranulin protein.19 For all forms of FTD, a pathogenic autosomal dominant mutation is found in up to 15–20% of FTD patients, particularly in those with a strong family history.20, 21

Certain clinical syndromes of FTD are statistically associated with a higher frequency of underlying tauopathy (Figure 1C). For instance, progressive supranuclear palsy syndrome (PSP), when meeting criteria for probable PSP, is highly specific for underlying FTLD-Tau.22 Additionally, the non-fluent/agrammatic variant of primary progressive aphasia (naPPA) is associated with underlying FTLD-Tau in roughly 70% of cases, according to most autopsy studies, although naPPA can also be associated with ADNC (Alzheimer’s disease neuropathologic change) and FTLD-TDP.2 The presence of upper and lower motor neuron signs consistent with co-occurring ALS is a strong predictor of underlying TDP-43 proteinopathy in FTD patients; however, the rate of motor neuron disease in FTD cohorts is roughly 15% and may occur later in the disease course.23 The semantic variant of primary progressive aphasia (svPPA) is one FTD syndrome with high predictive value for underlying TDP-43 proteinopathy type C, with roughly an 80% frequency of underlying FTLD-TDP found in most svPPA autopsy cohorts. Interestingly, FTLD-TDP type C has been found to have a second annexin A-11 proteinopathy co-occuring in the same inclusions, suggesting this subtype may be biologically distinct from other forms of FTLD-TDP.24 Finally, FTLD-FET is characterized by the presence of FUS, EWS and TAF15 proteins and is associated with a small percent of the behavioral variant of FTD (bvFTD), while familial forms of ALS-FUS have FUS inclusions alone without other FET proteins.1, 25 Despite moderate clinicopathological correlations for some phenotypes, predicting the pathology at the individual patient level remains challenging for the majority of FTD.

In summary, it can be difficult to clinically distinguish FTLD-Tau, FTLD-TDP, and ADNC from each other. Definitive diagnosis of underlying FTLD-Tau or FTLD-TDP is only possible through autopsy, except in patients with familial disease where a pathogenic variant reliably predicts the underlying neuropathology.1, 2 Indeed, there are currently no biofluid or imaging biomarkers clinically available that are specific for FTLD pathologies, compared to FDA approved biofluid and PET markers of ADNC. Thus, biomarkers sensitive to the underlying biology of FTLD-Tau and FTLD-TDP subtypes are critical to improve diagnosis and ultimately lead to disease modifying treatments.

## Retinal Imaging

Non-invasive retinal imaging is a diagnostic tool routinely used by ophthalmologists. Methods of non-invasive retinal imaging encompass several techniques; frequently used modalities include spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT), fundus color photography, and fundus autofluorescence (Figure 2).26 Fundus photography and autofluorescence can also be performed with confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscopy.

In recent years, increasing emphasis on OCT has been placed at the intersection between retinal imaging and neurodegenerative conditions. A PubMed search of articles using the words “optical coherence tomography and retina and dementia” generated 310 publications between 2014–2024 compared to only 18 publications between 2003–2013. The various OCT technologies allow clinicians to visualize multiple structures. For instance, OCT can visualize retinal structure (i.e., retinal layers [Figure 2 and 3]) and the optic nerve with about 5 micron axial resolution. For subjects without confounding retinal diseases, automated and semi-automated segmentation software can readily determine the thickness of retinal layers.27, 28 Effective retinal layer segmentation requires good image quality, which can decrease if a subject has had significant cognitive decline.29 In addition to OCT of retinal layers, OCT-Angiography can non-invasively allow visualization of flow within capillaries, and enhanced-depth imaging OCT can provide detailed images of the choroid, the vascular layer between the retina and sclera.30, 31 The quick, inexpensive nature of OCT combined with its high resolution, especially in comparison to brain imaging, makes OCT an appealing diagnostic tool.

## Retinal Imaging Biomarkers for FTLD-Tau

Recent studies have suggested abnormal thinning of retinal layers in FTLD-Tau patients.32 Through a cross-sectional study of 27 deeply characterized FTD patients compared to 44 normal controls, we found that patients with FTD had a significantly thinner outer retina (photoreceptor layers) (adjusted mean (standard error): 132 (2.1) μm for FTD vs. 142 (1.6) μm for controls, p=0.004).4 The outer nuclear layer (ONL), which is composed of photoreceptor nuclei and is the largest OCT outer retina layer, was also significantly thinner in FTD patients (adjusted mean (standard error): 88.5 (1.8) μm for FTD vs. 97.9 (1.4) μm for controls, p=0.003). While this was accompanied by nonsignificant thickening of the outer plexiform layer (OPL), an analysis of the ONL and OPL together also showed that this composite layer was significantly thinner in FTD patients compared to controls. When examining the subset of patients with probable/definite FTLD-Tau (pFTLD-Tau) based on clinical syndromes (e.g. PSP) associated with FTLD-Tau and/or autopsy/genetic variant in MAPT, which composed 19 of the 27 FTD patients, this subgroup also demonstrated outer retina thinning compared to controls, suggesting the effect was likely driven by those patients with FTLD-Tau pathology.4 Importantly, the FTD patients were analyzed for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers to exclude those with underlying ADNC, which can present with FTD clinical syndromes33 and potentially confound results. In a follow-up study of a subset of these patients followed longitudinally (mean follow-up of 15 months), the pFTLD-Tau patients showed progressive outer retina thinning, whereas normal controls exhibited minimal change over time. The ONL also had a significant rate of thinning compared to controls (−2.9 μm/year for pFTLD-Tau vs. 0.1 μm/year for controls).34 Importantly, ONL thinning visualized by OCT has been confirmed in FTLD-Tau patients followed to autopsy (also see retinal tissue section below).6 Furthermore, when a group of 21 pFTLD-Tau patients were compared to 24 patients with a CSF biomarker indicating probable ADNC (pADNC), only the pFTLD-Tau patients had ONL thinning,35 demonstrating some degree of disease-specificity for this OCT measure. This study also found that ONL thickness correlated with clinical measures, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Clinical Dementia Rating plus National AD Coordinating Center Frontotemporal Dementia Sum of Boxes (CDR-FTD)36 score among pFTLD-Tau patients, but not among pADNC patients. In this study, many of the pADNC patients were relatively young and had non-amnestic FTD clinical syndromes, and together these findings suggest OCT could add additional diagnostic value to young-onset dementias. Although pathologic accumulations of tau protein are associated with both FTLD-Tau and ADNC, FTLD-Tau is characterized primarily by 3R or 4R tau with straight filaments, while ADNC tangles have a mixture of 3R and 4R tau with paired helical filaments.2, 37 These different compositions of tauopathy can potentially contribute to different retina abnormalities.

Although evidence suggests that thinning of the outer retina and ONL is a marker of FTLD-Tau, larger studies are needed to definitively understand which retinal layers are affected in FTLD-Tau patients. Of note, the above-mentioned studies have not identified thinning of the inner retina (retinal nerve fiber layer [RNFL] and ganglion cell layer [GCL]) of FTLD-Tau patients, contrasting with inner retina thinning seen in neurodegenerative diseases such as clinical Alzheimer’s disease with presumed underlying ADNC.38 Interestingly, we also did not observe inner retinal thinning in our disease reference group of symptomatic pADNC patients.35 One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that, as aforementioned, many of these patients were younger at onset and had atypical syndromes. Indeed, ADNC biomarkers in young-onset atypical patients, such as CSF, may be different than older typical amnestic AD patients.39 Although animal models may not fully represent the complexity of human neurodegeneration, preclinical data of mice (rTg(tauP301L)4510) with FTLD-Tau pathology found that accumulation of phosphorylated tau (p-tau) was present in the retinal GCL, inner plexiform layer (IPL), and inner nuclear layer (INL) of the mice retina along with a reduced nuclear density of the central GCL;40 however, it is difficult to disentangle effects of promoters used to induce gene expression in transgenic murine models. In line with this model data, an OCT study of 17 FTD and 39 AD patients found that the RNFL and GCL layers in the retina of FTD and AD patients were both significantly reduced compared to 49 controls,41 and this finding of inner retina thinning has been supported by other studies as well.42, 43 This suggests that FTD may also exhibit thinning in retinal layers typically characteristic of ADNC, although it is unclear whether this effect seen in FTD patients was driven by a specific molecular subtype or the presence of ADNC. Adding to the complexity of reported findings, a study evaluated retinal layers associated with the risk of dementia development in 30,573 UK Biobank participants with OCT but no prior diagnosis of dementia.44 During the follow-up period, 8 patients developed FTD, as determined by diagnosis codes. A thinner superior subfield of the retinal pigment epithelium was associated with an increased risk of FTD development, but the FTD sample size was small and this association was not significant after excluding patients with less than 5 years of follow-up. In addition to these studies, retinal thinning has been evaluated in subtypes of pFTLD-Tau compared to normal controls; some studies of living PSP patients with likely underlying tauopathy have suggested thinning of the inner retina,45 including the GCL as well as the plexiform layers.46

A multicenter observational study of corticobasal syndrome (CBS) and PSP recently reported cross-sectional data.47 While no differences in retinal layers were observed between 42 CBS patients and 83 healthy controls, the 47 PSP patients exhibited loss of the GCL, IPL, and ONL compared to the controls. Of these layers, the strongest finding was for the loss of ONL volume (0.63 mm3 for PSP vs. 0.66 mm3 for controls, p<0.01). While some inner retina loss was additionally seen, this multicenter data independently provided data supporting that the ONL is the retinal layer predominantly affected in FTLD-Tau. It is unclear why OCT abnormalities were not seen among the CBS patients, but one possibility could be the underlying pathologic heterogeneity of this syndrome; CBS can be due to either ADNC or FTLD-Tau pathologies.48

Currently, structural OCT is the main retinal imaging modality used in FTD studies. Although available for clinical use in ophthalmology for the past decade, OCT-Angiography research does not yet have standardized methods49 and is in its infancy for FTD. An OCT-Angiography study performed on patients with FTD looked at 18 eyes of patients with FTD. There was unknown underlying FTLD neuropathology, although CSF measurements did not indicate ADNC. The researchers found significantly lower flow to the optic nerve head and parts of the macula compared to healthy controls.50 Another study evaluated 29 eyes of 19 FTD patients compared to controls; the FTD patients had a decreased macular perfusion density, vessel density, and capillary flux index.51 Supporting these findings, decreased vascular density was also observed with OCT-Angiography in a mouse model of tauopathy (P301S transgenic mice).52 Multimodal OCT could provide greater insights into FTD-related retinal changes, potentially allowing the use of more than one OCT measure as a biomarker.

## Retinal Imaging Biomarkers for FTLD-TDP/ALS

Data has been emerging to increase our understanding of how retinal changes are associated with FTLD-TDP. Using OCT, an early 2014 study including humans with progranulin (GRN) mutations before clinical symptoms of dementia found significant reduction in RNFL thickness compared to controls. There was also loss of ganglion cell complex volume, but not ONL volume, compared to controls.53 These findings were consistent with the researchers’ findings in mice with a knocked-out progranulin gene mutation that resulted in RNFL and GCL loss. However, a recent study (pre-print) of heterozygous GRN mutation carriers compared to healthy controls did not find thinning of the RNFL or the GCL-IPL.54 Instead, this study discovered ONL and retinal pigment epithlium thinning, as well as outer plexiform layer (OPL) expansion; the outer retinal abnormalities correlated with cognition and white matter brain deficits for the GRN mutation carriers. Moreover, lipofuscin deposits have been found in the retina, even in presymptomatic individuals. Indeed, homozygous GRN variants with complete progranulin loss can cause neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis, while humans with progranulin haploinsufficiency, including individuals pre-symptomatic for GRN-FTD, also can accumulate lipofuscin in the brain.55 As such, one current clinical trial for GRN-FTD (NCT04747431) uses OCT as a secondary endopoint for target engagement, demonstrating the utility of OCT in trials for FTD.

Given the relationship of ALS to FTLD-TDP, studies focusing on ALS are also informative for TDP-43 mediated retinal abnormalities. Thus far, the RNFL and GCL have been more implicated in patients with ALS. One study compared 21 ALS patients to 21 healthy controls and found that macular RNFL was significantly thinner in the ALS patients.56 Other studies have also found RNFL thinning in ALS patients compared to healthy controls.57–59 However, there has been some variability among study results, and RNFL thinning has not been a consistent finding for ALS.60

Studies which directly compare retinal layers between patients with probable FTLD-TDP and FTLD-Tau are scarce. A recent prospective, multi-center study compared the mean peripapillary RNFL between FTD patients with presumed tauopathy (FTLD-Tau) and TDP-43 (FTLD-TDP); clinical diagnoses were used to classify patients by presumed pathology (e.g. PSP=tauopathy, ALS or svPPA=TDP-43). The researchers found a significantly thinner peripapillary RNFL in the temporal sector of the TDP-43 group compared to the tauopathy group (adjusted mean 70.31 μm for TDP-43 vs. 85.77 μm for tauopathy, p=0.046).61 While rigorous in its analysis, this study was limited due to a relatively small sample size (14 subjects (28 eyes) in the TDP-43 group and 5 subjects (9 eyes) in the tauopathy group); the investigators acknowledged that the temporal sector RNFL thinning of TDP-43 patients was not statistically significant after correcting for multiple comparisons. This study was valuable as a launching pad for future studies investigating retina layer differences of FTLD-TDP vs. FTLD-Tau.

Of note, OCT findings of FTLD-TDP could also inform other disorders. Indeed, TDP-43 pathology is not specific soley to FTLD-TDP and ALS. TDP-43 aggregations within the brain can also be seen in other neurodegenerative conditions associated with aging such as limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE)62 and chronic traumatic encephalopathy. Likewise, concomitant TDP-43/LATE is common, especially in AD, and may affect retinal thinning findings when it co-occurs with other pathologies. A recent study has also found increased phosphorylated-TDP-43 (p-TDP) punctate aggregates within the retina INL of patients with chronic traumatic encephalopathy compared to controls.63 Future studies could investigate retinal differences between different diseases with TDP-43 accumulation.

## Retinal Tissue Analysis of FTLD-Tau and FTLD-TDP/ALS

Neuropathological examination of the brain is essential for diagnosing molecular pathology in FTD. However, examining retinal tissue from patients imaged with OCT during life provides additional means of critical validation of putative retinal imaging biomarkers. Indeed, morphological and immunohistochemical changes in the retina have been increasingly studied and support the potential use of the retina as a biomarker for FTD subtypes. A first report of 2 FTLD-Tau patients, imaged with OCT during life and followed to autopsy with retinal tissue analysis, demonstrated areas of ONL thinning at the macula, confirming the OCT findings (Figure 4).6 In addition to these FTLD-Tau retinal morphology findings, emerging immunohistochemistry data on tauopathies involving the retina may provide insights into pathophysiological processes contributing to retinal abnormalities observed during life.

Earlier reports have shown variable tau staining of the human retina.64 With an antibody for p-tau Ser202/Thr205 (AT8), one study showed p-tau at the photoreceptors of normal primates.65 Another study has shown p-tau staining (Ser202/Thr205), at the GCL, INL, and plexiform layers of 5 AD and 2 PSP patients, while no p-tau staining was seen for controls.66 Similarly, a study of 6 AD retinas and 6 healthy control retinas found p-tau Ser202/Thr205 staining at the OPL and IPL, and cases with a moderate to severe amyloid stage had more p-tau retinal staining.67 Recently, Walkiewicz, et al. evaluated 164 human retinas, most of whom did not have AD or a related dementia.68 They described a “primary retinal tauopathy” as they found p-tau (Ser202/Thr205) within the retina of cases with no associated history of dementia. Interestingly, p-tau seemed to follow a hierarchical progression, first involving the OPL, then the INL; some cases had p-tau staining within the OPL, INL, and IPL. The stage of retinal tauopathy correlated with age, decreased vision, and was associated with ocular diseases such as ocular inflammation. While the primary retinal tauopathy findings also correlated with cerebral β-amyloid phase, they did not correlate with BRAAK neurofibrillary tangle staging among the 21 cases for which this data was available. Further, Gallyas silver staining did not detect neurofibrillary tangles in any of the cases, and the molecular composition of the tauopathy differed from what is typically seen within the brain for dementia. Another recent and valuable study was reported by Hart de Ruyter et al. and involved retinal tissue and brains from patients with FTLD, AD, and controls.7 They also found p-tau within the OPL, INL, and IPL; increased levels of retinal p-tau were seen when there was brain tau pathology. Controls without brain tau pathology had no p-tau Ser202/Thr205 or Thr217 retina staining. In their study, p-tau Ser202/Thr205 and Thr217 did correlate with Braak stage of neurofibrillary tangles as well as the presence of p-tau Ser202/Thr205 in hippocampus and cortical brain regions, although retinal p-tau did not resemble neurofibrillary tangles. When interpreting these data, it also is worth considering that normal tau within the brain can be phosphorylated from mechanisms unrelated to dementia and then detected by AT8 and other p-tau specific antibodies.69 Together, these recent studies found p-tau in similar retinal layers and advanced the field, maintaining the biomarker potential of retinal imaging for tauopathies. Studies focused on p-tau pathophysiology within the retina are needed to further understand how retinal p-tau may differ from brain p-tau and how the presence of p-tau relates to retinal abnormalities imaged during life.

The last few years have also shown the development of data regarding TDP within the retina. Pediconi et al. found increased cytoplasmic TDP staining within the retina of 10 ALS patients compared to 11 controls.70 More recently, Dijkstra et al. evaluated retinal p-TDP (pSer409/410) staining for 7 cases of FTLD-TDP; these included 4 cases of C9orf72 expansion, one PRGN mutation, and 2 sporadic cases.8 They also evaluated 5 cases of FTLD-Tau, 1 FTLD-FUS, 3 AD, 2 ALS cases related to TDP, and 6 healthy controls. The researchers found p-TDP aggregates within the OPL of the FTLD-TDP patients but not the cases of FTLD-Tau, AD, ALS, or controls. While these papers provide mixed data regarding the presence of p-TDP in ALS retinas, the report by Dijkstra et al. suggests the potential for retinal p-TDP imaging to be a biomarker for FTLD-TDP.

There is a strong need for more retinal tissue studies on FTLD-Tau and FTLD-TDP complete with morphology data, immunohistochemistry studies, and biochemical analysis of tissue for protein aggregates. Such studies are challenging because of the difficulty of acquiring tissue, and interpreting studies can be difficult due to variations in tissue fixation and processing within and between studies. Nevertheless, the field has gained significant traction from retinal tissue data supporting the goal of a noninvasive retinal imaging biomarker for FTLD-Tau or FTLD-TDP.

## Retinal Biomarkers for Alzheimer’s Disease

Retinal biomarkers have been investigated for use in other neurodegenerative diseases, with clinical AD being the highest focus. Other review articles extensively cover the topic of AD retinal imaging abnormalities;5, 71, 72 here, we highlight several AD topics related to FTD. First, while inner retina loss has been the predominant finding for clinical AD with presumed ADNC,38 there has been mixed and less extensive data regarding the outer retina. One study specifically evaluated the outer retina of AD patients and did not see a difference compared to controls,73 while some other data has suggested ONL loss in advanced cases of AD, although less than the magnitude of inner retina loss.74 Second, it is noteworthy that some studies have not used validated biomarkers (CSF, PET, or plasma) to confirm the presence of underlying ADNC pathology. This is a likely reason for some of the variable results among AD studies and can contribute to the overall modest diagnostic accuracy reported in studies, although this may improve with increasing use of FDA approved ADNC markers. Interestingly, a meta-anlaysis of only those studies with ADNC biomarker-determined groups did not find a strong signal for RNFL or GCL thinning, although differences in study design may have contributed to these results.75 Third, an important concept is that AD is often pathologically heterogeneous with mixed neuropathologies (further discussed in below section), and this may also contribute to the literature’s variable results. Finally, retinal imaging has been understudied in atypical variants such as early-onset AD and nonamnestic AD, which commonly have overlapping clinical characteristics with FTD.76 These atypical AD variants may not consistently have inner retina thinning at the time of presentation;35 larger retina imaging investigations with biomarker-determined groups are warranted to compare atypical AD with late-onset AD and other dementias such as FTD.

## Challenges for FTD Retinal Biomarker Studies

Despite the progress discussed, some challenges remain for this research field. A key underlying issue for retinal imaging studies of FTD and other dementias is the lack of mechanistic understanding for retinal abnormalities. It remains unclear how observed retinal changes are mechanistically linked to dementia pathophysiology within the brain. A recent study of autosomal dominant AD patients found an association between inner retinal layer thinning (primarily the GCL-IPL) and optic tract integrity, as measured by retinotopically defined optic tract diffusion tensor imaging measures. This association was not seen for the ONL thickness.77 Importantly, the retinotopic association suggests that inner retina thinning is systematically related to degeneration within the brain, possibly from a retrograde degeneration towards ganglion cell nuclei.

One prominent hypothesis in this field is that the presence of dementia-relevant proteinopathies (p-tau, p-TDP-43, Aβ42) within the retina is neurotoxic and potentially fatal to retinal cells.78 Depending on where the site of toxicity first happens for a neuron (retina vs. brain), the degeneration could progress in an anterograde or retrograde direction, or degeneration could even occur simultaneously.79 Another potential mechanism is a prion-like transneuronal propagation of proteinopathies. One study found that prion protein injected intracerebrally into mice was later found primarily in retinal photoreceptors, consistent with findings of chiefly ONL loss in FTLD-Tau.80 On the other hand, studies have linked RNFL and/or GCL loss to cognitive ability regardless of a specific dementia diagnosis.81, 82 Using population-based data from 4 prospective cohorts, a study has found that macular RNFL thickness was associated with a higher incidence of all-cause dementia and AD.43 It is possible that more than one mechanism could be at play, depending on the retinal layer and neurodegenerative condition. More work is needed to understand potential pathways of degeneration between the brain and retina of FTLD.

Other possible mechanisms for thinning of retinal layers include neuroinflammation and oxidative stress. Aligning with ONL loss seen in heterozygous GRN mutation carriers, novel data (pre-print) from Grn−/− mice has shown subretinal microgliosis that precedes photoreceptor loss and may be related to mitochondrial dysfunction.54 Furthermore, a recent study has shown microgliosis at areas of amyloid-β deposition within human retina,78 and a retinal microglial reaction has also been seen with the P301S mouse model of tauopathy, which found pathological tau proteins in several retinal layers including ganglion cells and inner/outer segments of photoreceptors.83 These latter studies suggest that microglia are associated with synapse alterations. Studies of FTD patient retinas evaluating microglia and apoptosis in relation to p-tau or p-TDP deposition are warranted to provide mechanistic insights. Additionally, oxidative stress could play a role in the outer retinal loss observed in FTLD-Tau patients. Photoreceptors experience significant oxidative stress due to their high metabolic activity and light exposure. This oxidative stress may lead to pathologic tau in patients predisposed to a tauopathy and explain the predominant loss of the ONL compared to the inner retina.84

With regards to study design, careful exclusion of confounding eye diseases is essential. As examples, this includes sometimes subtle epiretinal membranes that affect inner layer measurements,85 as well as diabetic retinopathy, which may still affect the inner retina when there is minimal or no visible diabetic retinopathy.86 Exclusion of eye disease also is especially notable for glaucoma. Glaucoma is an optic neuropathy with a global prevalence of 3.5%, and a systematic review and meta-analysis estimated that more than half of glaucoma cases are undiagnosed with 67 million people estimated to have undetected glaucoma by the year 2040.87 A national cohort study found that, compared to controls, patients diagnosed with glaucoma had a higher risk of being diagnosed with AD, vascular dementia, and all-cause dementia.88 The exact cause of this is unknown, but pathological tau protein and vascular dysregulation may play a role in the pathophysiology. Since glaucoma is associated with RNFL and GCL thinning, and early glaucoma may be subtle clinically, careful exclusion of glaucoma cases is important. It is also possible that some cases of normal-tension glaucoma may have pathophysiology related to dementia, adding complexity to this topic.89

Another possible explanation for some of the inconsistent findings of different OCT studies is variation in reported outcomes, which deserves discussion. First, there has been gradual movement towards an increased consistency of quality assessment and reported outcomes. The OSCAR-IB and Apostel 2.0 recommendations are important steps in this direction.49, 90 Second, these guidelines both note how an off-center OCT beam can affect measurements of the RNFL, OPL, and ONL. The backscattering related to Henle’s fiber layer can affect OPL and ONL measurements.91, 92 In addition to reporting OPL and ONL thickness measurements, it may be helpful to report measurements for the OPL and ONL combined. Third, data suggests that the peripapillary RNFL may be a more reliable measure than the macular RNFL as the peripapillary RNFL is thicker and less prone to measurement error.93, 94 As done in some studies, it may be important for future studies of macular layers to also report peripapillary RNFL outcomes. Fourth, there are inconsistencies in how retina regions are analyzed in OCT studies, including macula regions, peripapillary regions, and thickness vs. volume outcomes. For the macula, many studies use the fovea-centered ETDRS grid, but some groups report averages for combined areas or rings of the grid, while others report data comparing cases and controls for each of the 9 regions.4, 44 These inconsistencies add to the difficulty of interpreting data across different papers.

Finally, the concept of mixed pathologies may be unfamiliar to investigators outside of neurology and is worth highlighting. While some patients can have discordant clinical vs. neuropathologic diagnoses, other patients have mixed neuropathologies. Autopsy-based studies have demonstrated that 30–50% of clinically diagnosed AD has mixed pathology.95 In some autopsy case series, about 10% of clinically diagnosed AD patients can have mixed pathologies with FTLD.96 For example, a patient can have both significant ADNC and PSP. In such cases, it is unclear how significant levels of mixed brain pathologies might affect the retina, and this may relate to some of the variable data of imaging studies of living patients. However, a recent report evaluating the presence of dementia-related proteins within retinal tissue accounted for the presence of brain co-pathologies. The investigators found that the abnormal retinal proteins identified (e.g. p-tau for tauopathies and p-TDP-43 for FTLD-TDP) were those of the primary brain pathology, suggesting that dementia-related pathologies found in the retina are associated with the primary brain pathology.97 These cases had low to moderate co-pathology levels, and this study provided a first evaluation of whether mixed brain pathologies manifest in the retina. Related to mixed pathologies, the presence of significant cerebral small vessel disease as a co-pathology could also complicate study results. Reports suggest that cerebral small vessel disease is associated with changes in retinal layer volume due to alterations in retinal vasculature.98 While studies may exclude pure vascular dementia cases, this issue is complicated as dementia can have vascular disease coincident or as part of the dementia pathophysiology, such as cerebral amyloid angiopathy intertwined with the dementia.99 Ideally, these issues may be resolved with additional needed retinal studies that account for patients with predominantly one neuropathology vs. those with significant levels of mixed pathologies.

## Future Directions and Clinical Implications

FTD retinal imaging studies are expected to gain further traction. To ameliorate difficulties noted above, longitudinal studies of deeply phenotyped patients with biological interrogation for ADNC biomarkers and clinical/genetic signs of FTLD pathology are critical and may provide key mechanistic insights. Following patients to autopsy for both neuropathological and retinal histological analysis, when possible, is of utmost importance to validate results for clinical use.

Newer technologies are rapidly emerging and are expected to make a great impact. This could include next-generation OCT, integration of adaptive optics for even higher resolution to image individual cells (Figure 5),100 and possibly tracers to label specific proteins within the retina (NCT06514001). Recent vision science developments such as optoretinography have enabled functional testing at the level of individual photoreceptor cells.101 Current generation OCT demonstrates retinal abnormalities in FTD patients, but next generation imaging systems will likely be necessary for a robust biomarker. Although requiring larger datasets, there is opportunity for machine learning. Machine learning models based on retinal images for AD have already been reported.9 Data sharing among institutions will enable larger datasets. Rigorously controlled studies accounting for potential confounders of retinal thickness, as well as a continued movement towards standardization of methods and reporting, such as the OSCAR-IB and APOSTEL recommendations, will facilitate sharing and comparisons of data.49, 90 Finally, continual advances in transcriptomic and proteomic technologies are expected and will provide mechanistic clues when applied to retinal tissue.

These potential advancements will help push FTD research into clinical care, complementing recent advances in FTD candidate therapies. With a needed emphasis on autopsy validated biomarkers and relating imaging findings to tissue, steady improvements to research designs and technologies will further our understanding of FTD, its associated effect on the retina, and how the retina may become a marker for FTD pathologies to accelerate drug development.

This narrative synthesis of evidence was based on PubMed literature searches, which were tailored according to the authors’ expertise in this field and emphasized recent human studies.
