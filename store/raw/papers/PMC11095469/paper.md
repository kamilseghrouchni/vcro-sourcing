# The shift in the fatty acid composition of the circulating lipidome in Alzheimer's disease

## Abstract

### INTRODUCTION

Fatty acids (FAs) are the building blocks of complex lipids and signaling compounds; the role of the lipidome fatty acid profile (LFA) in AD progression remains unclear.

### METHODS

The LFA of plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples from 289 participants (103 AD patients, 92 MCI patients, and 94 controls) was determined by GC‐FID. The MCI subjects were followed up for 58 ± 12.5 months.

### RESULTS

In controls, CSF has a more neuroprotective LFA than plasma. In CSF, a higher content of docosahexaenoic acid was associated with a reduced risk of MCI‐to‐AD progression. In plasma, higher oleic acid content was associated with lower risk of AD, MCI, and MCI‐to‐AD progression, whereas higher levels of vaccenic acid and docosahexaenoic acid were associated with greater risk of AD and MCI, and higher rate of MCI‐to‐AD progression, respectively.

### DISCUSSION

The circulating LFA is involved in the pathogenesis and progression of AD.

## BACKGROUND

1

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurological disorder that results in synapse loss and neuronal atrophy. The disease presents clinically as initial memory loss and progressive effects on other cognitive and behavioral abilities. 1 The etiology of AD is unknown, but genetic, 2 metabolic, 3 , 4 environmental, and lifestyle factors 5 are believed to take part in the onset and progression of this disease. In fact, beyond the known amyloid and tau pathologies, many molecular alterations, including mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and lipid dysregulation, are present in the preclinical AD brain. 6 , 7

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD is considered a predementia stage in which patients, despite being independent in performing routine daily tasks, have lower levels of cognitive performance than expected for their age and level of education. The decline in cognition is likely the consequence of the growing decrease in neuronal connectivity initially generated by the loss of synapses, followed by neuronal death. 8 In this regard, soluble oligomeric species of both amyloid beta (Aβ) 9 and tau 10 proteins, inflammation, 11 and oxidative stress 6 , 7 , 12 , 13 have been shown to be deleterious for synapses and can be drivers of cognitive decline and AD progression.

Alterations in lipid metabolism have been strongly linked to AD development in clinical 14 and genetic 15 studies. Fatty acids (FAs) are building blocks of more complex lipids and play roles as lipid mediators. Lipid mediators are potent signaling molecules that regulate many cellular responses, including cell growth, death, and inflammation, via receptor‐mediated pathways. 16 Therefore, investigating FA abnormalities in patients with AD may provide clues regarding the involvement of these molecules in AD development and progression and guide us to potential therapeutic avenues.

Considering the difficulties and limitations of the use of brain samples for studying AD‐related brain alterations, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is considered a sample of choice. CSF contains molecules of neural cell origin, which reflect, at least in part, the metabolic activity and needs of the brain. In the molecular profile of CSF, lipids are a preponderant component. It is estimated that the total lipid content of human CSF is approximately 0.2% of the plasma content. 17 Indeed, CSF shows its own lipid profile, distinct from that of plasma 18 and marked by the presence of approximately 200 to 300 different lipid species; among them, glycerophospholipids are the most abundant, followed by neutral lipids and sphingolipids. 17 , 18 Consequently, CSF provides a valuable tool for exploring lipid homeostasis in patients with AD and identifying novel biomarkers for their diagnosis and prognosis.

However, a very limited number of studies 19 , 20 have used CSF to determine AD‐related FA alterations. These alterations in AD are mainly investigated in plasma (reviewed by 21 ). A priori, the use of plasma has several advantages, including the accessibility of samples; the noninvasiveness of their acquisition; and utility for studying the effect of systemic dysregulations, which are very common in AD, on systemic FA composition. However, the existing evidence is not conclusive, and the literature lacks studies in which FA dysregulation has been evaluated in both CSF and plasma. In addition, the association of FAs with the progression and cognitive evolution of patients was not addressed in previous studies.

This study is designed to determine the circulating (plasma and CSF) lipidome fatty acid profiles (LFA) of healthy subjects and patients with MCI and AD. Furthermore, MCI patients were followed up for a long period to assess their progression to dementia. In this scenario, our objectives were as follows: (1) to define the LFA profiles and main traits of both plasma and CSF in healthy adult subjects; (2) to evaluate the CSF and plasma LFA alterations in patients with AD and MCI compared to control subjects; (3) to search for the association of CSF LFAs with CSF AD biomarkers; and (4) to investigate the relationship of CSF and plasma LFA levels with MCI‐to‐AD progression and the rate of cognitive decline in these patients.

## METHODS

2

### Study population

2.1

Patients with amnestic MCI and AD were recruited prospectively and consecutively from a sample of outpatients who visited the Cognitive Disorders Unit of the Hospital Universitari Santa Maria de Lleida (Lleida, Spain) from June 2014 to December 2016. Included patients were (1) males and females without specific treatment for dementia at the moment of the inclusion, with a new diagnosis of MCI or mild and moderate AD (Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE] ≥20) according to the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) criteria 8 , 22 ; (2) absence of visual or hearing problems that, in the investigator's judgement, would decrease the compliance with the neuropsychological examination; (3) an informed consent form signed by the patient and the responsible caregiver (and/or if applicable, the legal representative if different from the responsible caregiver); and (4) a knowledgeable and reliable caregiver who accompanied the patient to all clinic visits during the study.

For AD and MCI patients, the exclusion criteria were (1) diagnosis of dementia other than AD or any somatic, psychiatric, or neurological disorder that might cause cognitive impairment and (2) the presence of thyroid and/or vitamin B12 deficiency.

The cognitive performance of the study population was assessed using MMSE 23 at baseline. For MCI subjects, this test was applied at annual visits until the end of the follow‐up. Epidemiological data, including age, sex, and the time of symptom onset, were recorded using a structured interview conducted during the initial patient visit. Individuals with no neurological or neuropsychiatric diseases were recruited as controls. Seventy‐four plasma samples originated from the Universidad Pablo de Olavide, Sevilla. These controls were part of a cohort of healthy older subjects whose negative amyloid status had been confirmed by Aβ‐positron emission tomography (PET) scans and whose cognition was normal according to the MMSE score.

The rate of progression from MCI to AD dementia was defined as the time between the baseline visit and the date of AD diagnosis. Progressive cognitive decline into AD was defined as (1) losing more than three points between the first and last MMSEs, (2) having dementia at follow‐up, or (3) scoring under 24 on the last MMSE. 24

### Sample collection

2.2

Fasting blood and CSF samples were collected between 8:00 and 10:00 a.m. CSF samples were collected in polypropylene tubes and then centrifuged at 2000 × g at 4°C for 10 min to exclude cells or other insoluble material. Blood samples were collected in EDTA‐containing tubes and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 20 min to obtain the plasma and buffy coat. The buffy coat cells were used for DNA extraction and subsequent apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotyping. All samples were stored at −80°C until use. Samples were obtained with support from IRBLleida Biobank (B.0000682) and PLATAFORMA BIOBANCOS PT17/0015/0027.

### AD biomarker measurements

2.3

The levels of CSF Aβ42 (INNOTEST β‐AMYLOID (1‐42)), total tau (Ttau) (INNOTEST hTAU Ag), and phosphorylated tau (Ptau) (INNOTEST PHOSPHO‐TAU (181P)) were determined by enzyme immunoassay methods according to the manufacturer's instructions (Fujirebio Europe, Ghent, Belgium). Levels of Aβ42 < 600 pg/mL, Ttau > 425 pg/mL, and Ptau > 65 pg/mL were considered positive/abnormal. 25

### APOE genotyping

2.4

DNA was extracted from the buffy coat cells using the Maxwell RSC Buffy Coat DNA Kit (Promega Biotech Ibérica SL, Madrid, Spain) on the Maxwell RSC instrument according to the manufacturer's instructions. APOE genotyping was performed by real‐time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with 2.25 μL of extracted DNA using TaqMan single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotyping assays (C_3038793_20 and C_904973_10) according to the manufacturer's user guide (Publication No. MAN0009593, revision B.0).

### Analysis of the lipidome fatty acid profile

2.5

The LFA profiles of plasma and CSF samples were determined by gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC‐FID) as previously described. 26 The total lipid content of each sample was extracted with chloroform/methanol (2:1, v/v) in the presence of 0.01% butylated hydroxytoluene. The chloroform phase was separated and evaporated under nitrogen (N‐evap model 113 Organomation Association, Berlin, MA, USA) for 20 min. The FAs were transesterified by incubation in 2 mL of 5% (v/v) methanolic HCl at 75°C for 90 min. The resulting fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) were extracted by adding 1 mL saturated NaCl solution and 2 mL pentane to each sample. After centrifugation at 4400 rpm at 4°C for 15 min, the pentane fraction was separated and evaporated under nitrogen. Finally, the sample was redissolved in carbon disulfide (CS2), and 2 µL was used for GC analysis.

The analysis was performed on a GC System model 7890A with a series injector 7683B and FID detector, equipped with a DB‐23 capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) (Agilent Technologies, Barcelona, Spain). The injections were performed in splitless mode. The injection port was maintained at 220°C, and the detector was maintained at 250°C. The flow rate of the helium (99.99%) carrier gas was maintained at a constant rate of 2 mL/min. The temperature program was 100°C for 1 min, then increased 10°C/min to 200°C, 5°C/min to 240°C, and finally hold at 240°C for 10 min.

Identification of FAMEs was performed by comparison with known FAME standards (Table S1). The results were expressed as mol%. The FA profile detected, identified and quantified represented more than 95% of the total chromatogram. In addition, the following indices were calculated:

Saturated fatty acids (SFAs) = Σ mol% saturated fatty acids; unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) = Σ mol% unsaturated fatty acids; monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) = Σ mol% monounsaturated fatty acids; polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) = Σ mol% polyunsaturated fatty acids; polyunsaturated fatty acids from the n‐3 series (n‐3 PUFAs) = Σ mol% n‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acids; polyunsaturated fatty acids from the n‐6 series (n‐6 PUFAs) = Σ mol% polyunsaturated fatty acids n‐6; average chain length (ACL) = [(Σ% total C14 x 14) + (Σ% total C16 x 16) + (Σ% total C18 x 18) + (Σ% total C20 x 20) + (Σ% total C22 x 22)+ (Σ% total C24 x 24)]/100; the density of double bonds in the lipidome was calculated with the double bond index (DBI) = [(1 x Σmol% monoenoic) + (2 x Σmol% dienoic) + (3 x Σmol% trienoic) + (4 x Σmol% tetraenoic) + (5 x Σmol% pentaenoic) + (6 x Σmol% hexaenoic)]; the susceptibility of the lipidome to peroxidation was calculated with the peroxidability index (PI) = [(0.025 x Σmol% monoenoic) + (1 x Σmol% dienoic) + (2 x Σmol% trienoic) + (4 x Σmol% tetraenoic) + (6 x Σmol% pentaenoic) + (8 x Σmol% hexaenoic)]; and the anti‐inflammatory index (AI) = [[(C20:3 (n‐6) + (C20:5 (n‐3) + (C22:6 (n‐3)]/(C20:4 (n‐6)] × 100.

The estimates of elongase and desaturase activity were calculated from specific product/substrate ratios: delta‐5 fatty acid desaturase (Δ5 Fads1) activity = C20:4 (n‐6)/C20:3 (n‐6); Δ6 Fads2 activity = C18:4 (n‐3)/C18:3 (n‐3); Δ8 Fads2 activity = C20:3 (n‐6)/C20:2 (n‐6); Δ9 desaturase (stearoyl‐CoA desaturase‐1) activity = C16:1 (n‐7)/C16:0; Δ9 desaturase activity = C18:1 (n‐9)/C18:0; global enzymatic activity of n‐6 pathway = C22:5 (n‐6)/C18:2 (n‐6); global enzymatic activity of n‐3 pathway = C22:6 (n‐3)/C18:3 (n‐3); elongase 3 activity = C20:1 (n‐9)/C18:1 (n‐9); elongase 5 activity = C20:2 (n‐6)/C18:2 (n‐6); elongase 6 activity = C18:0/C16:0; elongase 1, 3, and 7 activity = C20:0/C18:0; elongase 1, 3, and 7 activity = C22:0/C20:0; elongase 2 and 5 activity = C22:4 (n‐6)/C20:4 (n‐6); and elongase 2 and 5 activity = C22:5 (n‐3)/C20:5 (n‐3).

### Statistical analysis

2.6

One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (or the nonparametric Kruskal‒Wallis H test) and the chi‐square test (or Fisher's exact test) were used for the analysis of quantitative and qualitative variables, respectively, among the three diagnostic groups. Student's t test (or the Mann‒Whitney U test) and the chi‐square test (or Fisher's exact test) were used for the analysis of quantitative and qualitative variables between the progressive and nonprogressive groups. The quantitative variables are presented as the mean (± standard deviation, SD) or median (25th;75th percentiles), and the qualitative variables are presented as the percentage (frequency).

The association of experimental variables with quantitative outcomes (measures of Aβ42, Ttau, and Ptau in CSF and rate of progression) was assessed using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. The association of the experimental variables with categorical outcomes (diagnosis, CSF AD biomarker status, and progression/no progression) was studied using regression models. This analysis is a set of statistical processes for estimating the relationships between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. It expresses the probability that an event will occur (vs. not occurring) (e.g., progression vs. no progression) based on some independent variables (e.g., FAs). Cox proportional hazards analysis was used to assess the association of variables with the rate of progression. Cox regression allows for investigating the effect of one or several independent variables upon the time a specified event takes to happen. For all regression analyses, the selection of variables was performed based on step‐by‐step forward selection with conditional criteria. All statistical analyses were adjusted for age, sex, MMSE, and APOE ɛ4 allele, including these parameters as predictors. The significance levels were adjusted at 5% (p < 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 25 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, United States). Principal component and heatmaps analyses were performed using Metaboanalyst software.

### Lipidome's fatty acid profile (LFA) in plasma and CSF of healthy adult subjects

3.2

The first objective of the current study was to characterize the basal differences between CSF and plasma in healthy adult subjects according to their LFA composition. The LFA profile showed marked differences between CSF and plasma (Table 3). Lower levels of C18:1(n‐9), C18:2 (n‐6), C20:3 (n‐6), C20:4 (n‐)6, C20:5 (n‐3), C22:4 (n‐6), and C22:6 (n‐3) and higher levels of C14:0, C16:1 (n‐7), C18:0, C18:1 (n‐7), C18:3 (n‐3), C20:0, C22:0, C22:1 (n‐9), and C22:5 (n‐3) were detected in the CSF than in the plasma. In terms of magnitude, the most significant changes occur at the content of C22:1 (n‐9) and C18:1 (n‐7), which exhibit a 21‐fold and 8‐fold reduction, respectively, in CSF compared to plasma. Differences in LFA composition result in lower levels of UFAs (including PUFAs and n‐6 PUFAs) and higher levels of SFAs, MUFAs, and n‐3 PUFAs in CSF, leading to lower values of DBI and PI and higher AI. Modifications in estimated desaturase and elongase activity may be behind these specific lipotypes (Table 3).

### Association of FAs with the diagnosis of MCI and AD

3.3

The association of LFAs and FA‐related indices with diagnosis was evaluated by multinomial regression analysis. CSF levels of FA were not associated with the differential diagnosis of MCI and AD patients versus controls. However, we found that the content of erucic acid (EA, C22:1 (n‐9)) was increased by 1.7‐fold in MCI and AD patients versus controls, representing the most significant change in terms of magnitude (Table S2). Besides, AI was associated with a decreased risk of MCI versus AD (odds ratio [OR] 0.350, 99% confidence interval [CI] 0.132–0.924, p = 0.005). Our analysis revealed that, AI had a significant negative correlation with the CSF measures of Ptau and Ttau (r = −0.253, p < 0.001) after adjusting of the data for age, sex, MMSE, and APOE ε4 and a significant positive correlation with MMSE (r = 0.230, p = 0.002) while controlling for age, sex, and APOE ε4 (Table S3). ## DISCUSSION

4

In a first approach, the objective was to characterize the basal differences between CSF and plasma in healthy subjects according to their LFA. CSF was characterized by decreased levels of UFAs, including PUFAs and especially n‐6 PUFAs, and enrichment of SFAs, MUFAs, and n‐3 PUFAs compared to plasma, resulting in a lower DBI and PI and a higher anti‐inflammatory index. Modifications in estimated desaturase and elongase activity, or specific selective mechanisms, among other factors, may be behind these specific lipotypes. Interestingly, both biofluids maintain an average chain length of 18 carbon atoms. Considering this scenario, these properties provide greater resistance to lipid peroxidation and, consequently, a protective environment for neural cells. This neuroprotective condition seems to be altered in AD.

In this study, in CSF, higher levels of AI decreased the risk of AD diagnosis versus MCI. In addition, higher levels of DHA were associated with a reduced risk of MCI‐to‐AD progression. Investigating LFA alterations in the CSF of patients on the AD continuum is a less studied area. Since CSF is believed to reflect brain alterations, we searched for studies on the FA profile in the AD‐affected brain. The available observations are limited and data suggest that alterations are region‐specific. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Therefore, one possibility concerning the lack of association between the CSF FA profile and diagnosis in our study is that CSF may not be an accurate mirror reflecting the alteration that takes place in only some regions of the brain.

Our results indicate an imbalance between n‐3 and n‐6 FAs that may reflect the involvement of inflammatory processes in the progression of AD. This finding agrees with the existing evidence that inflammation is a key process in AD pathogenesis and progression. 30 Disruption in the equilibrium of anti‐inflammatory and proinflammatory pathways can promote a proinflammatory environment in the central nervous system, leading to neuroinflammation and cognitive decline. 31 , 32

Our study is the first report that links DHA levels in CSF with the risk of progression. This result is in line with the previous observational studies and interventional trials performed in MCI patients. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 Animal and cellular studies suggest that the protective effect of DHA may be related to its positive effects on the modulation of neuroinflammation, 37 reduction of Aβ production and accumulation, 38 , 39 and promotion of signaling pathways leading to neuronal survival at synapses. 40 , 41

Concerning the association of plasma VA and OA levels with AD diagnosis, our findings are in agreement with some of the previous reports. 28 , 42 , 43 , 44 However, some studies did not detect dysregulation of these FAs. 27 , 45 Consistent with our results, a recent meta‐analysis showed dysregulation of both VA and OA in AD/MCI patients. 21 Our results also associated higher levels of plasma OA with a lower risk of progression for the first time. This result further emphasizes the protective effect of OA against cognitive deterioration in AD as found previously in other cohort studies. 43 Interestingly, despite the different functional role of both VA and OA, structurally they only differ in the position of the double bond, since VA belongs to the n‐7 and OA to n‐9.

OA is one of the most abundant FAs in the human body. Although some in vitro results have pointed to the deleterious effects of OA on AD pathology, 46 there is a substantial body of data evidencing the beneficial effects of OA on amyloid pathology 47 , 48 and adult hippocampal neurogenesis. 49 For instance, it has been shown that an OA‐enriched diet can reduce the levels of beta‐site APP cleaving enzyme (BACE) and presenilin along with reduced amyloid plaques in the brain of AD transgenic mouse. 48 In addition, there is also evidence that OA can decrease blood pressure 50 and exert anti‐inflammatory effects. 51 , 52

VA is a trans FA (TFA). TFAs occur in small amounts in nature, but they are widely produced by the food industry. Several reports associate higher consumption of TFA with a higher risk of AD 53 , 54 and of a key AD risk factor, cardiovascular disease. 55 Therefore, VA may indirectly contribute to a reduction in cerebral perfusion, leading to brain dysfunction, cognitive impairment and, in combination with other factors, to the neuropathology of AD. Taken together, our results regarding the detrimental and beneficial effects of VA and OA, respectively, on AD development suggest the possibility of nutritional interventions to protect against AD.

EA (22:1 (n‐9)) was remarkably increased by 21‐fold in CSF compared to plasma. Furthermore, CSF content of this FA was also increased by 1.7‐fold in AD and MCI compared to controls (although these changes did not observe in plasma). Although no data are available concerning the AD‐related alterations of EA in CSF, evidence reveals changes of EA in plasma free FAs and phospholipids from AD and MCI patients. 28 Higher plasma levels of EA were associated with a poorer executive function in elderly people with a parental history of AD, 56 and its consumption was inversely associated with cognitive impairment. 57 Interestingly, in human brain EA content is highly conserved across 13 different brain regions during the ageing process, 58 except in hippocampus, which is a region affected during the initial stages of the disease. 59 Since EA crosses the blood‐brain barrier (BBB), 60 these results suggest a possible role of EA in the progression of AD that could be expressed in CSF. However, more studies are needed to identify and evaluate the changes of EA in CSF and brain tissue of AD patients.

Little is known about the factors that affect and help to predict the rate of progression. Based on our results, higher levels of plasma DHA were associated with a faster rate of progression. Although lower serum levels of DHA have been related to an increased risk of AD development or cognitive decline in those already with AD 61 , 62 and to higher levels of amyloidosis in older adults, 63 DHA supplementation has resulted in contradictory results. 33 , 64 In a study by Quinn et al., cognitive ameliorations were observed in the DHA‐treated APOE ɛ4 noncarrier subgroup, and the cognitive benefit was not evident for DHA‐treated APOE ɛ4 carriers. These investigators observed that DHA‐treated APOE ɛ4 carriers had a smaller increase in CSF DHA than DHA‐treated noncarriers. 64 These observations were also confirmed by Arellanes et al. who demonstrated that only a small percentage of plasma DHA is transported to CSF and that APOE ɛ4 is associated with reduced delivery of this FA to the brain. 65 These findings indicate that DHA concentrations in plasma and CSF are affected by APOE isoforms. A recent investigation has demonstrated that the APOE ɛ4 isoform disrupts BBB function in the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe compared to the other APOE isoforms (ε2/ε3). 66 A large percentage of our progressive MCI patients were APOE ɛ4 carriers (63%), and we found a significant difference in the plasma levels of DHA between carriers and noncarriers of APOE ɛ4 (p = 0.012). Therefore, our results may indirectly indicate a disruption in the transport of DHA from plasma to CSF. In agreement with our hypothesis, Coughlan et al. reported an inverse association between serum DHA levels and spatial navigation performance in APOE ɛ4 carriers. 67 The existence of deficiency in DHA transporters across the BBB is another possibility that may have led to this result. Major facilitator superfamily domain containing 2A (MFSD2A) transports DHA in the form of lysophosphatidylcholine across the BBB. 68 A recent study demonstrated progressively lower blood levels of this transporter in mild and severe AD patients compared to controls. 27 Therefore, it is possible that more rapidly progressing MCI patients have lower levels of transporters, which, in turn, would limit DHA access to the brain (CSF) but increase DHA levels in the blood.

Our study has some strengths and limitations. We evaluated both the CSF and plasma of the subjects, and the MCI patients had a long follow‐up time of 5 years. However, use of a methodology that requires FA transesterification does not allow to discriminate between esterified and nonesterified FAs in the lipidome, which would be of great interest since phospholipase A2 activity is linked to AD pathology. Furthermore, we cannot discern whether the observed differences in the FA profile can be attributed to any particular lipid category or class, for which a lipidomic analysis would be required. Consequently, further studies are needed for a better understanding of the role of lipids in the onset and progression of AD and to find out their potential diagnostic use. We believe that our results add foundational knowledge to the existing literature and justify the necessity to go beyond and explore the potential pathways that link FAs dysregulation to AD development.

In conclusion, our results suggest that FA dysregulations at both the central (CSF) and systemic (plasma) levels may be involved in the pathogenesis and progression of AD and that these effects do not necessarily occur by the same pathways. Our results warrant for further investigation of potential pathways where FAs may be involved in AD development.
