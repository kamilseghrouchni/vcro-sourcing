# Dysregulation of hair-strand-based elemental biodynamics in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

## Abstract

### Background

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a rare motor neurodegenerative disorder and is predominantly diagnosed in older adults. Altered levels of essential and toxic elements have been implicated in ALS pathophysiology; however, little is known about the longitudinal biodynamic patterns of these elements in patients with ALS.

### Methods

Using a single individual hair strand, we generated time series data of 400–800 time points approximately at 2 to 4 hourly resolution on 17 elemental intensities in ALS-positive cases and ALS-negative controls from a national collection and a regional centre in the US (on a total sample of 391, with 295 cases and 96 controls, with median age at hair collection over 60 years). The elements included were Li, Mg, P, S, Ca, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Sr, Sn, Ba, and Pb. We analysed the growth increments in single hair strands using laser ablation-inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry to create time-resolved signals of elemental exposure and intensity along the hair strand. Two complementary information-theoretic methods, cross-recurrence quantification analysis and transfer entropy-based network analysis, were employed to generate time-resolved features that quantify the synchronisation of multi-element biodynamics.

### Findings

Male ALS-positive cases had significantly lower synchronicity in Cu–Zn temporal biodynamics than ALS-negative controls (recurrence: log(β) = −1.64, p-value < 0.001, q-value = 0.03). Female ALS-positive cases had lower synchronicity in Cr–Ni temporal biodynamics than ALS-negative controls (recurrence: log(β) = −1.59, p-value < 0.001, q-value = 0.46). In both males and females, multiple centrality measures of Cu (that quantify the importance of Cu within a network of all elemental intensities) were significantly lower in ALS-positive cases than in ALS-negative controls [in males, closeness centrality of Cu: log(β) = −0.64, p-value = 0.002, q-value = 0.04; in females, eigenvector centrality of Cu: log(β) = −0.53, p-value = 0.02, q-value = 0.97].

### Interpretation

We demonstrate that ALS-positive cases have significantly higher odds of collapse in the synchronisation of elemental biodynamics and worse connectedness in copper-based networks compared to ALS-negative controls.

## Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive and fatal degenerative motor neuron disease.1 There is no known cure for ALS, and individuals diagnosed with the disease have a life expectancy of 2–5 years from the onset of symptoms, which often coincides with the initial diagnosis.2 The aetiology of ALS is largely unknown, with only a small proportion (5–10%) of all cases driven by familial-type ALS3; the majority of ALS cases are sporadic and more likely driven by the complex interaction of both genetic and environmental factors.4 Although multiple fluid- and non-fluid-based biomarkers exist for ALS diagnosis, there remains significant complexity in incorporating them in a timely manner within clinical settings.5, 6, 7 From the time of the onset of symptoms, the average diagnosis of ALS remains around 10–16 months.5 Further, there are considerable racial disparities in ALS diagnostic delay, with black patients experiencing 55% longer diagnostic delays compared to white patients.6,7 Therefore, any promising diagnostic biomarker that reduces such wait time would be hugely beneficial.

Although little is known about environmental chemical exposures and ALS risk, a few recent studies have shown that potential environmental risk factors for ALS include exposure to altered levels of essential and toxic elements.8,9 Previous studies have linked overall levels of elemental exposure to ALS and reported differences in metal biomarker concentrations between cases and controls.9, 10, 11 However, the time-varying biodynamics of elemental metabolism and their role in ALS risk remain unknown, possibly due to a lack of access to readily available time-resolved signals. The biodynamics of elemental homeostasis are tightly controlled by regulatory mechanisms that respond to environmental signals of various essential and non-essential elements. Any alterations due to external perturbations may lead to disease outcomes.12, 13, 14, 15 Understanding such nuanced alterations to elemental biodynamics and identifying key predictive signatures holds promise as a future diagnostic biomarker. Prior work utilising elemental exposure history found in shed teeth showed that elemental dysregulation within the first 15 years of life can increase the risk of ALS diagnosis.16 Although this work pioneered the use of elemental biodynamics to uncover the pathology underlying ALS, obtaining shed teeth later in life is a challenging task.

Here, we analysed the growth increments in single hair strands using laser ablation-inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) to create time-resolved signals of elemental exposure and intensity at approximately 2- to 4-hourly resolution along the hair strand. The elements included were Li, Mg, P, S, Ca, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Sr, Sn, Ba, and Pb. Elemental data were normalised to sulphur (e.g., 66Zn:34S) to control for variations in the density within a hair and between hairs from different study participants. We leveraged this time series data to study the temporal biodynamics as a biomarker of elemental metabolism, assessing the patterns of deposition in hair over the period of growth represented by the sample. We employed tools such as recurrence quantification analysis, which quantifies the rhythmicity in temporal patterns of elemental intensities, and transfer entropy-based network centrality measures that quantify the strength of connectivity between individual elements.17, 18, 19, 20 The aim of this paper, therefore, is to identify signatures of systemic dysregulation through elemental biodynamic patterns that may differ between ALS-positive cases and ALS-negative controls.

## Methods

### Study population and design

Hair samples were collected and analysed from 391 participants, with 295 ALS-positive cases and 96 ALS-negative controls. Table 1 provides a demographic breakdown by age at hair collection, sex (self-reported by study participants), cohort, and mean number of time points obtained per sample. Hair samples used in this study were provided by participants based either on a regional clinical population (Dartmouth Biobank; n = 226) or from a nationwide Biobank maintained by the CDC National ALS Biorepository (n = 165). The clinical population recruited into the Dartmouth Biobank consisted of patients from the Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Centre and the Dartmouth Health Department of Neurology, with positive (case) or negative (control) diagnoses for ALS. In particular, the ALS-negative controls were recruited from a hospital-based clinical population who visited the Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Centre but were not eventually diagnosed with ALS. Due to restrictions on data sharing, no further information was available on any additional co-occurring conditions or diseases in the ALS-negative controls. Samples drawn from the CDC National ALS Biorepository were exclusively for ALS-positive cases. For the Dartmouth Biobank, hair samples were collected on the subsequent visit after diagnosis (within a window of three months). For the CDC National ALS Biorepository, most hair samples were collected after diagnosis, with a few scenarios where the hair samples were collected before diagnosis (the mean (SD) time between ALS diagnosis and hair sample collection was 1 (2.04) year). Most participants also provided more than one strand of hair. Further, given the nature of the collected hair samples, we do not have information on the head region from which the hair sample was collected. ### Ethics

Written and informed consent was obtained from each individual in this study. The institutional review boards at Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, Columbia University and Dartmouth University reviewed and approved this study (Dartmouth IRB Study# 00028053, Columbia University IRB Study# IRB-AAAT2263 and Mount Sinai IRB Study# IRB-16-00742).

### Hair analysis with LA-ICP-MS

A single hair strand was selected from each participant and washed in a solution of 1% Triton X-100 and ultra-pure water (18.2 MΩ cm−1) with ultrasonication for 1 min, followed by successive rinses with ultra-pure water that removes any traces of the surfactant. Washed hairs were dried in an oven at 60 °C overnight and mounted on plain glass microscope slides using double-sided tape. Elemental analysis along the hair strands was performed using a New Wave Research NWR-193 (ESI, USA) laser equipped with a 193 nm ArF excimer laser, connected to an Agilent Technologies 8800 triple-quadrupole ICP-MS (Agilent Technologies, USA). Samples were ablated under a flow of helium and mixed with argon via Y-piece before introduction to the ICP-MS. NIST SRM 612 (trace elements in glass) was used to tune sensitivity (maximum analyte ion counts), oxide formation (232Th16O+/232Th+, <0.3%), and fractionation (232Th+/238U+, 100 ± 5%) each day. In addition to the washing method, the risk of contamination from the environmentally exposed external surface of the hair was minimised by pre-ablating at low laser energy (0.27–0.32 Jcm−2) to remove the outer layer of the hair shaft. To ensure analytical accuracy, matrix-matched standards containing sulphur (S) levels equivalent to those in human hair were prepared from a mixture of Polyvinyl alcohol, glycerol, and K-carrageenan, and doped with a mixture of metals at varying levels. Additionally, three QC standards were prepared and spiked independently with the elements at low, medium, and high concentrations. These standard materials were used to verify that ICP-MS instruments were in control whilst samples were being tested. The standards were produced at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, and concentrations were verified externally after acid digestion. The standards were characterised in the LinusBio clinical laboratory using the same method parameters as those for hair samples. The hair was then scanned along the same path at a higher energy (0.50–0.55 Jcm−2) to collect element intensity signals along the hair strand. The scan was conducted on approximately 1.5 inches of hair, with about 400–800 sampling points per hair, where the distance between each time point represents an approximately 2- to 4-hourly resolution.21 To control for any local variations in the density within a hair and between samples, ablation efficiency and signal drift, each element’s raw ion counts were normalised pointwise to the corresponding sulphur (S) count recorded at the same time point (e.g., 66Zn:34S).22 This normalisation was applied to all elements except sulphur, resulting in relative count ratios reflective of localised concentration dynamics along the hair shaft. These sulphur-normalised elemental time series served as input to all downstream modelling. Instrument performance was continuously assessed using the matrix-matched standards. Calibration curves were constructed using at least three standards for each element, and linearity and percent deviation from expected values were reviewed. Quality Control (QC) materials were run at least every 3 h, and instrument performance was assessed before samples were cleared to run. Instrument performance was also monitored using control charts of the mean concentration of the QC materials, following the guidelines set out in-house. Data that passed QC was cleared for further statistical analysis.

### Statistics

Data processing and analyses were performed in R version 4.3.0 (R Core Team, 2023). The threshold level for multiple comparison p-values was estimated based on the eigenvalue-based effective number of features.23 However, while presenting the log (odds), we utilised a bit more restrictive q-value that controls the expected proportion of false discoveries.24 Analysis of hair signals involved two successive stages of signal processing and statistical analysis. Recent literature suggests that there might be differences in symptoms of ALS between men and women.25,26 Further, elemental metabolism may have significant sexual dimorphism.27 Therefore, we conducted our analysis separately for males and females.

The analysis was conducted in multiple stages. First, we conducted initial individual element-wise regression analysis, starting with mean intensity levels that average out the intensity over the temporal scale. Second, to incorporate the temporal biodynamics of elemental intensities, we estimated a recurrence measure called “determinism” using the recurrence quantification analysis and conducted subsequent regression analyses.28,29 Determinism measures the predictability or regularity of a dynamical system.29 However, note that analyses based on individual features may be a simple approximation of a far complex higher-order system, such as human elemental metabolism.30 Indeed, recent literature suggests that higher-order interactions play a key role in biological systems.31 Multiple studies, particularly in exposomics, have highlighted that higher-order interactions of elemental exposures may be associated with an increased risk of later-stage health outcomes, such as autism spectrum disorder, neurodevelopmental disorders, and alterations in gut microbial diversity.32, 33, 34 Recent dentine-based studies on elemental biodynamics have shown early-life pair-wise Zn–Cu metabolic cycles may predict the risk of autism spectrum disorder in children.30 Therefore, our main analysis involves exploring higher-order elemental biodynamics, starting from pair-wise temporal interactions. We used a modified version of the cross-recurrence quantification analysis (CRQA) to quantify the pair-wise synchronisation pattern (within a time window that maximises the correlation) between two intensity-based time series (referred to as in-sync CRQA).35 To complement this method, we constructed network-based measures using transfer entropy, which quantifies the degree of temporal dependency in the biodynamics of elemental pairs.20

### In-sync CRQA-based analysis

This method identifies a pair-wise synchronisation pattern (within a time window that maximises the correlation) between two intensity-based time series, specifically a pair of elemental time series data. Each pair is “synced”, i.e., a time window of a certain length is determined between the two pairs such that the Spearman correlation is maximised or the Euclidean distance is minimised (so long as the intensities are scaled). Once this window is determined, the main analysis proceeds with the usual CRQA analysis.35 The hypothesis behind syncing the two time series is that certain pairs of elemental biodynamics in ALS-positive cases may exhibit reduced synchronicity compared to the ALS-negative controls. The CRQA analysis was conducted at a radius of 0.4, with delay and embedding parameters set at the baseline initial values (at radius values of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3, a few missing values were present in synchronicity features; however, at a radius of 0.4, no missing feature values were observed. Therefore, we kept the radius at 0.4 as the minimal parsimonious estimate). For each pair, nine separate CRQA-based measures were estimated. The measures are (1) RR (Recurrence), the percentage of recurrent points that fall within the specified radius (here it is 0.4), (2) DET (determinism), the proportion of recurrent points forming diagonal line structures, (3) NRLINE, estimates of the total number of lines in the recurrent plot, (4) L, the average length of lines in the recurrent plot, (5) maxL, the length of the longest line segment in the plot excluding the diagonal line, (6) LAM (laminarity), the proportion of recurrent points forming vertical lines, (7) TT, the mean length of the vertical lines, and (8) ENTR, the Shannon entropy of the diagonal lines longer than the lines that are shorter than minimum specified length (here it is two), (9) rENTR, relative Shannon entropy that is normalised by the number of lines in the plot, and lastly (10) maxCorr, the maximum correlation observed between two in-synced elemental time series. These features, being outcome agnostic, were directly used in the downstream regression analyses. In the regression analysis, only those pairs with non-null variance were kept.

### Transfer entropy-based network analysis

For each subject, an elemental connectivity network was constructed, where the nodes represented elements, and the edge between each pair of elements was defined by the transfer entropy between their time series. Since transfer entropy is an asymmetric measure, the higher entropy was chosen for each node pair. Within each subject’s network, edges with transfer entropy values not reaching statistical significance were pruned away. Statistical significance here was assessed by using Markov block bootstrapping to simulate the distribution of transfer entropy estimates under the null hypothesis of no information flow. For each subject’s pruned network, a number of centrality measures were computed: node (element) degree, eigenvalue centrality, betweenness, closeness, eccentricity, and clustering coefficient. Each measure was computed per node.

For ease of interpretation, all the pair-wise in-sync CRQA features were transformed into binary indicators (interpreted as high vs. low synchronicity) (in total, 1226 features). The centrality measures were transformed into quartiles (higher values are interpreted as a higher degree of connectedness) (in total, 112 features). We estimated associations with ALS diagnosis using feature-wide logistic regression models, adjusting for the patient's age at the time of hair sample collection (additional covariate-adjusted models were presented in the sensitivity analysis). Our primary analyses were conducted using the Dartmouth Biobank, and the statistically significant features were then verified through an exploratory sensitivity analysis that combined data from the Dartmouth and CDC Biobanks. The false discovery rate was controlled using the q-value.24,36 Although multiple in-sync CRQA and network centrality features were statistically significant, we only present those associations with the lowest q-values. Lastly, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses, the results of which are provided in the section below.

## Results

### Study population description

The basic demographics of the study samples were presented in Table 1. The mean age at hair collection was similar for both ALS-positive cases and ALS-negative controls, around 60 years. The length of the hair samples, i.e., the number of time points, was also similar between cases and controls. There were more males (58.6%) than females (41.4%) in this sample. Given that we only had access to ALS-positive cases from the CDC National ALS Biorepository, this sample has a higher proportion of cases (25%) than controls (75%). The length of hair used for the elemental analysis was approximately 1.53 inches (SD was 0.2), with around 766 time points measured at a 2- to 4-hourly resolution. The overall scaled Cu intensity levels did not differ significantly between cases and controls; however, the importance of Cu biodynamics (in a network of elemental connectedness) was significantly different between cases and controls.

We presented results on individual elemental level analysis from two modalities: mean elemental intensities (which collapsed the temporality of intensities) and determinism from RQA-based biodynamic analysis (which preserved and exploited the temporality of intensities) (Fig. 2). Only individual determinism measures using Pb and Zn in males were statistically significant (Fig. 2b). ### Cu biodynamics is more centrally connected in controls

Multiple Cu centrality measures (that indicate the degree to which each element is connected to others throughout a network) were higher in controls than in cases (Fig. 4c and d). Given the number of centrality measures, the adjusted p-value threshold was estimated to be 0.01 (Fig. 4c and d). The associations remained significant in males even after controlling for multiple comparison errors. In males, multiple centrality measures of Cu were significantly lower in cases than in controls [closeness: log(β) = −0.64, p-value = 0.002, q-value = 0.04; betweenness: log(β) = −0.54, p-value = 0.002, q-value = 0.04; eigenvector: log(β) = −0.60, p-value = 0.003, q-value = 0.04] (Fig. 4c). For illustration, we presented the distribution of closeness centrality of Cu as a violin plot to show the stark difference between cases and controls in males (Fig. 5c). For females, similarly, Cu eigenvector centrality was lower in cases than in controls [eigenvector: log(β) = −0.53, p-value = 0.02, q-value = 0.97] (Fig. 4d). However, none of the centrality measures had an adjusted p-value of less than 0.01. However, similar to the males, we presented the distribution of eigenvector centrality of Cu as a violin plot to show the stark difference between cases and controls in females (Fig. 5d). Therefore, in both males and females, we observed a collapse of Cu-based network structure in the cases rather than in the controls.

### Sensitivity analysis

Multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted to substantiate the main results. (1) For males, the in-sync CRQA analysis was repeated at 0.3 and 0.5 radius to ensure that the laminarity of Cu–Zn recurrence was still one of the top features (Supplementary Table S1). The hypothesis behind this analysis was that subtle changes in radius should not wholly alter the top synchronicity feature. The radii values 0.1 and 0.2 were not chosen since some of the recurrence measures could not be estimated because of the absence of any recurrence at those small values. Note that at both the radii values 0.3 and 0.5, the laminarity of Cu–Zn Recurrence remains one of the top features. (2) In the same in-sync CRQA analysis at 0.4 radii, the association estimates of other Cu–Zn recurrence measures (within the top 10% features) were reported to ensure that not just laminarity but also multiple similar aspects of the Cu–Zn biodynamics are also potential discriminatory features (Supplementary Table S2). (3) Since the unadjusted p-values estimated through the regression analyses strongly relied on large sample asymptotic assumptions, we estimated robust permutation-based p-values (iterated 105 times) for the main in-sync CRQA and centrality-based measures (which were presented in the results section). In all the scenarios, the permutation-based p-values were much smaller than the asymptotic-based p-values (Supplementary Table S3). (4) We repeated the analyses on the reported main associations using samples from both the CDC Biobank and Dartmouth cases and controls to check sensitivity to including additional samples. All the major associations remained statistically significant even when the ALS cases from the CDC Biobank were included along with the Dartmouth cases and controls (see Supplementary Table S4). (5) The regression analyses for both males and females were also repeated, adjusting for additional covariates like run order of elemental analysis to control for any plausible batch effect, as well as self-reported ethnicity. However, all main results remained unaltered.

## Discussion

In this paper, we have utilised temporally dense time-series data of elemental intensities to demonstrate that there is a systemic dysregulation of multiple elements (particularly Cu) in ALS-positive cases, involving altered dependencies among elemental pathways. By generating time series intensity data of approximately 400–800 points at 2–4 hourly resolution for 17 elemental intensities from single individual hair strands and utilising state-of-the-art information theory-based tools, we demonstrate the feasibility of developing a non-fluid-based diagnostic biomarker for ALS. The most prominent fluid-based biomarker that is currently used for ALS is neurofilament light chain (NfL), which is typically measured in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and can indicate neuronal damage associated with the disease; other potential biomarkers include TDP-43, total tau, and cystatin C, also measured in CSF.37,38 A non-fluid-based biomarker for ALS currently being investigated is structural imaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging, specifically utilising methods like cortical thickness measurements (surface-based morphometry), which can reveal progressive brain atrophy in regions associated with motor function, considered a key feature of ALS.39 However, a simple hair-based biochemical test for ALS can potentially hold strong promise due to the inherent ease of hair-strand collection. We note that these current methods will not directly translate to a diagnostic test. This is because associations do not necessarily imply strong prediction. However, our current association-based results provide initial proof of concept that there exist stark differences between hair-based elemental biodynamics among ALS cases and controls. This information (elemental biodynamic features showing discrimination from controls) can be leveraged in a comprehensive and properly tuned machine-learning model to create a prevalence-adjusted diagnostic test for ruling in ALS cases.

ALS has also been associated with perturbations in essential elements. Specifically, the ALS-related dysregulation in Cu biodynamics was particularly significant. Copper is a cofactor of SOD140, 41, 42, 43 and plays a crucial role in an established molecular pathway in ALS pathology. Increased Cu concentrations have been documented in the spinal cord of mutant TDP-43 ALS mice and ALS patients.42,44 In various peripheral biospecimens, Cu was found to be elevated in some cases but decreased or unchanged in others.45 Furthermore, the Cu-containing pesticide, copper sulphate, can dissolve in blood after exposure and be carried throughout the body and transported across the blood-brain barrier (BBB) as a free Cu ion.46 Our prior work also demonstrated evidence of copper dysregulation in relation to ALS diagnosis using teeth-based elemental biomarkers.16

As noted earlier, NfL is a widely recognised biomarker for axonal injury and neurodegeneration.47 However, in light of our current results on Cu biodynamics, not much is known about their complex interplay and any potential role in neurodegenerative diseases. A few studies suggest that an imbalance in Cu homeostasis can contribute to neuronal dysfunction and the accumulation of protein aggregates, including those involving NfL.48 Cu ions, particularly in the presence of hydrogen peroxide, may induce aggregation and modification of NfL in vitro, potentially leading to the formation of amyloid-like structures.48 Some research suggests that copper exposure might influence the aggregation of proteins involved in Parkinson's disease, such as alpha-synuclein.49 While the exact mechanisms are still being investigated, the interaction between copper and NfL is likely to contribute to the progression of neurodegenerative processes.50

We also found evidence supporting previously reported effects involving Cr, Sn, Ni, and Zn.51 Although previous studies used tooth samples to compare elemental intensity levels in childhood, the current study employed hair samples collected later in life, revealing alterations in the biodynamics of similar elements. The interaction of Cr and Ni has been previously associated with lung cancer risk and genotoxic effects, but not much is known about their joint effect on ALS.52,53 However, there is little epidemiological evidence of their individual effects. A recent study found that ALS cases had higher chromium uptake after the age of ten years than controls, whereas another case-control study found Ni to be a protective factor.9,51 In animal models, nickel-induced brain injuries have been shown to eventually induce neurodegeneration in the rat brain.54 While the exact mechanisms of Cr and Ni interactions are not fully understood, some studies suggest that these elements may share similar cellular pathways, potentially amplifying their combined toxic effects when present.55

The limitations of this study are primarily related to the characteristics of the participants. We pooled data from two biobanks with differing characteristics for validation; however, the primary analysis used ALS-positive cases and ALS-negative controls identified from the same geographic area. Additionally, our sample was unbalanced, with a greater number of cases than controls. Our study cohorts did not account for occupational, socioeconomic, and other exposures; however, these exposures would be captured through the hair biomarkers we used in this study. This study also lacked information on any additional co-occurring conditions or diseases in the ALS-negative controls, as well as details on possible ALS subtypes, which would have facilitated conducting stratified analysis. Such endeavours can be undertaken in larger future studies. We acknowledge that although we undertook multiple safeguards against possible external perturbations (such as shampoos and dyes), a few residual risks of contamination may remain.

Our study introduced a novel information-theoretic framework to model pair-wise elemental biodynamics in epidemiological studies. A key strength of our study lies in utilising hair-based biochemical analysis to elucidate the intricate temporal patterns of elemental biodynamics. We demonstrate that ALS cases have significantly higher odds of collapse in synchronisation of elemental biodynamics and worse connectedness in copper-based networks compared to controls. Although this study does not develop a biomarker test for ALS due to a lack of additional balanced samples, the association-based results show strong promise. In conclusion, we provide evidence that elemental biodynamics, as measured in hair strands, may be related to ALS pathology. Future work will expand the use of hair samples to create predictive biomarkers in distinguishing ALS cases from controls.
