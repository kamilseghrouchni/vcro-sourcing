# Improving the Detection of Analyte Degeneracies in Untargeted Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry Data

## Abstract

Advances in liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) and chemometrics have driven the field of untargeted metabolomics forward. However, interpretation of these studies can be challenging, since these data sets often return thousands of features with only a small fraction identifiable. Most of these unidentified features arise from degeneracy, where a single analyte produces multiple features from adduct formation, in-source fragments, and isotopes. This work improves the detection and clustering of these degenerate features with a new peak shape consistency metric, termed lack-of-fit (LOF). This metric quantifies the residual error between two features within a time window, where a LOF <20% suggests degeneracy. To first evaluate metric performance, 21 analytes were spiked into brain dialysate and features were discovered using tile-based Fisher ratio (F-ratio) analysis. Incorporating the proposed LOF metric not only reduced the feature list and retained all the spiked analytes in the top 25 hits but also outperformed other data-driven degeneracy methods by ~18–48%. LOF clustering was then applied to reduce the number of features detected in brain dialysate at different preconcentration levels and mobile phase gradient lengths. LOF clustering provided a 90% degenerate feature classification accuracy, surpassing existing LC–MS/MS data processing methods. Despite the chromatographic complexity, this metric could resolve features from coeluting compounds at lower resolutions (Rs ≤ 0.3) than the standard correlation-based method. LOF clustering reduced this untargeted data set by ~79–86%, ultimately revealing ~583 unique metabolites in brain dialysate. These results collectively demonstrate that the LOF metric can improve and strengthen the interpretation of untargeted metabolomics data.

## INTRODUCTION

Metabolites are the ultimate downstream products of the genome. These small molecules, along with others to which an organism ingests or is exposed, provide an important key in understanding cellular function and phenotype. Studies of these small molecules using untargeted metabolomics can discover new disease biomarkers, generate hypotheses of biological mechanisms, uncover responses to xenobiotics or other manipulations, and differentiate between phenotypes among other applications.1–4 Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) is typically used in untargeted metabolomics for its high resolving power in both the time and m/z dimensions.5 A challenge in untargeted metabolomics is that the majority of the features, signals with a given peak shape, retention time, and m/z, detected by LC–MS remain unidentified. A recent work has also shown that many detected features are degenerate (i.e., arising from the same compound).6–8 This report describes a new approach to detect these degeneracies, improving the interpretation of statistical analyses and identification of compounds within a complex sample.

In untargeted metabolomics, data processing workflows automatically extract chemical features from the full-scan (MS1) data.9–12 Identification of these features relies on collecting MS/MS spectra that can be searched against experimental13–15 or in silico16,17 databases. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) is commonly used to target specific ions from the MS1 data for subsequent fragmentation.18 Since DDA preferentially selects high intensity ions, strategies like rolling precursor ion exclusion and iterative DDA runs can improve annotation for coeluting and/or low abundance features.19,20 However, feature identification remains limited because experimental spectra may lack sufficient product ions for confident matching or reference MS/MS spectra are absent from current libraries.15,21 As a result, many features detected in a metabolomics sample (~80–95%) remain unknown.4,6,19,22

While many unidentified features correspond to unknown metabolites,23 studies have also shown that many of these signals could be due to contaminants, artifacts, or degeneracies.6–8 The latter refers to multiple features that arise from a single analyte (e.g., in-source fragments, unannotated adducts, and isotopes).24 For example, Mahieu and Patti distilled a metabolomics data set containing ~25,000 features down to ~900 unique metabolites by applying degeneracy filters.6 The large discrepancy between the number of features and compounds in a data set complicates metabolite identification and interpretation efforts. Features due to contamination or artifacts can be easily removed through the analysis of blanks or use of an isotope-based credentialing process.6,25 However, identifying degeneracies can be challenging since reference MS/MS spectra may not be available.26 Because some degeneracies (e.g., in-source fragments, multimers) are not well characterized,27,28 rule tables may not capture all possible relationships and manual refinement is required.6,29,30 Therefore, data-driven methods utilize the Pearson correlation coefficient to calculate the similarity between features27,31–34 or chemometric decomposition models to mathematically resolve the signals.35–37 For instance, CAMERA27 and MS-CleanR33 consolidate feature groups based on Pearson correlations while multivariate curve resolution-alternating least-squares (MCR-ALS) is a standard chemometric decomposition model for two-way data.35–37 Yet the accuracy of these methods is greatly reduced when features correspond to coeluting analytes, e.g., at resolution (Rs) ≤ 0.3.38–43

To improve the detection of feature degeneracies in LC–MS/MS data, this work establishes the use of a new computational approach based on a lack-of-fit (LOF) metric. This metric was first proposed for gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS),41–44 where unit-resolution mass spectrometers often leads to analytes sharing similar m/z. For GC–MS, the LOF metric was primarily used to separate overlapping compounds with similar m/z.41–44 This metric has not been applied in LC–MS/MS metabolomics workflows, especially those aimed at clustering degenerate features (e.g., adducts, isotopes, in-source fragments) from the same compound. This work shows that LOF clustering is suitable for LC–MS feature reduction, offering a more accurate assessment of degeneracies than existing approaches using correlation coefficients or chemometric decomposition. LOF clustering also groups degenerate features without predefined adduct lists, enabling the detection of unknown or unexpected degenerates that other approaches may miss. We use two data sets to demonstrate the advantages of the LOF clustering. Initially, 21 compounds were spiked into brain dialysate, and LOF clustering was used to detect degeneracies in the resulting feature list from Fisher ratio (F-ratio) analysis. The LOF metric was subsequently applied to deepen the coverage of the brain metabolome by examining how sample preconcentration and separation time can improve the number of identifiable compounds. The accuracy of LOF clustering for analyzing these biological samples is demonstrated in comparison with three established approaches: CAMERA,27 MS-CleanR,33 and MCR-ALS.35–37 Ultimately, this work demonstrates that this approach is effective at detecting degeneracies and can be easily implemented in standard data processing workflows.

## METHOD FUNDAMENTALS

LOF clustering for LC–MS data aims to group degenerate features based on their similarity in the peak shape. This goal is distinct from its previous uses in GC–MS, where the LOF metric was used to discover the purest m/z to identify and quantify overlapping analytes.41–44 LOF clustering also differs from correlation-based methods (e.g., CAMERA and MS-CleanR), for clustering degenerate features, which rely on global measures of covariance,27,33 and MCR-ALS, which decomposes the pure chromatographic profiles and mass spectra for all analytes at once.35–37 These three standard approaches can include features from neighboring compounds, while LOF clustering is focused on localized differences in retention times and peak widths.

The analysis workflow for LOF clustering is shown in Figure 1. This approach can be applied to MS1-level data collected in either positive or negative ionization modes. The main input for LOF clustering is a preprocessed feature list, which can be generated from in-house chemometric workflows (e.g., tile-based F-ratio analysis), instrument vendor software, or opensource approaches, such as XCMS, MS-DIAL, and MZmine. Features with similar retention times, based on a user-defined “cluster window”, are then identified for LOF testing. The cluster window size should be 50–75% of the average peak width.45 Within this cluster window, a pairwise comparison of peak shape by using the LOF metric is performed. The LOF metric is calculated as (1)LOF=∑i(SB-SA)i2∑iSAi2×100 where SA and SB are the chromatographic profiles for two features in the comparison.41,43,44,46 SB was normalized to the area of SA prior to calculating the LOF using eq 1.

Features with a LOF ≤20% are determined to be degenerate. Although previous GC–MS studies suggested that this threshold would be suitable,41,44 we validated its applicability to clustering LC–MS degeneracies by testing the LOF threshold ranging from 10 to 30%. The accuracy of these thresholds was evaluated using 21 spiked compounds, representing the mass range, chromatographic space, and physicochemical properties previously observed in dialysate (Table S1; Figures S1 and S2).20 A LOF threshold of 20% balances high classification sensitivity and specificity, resulting in an overall accuracy of 97.8% (Table S2). LOF clustering requires proper chromatographic preprocessing. For example, poor baseline correction, excessive smoothing, and retention time misalignment can distort peak shapes, thereby inflating LOF scores. The output from LOF clustering is an updated table with all degeneracies grouped into final “feature groups”. These feature groups provide the best representation for the number of compounds present in a metabolomics sample. While LOF clustering operates in an unsupervised fashion, in silico databases16,17 or rule tables from other approaches27,29 can be applied to each feature group to provide structural annotation. However, these methods may leave some adducts unannotated (more details in the Supporting Information; Table S3).

## EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

### Data Generation.

All animal experiments were conducted using an approved protocol by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Michigan. Dialysate was collected from four female Sprague–Dawley rats (~300–350 g) using CMA 12 Elite microdialysis probes (4 mm long membrane, 0.5 mm O.D., 20 kDa cutoff; Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA) at 1 μL/min for 12 h and pooled. The pooled dialysate sample was then split for the two study aims: one portion was spiked with 21 analytes at 100 and 200 ppb, while the other was analyzed at both its native concentration (1×) and as a 10× concentrate. All samples were analyzed using a HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA) on a Vanquish Horizon LC coupled to an Orbitrap ID-X MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Both MS1 and MS/MS data were collected in the positive ion mode. Five iterative DDA runs were performed on the 1 and 10× dialysate samples to improve MS/MS acquisition. Additional details regarding data generation can be found in the Supporting Information.

### Data Analysis.

For LOF clustering, all chromatograms were converted into.mzXML files using MSConvertGUI (ProteoWizard, Palo Alto, CA)47 and imported into Matlab 2024a (Mathworks, Inc., Natick, MA). A region of interest (ROI) preprocessing algorithm11,12,35 was used to compress the MS1 data prior to feature discovery. Based on the chromatographic data and instrument accuracy, signals with a width of ≥6 data points, a mass deviation ≤5 ppm, and intensity ≥1 × 104 were extracted. Noise on these ROIs were further filtered using the centWave algorithm48 before being reinserted into an empty chromatogram at their original retention times and average m/z values. The resulting chromatograms were then baseline corrected using a rolling ball minimum and normalized to the sum of the total ion current (TIC) chromatogram. To discover features corresponding to the 21 spiked analytes, tile-based F-ratio analysis12,49 compared six replicates of the 100 and 200 ppb spiked dialysate samples using a tile size of 12.5 s. This tile size was selected based on the observed peak widths and retention time shifting in the data set. Features were then ranked in the descending order of the F-ratio. In parallel, features from the second untargeted brain metabolomics data set were discovered if they had a peak area greater than threefold that of the artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) blanks. Features in both data sets were grouped together using a cluster window size of 6.5 s and a LOF threshold ≤20%.

Details regarding the implementation of CAMERA, MS-CleanR, and MCR-ALS can be found in the Supporting Information. Briefly, degenerate features were grouped by CAMERA (XCMS package, R 4.5.1) and MS-CleanR (MS-DIAL 5.1) based on a Pearson correlation ≥0.75 and ≥0.8, respectively.27,33 For MCR-ALS (PLS Toolbox 9.3, eigenvector Research, Inc., Manson, WA), a 12.5 s chromatographic window was extracted around each analyte peak was extracted. The number of components was first visually estimated and then optimized by testing ±2 components from that estimate. The lowest rank model that best preserved the original peak profiles was selected for further analysis.

Compound identifications were made in a top-down fashion using MS/MS matching to either in-house or public libraries, including NIST20 (National Institute for Standards and Technology, Gaithersburg, MD), LipidBlast,14 and MassBank of North America (MONA, www.mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu). The observed m/z for each feature was initially searched as a precursor ion in the experimental MS/MS data using a 5 ppm tolerance. If a match was found, then the experimental MS/MS spectrum was queried against these libraries using the same 5 ppm of precursor ion threshold. MS/MS match quality between the query and library MS/MS spectra was quantified as both a dot product (DP) and spectral entropy score (ES), where tentative identifications were made for high quality matches (DP > 700 and ES > 0.65).20,50,51 Retention time matching with a tolerance <6 s was also used to identify the 21 spiked analytes.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### Demonstration on Spiked Dialysate Data Set.

To evaluate the performance of the LOF metric in the context of an untargeted LC–MS/MS analysis, brain dialysate was spiked with 21 compounds at two concentrations (100 vs 200 ppb). These analytes were selected to create a challenging data set for feature discovery due to their low Rs values with other compounds native to dialysate. They were also chosen to span the metabolite-derived chromatographic window, mass range, octanol–water partition coefficients (logP), and main chemical classifications previously observed dialysate20 (Table S1; Figures S1 and S2). The TIC chromatogram obtained for the spiked dialysate sample is shown in Figure 2A and corresponding retention time locations for spiked compounds are depicted in Figure 2B. Tile-based F-ratio analysis12,49 was used to generate a feature list by comparing six injection replicates of each spiked sample class. Figure 2C highlights the F-ratio distribution, where red asterisks mark the number of identified spikes found in each bin. Table 1 lists the features identified as one of the 21 spiked compounds based on a MS/MS similarity match. Both Figure 2C and Table 1 show that all the spiked compounds were discoverable with an F-ratio >1000 as expected. However, these identified features were also interspersed with over 300 “unknown” features, for which an experimental MS/MS spectrum was not acquired and/or a reference was unavailable. Given that the samples were nominally identical except for the spiked compounds, this large number of unknowns with high F-ratios is illustrative of potential degeneracy that can affect statistical interpretations.

Figure 3 explores the relationship between degeneracy and analyte discovery in a comparative analysis with two representative spiked analytes: (A) creatinine and (B) picolinic acid. Creatinine is an ideal case where the first feature discovered while mining the F-ratio hit list provides confident identification. The F-ratios calculated per-m/z (i.e., the F-ratio spectrum; purple) for creatinine align with the recorded intensities in its MS1 spectrum (black; Figure 3Ai). Since the [M+H]+ ion at m/z 114.0661 had the largest F-ratio, this feature would be discovered first during top-down mining of the hit list. An MS/MS spectrum for this ion was also recorded, enabling identification via spectral matching (DP = 963, ES = 0.95). Degenerate features from isotopes and salt adducts, such as the [M+Na]+ ion at m/z 136.0480 (Figure 3Ai), had a lower F-ratio selectivity and were not selected for MS/MS fragmentation during DDA. Figure 3Aii illustrates the similarity in retention time and peak shape (including the peak tailing) between the [M+H]+ and the [M+Na]+ ions of creatinine, resulting in a LOF equal to 12.9%. Applying LOF clustering to all features in the F-ratio hit list revealed that the isotope of the [M+H]+ ion for creatinine also exhibited an F-ratio >1000 (Figure 3Aiii). Although it would be expected that a feature with the largest F-ratio would be an excellent candidate for MS/MS identification, this was not observed for all of the spiked analytes. For example, the [M+H]+ ion at m/z 124.0392 for picolinic acid provided a high-quality MS/MS spectrum for identification (DP = 953; ES = 0.99) despite having an F-ratio barely above the threshold of 1000 (Figure 3Bi). Interestingly, the largest F-ratio was calculated for the feature at m/z 80.0494, which corresponds to the [M+H–CO2]+ ion for picolinic acid.52 However, a MS/MS spectrum for this ion was not experimentally recorded, and even if it was, a matching spectrum does not exist in current libraries. Fortunately, LOF clustering demonstrates these two features are correlated to picolinic acid, yielding a low LOF of 4.7% (Figure 3Bii). In fact, LOF clustering discovered several degeneracies for picolinic acid, including isotopes and insource fragments (Figure 3Biii). Without prior knowledge of this study, picolinic acid would have only been identifiable via the [M+H]+ ion, which was ranked lower in the F-ratio hit list compared to other features. Thus, LOF clustering is advantageous for discovering degeneracies a priori, using a quantitative metric rather than querying known adduct relationships.

Table 2 provides an updated F-ratio hit list after clustering degenerate features using the LOF metric. The hit list was reranked by using the feature with the highest F-ratio. The relationship of the ion to the analyte is also provided, where the bold text indicates the original precursor ion from Table 1 that triggered an MS/MS identification. All 21 spiked analytes were discovered near the top of the hit list, and only two false positives were found toward the bottom of the abbreviated hit list. Table 2 reveals that many of these “unknown” features with F-ratios >1000 were different degeneracies (e.g., isotopes, salt adducts, in-source fragments) of the spiked compounds. In fact, the original precursor ions that generated an MS/MS match were not even ranked in the top 3 F-ratio m/z for almost half of the spiked analytes (Table S4). Interestingly, the top F-ratio m/z for vanillic acid and carbamazepine were due to analyte adduction with background ethanol and sodium nitrate hydrate ions (Figures S3 and S4). Previous works have noted that this phenomenon can give rise to unannotated degeneracies, which can be difficult to trace due to differences in samples, reagents, and instrumental methods.6,29 As the stark difference in F-ratio rankings between Tables 1 and 2 suggests, the presence of degenerate features increases the analytical burden by extending the time required to reach meaningful identifications for unique metabolites at lower F-ratios. Accurately clustering degenerate features not only improves the overall F-ratio rankings but also minimizes effort spent on reidentifying features linked to already known compounds. Clustering these degeneracies can also provide a clearer picture of the unknown metabolome, redirecting analytical efforts to improve characterization of those unidentified compounds.23,53

Improvement in F-ratio analyte discovery using LOF clustering can be rigorously evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which encompasses the number of true positives, false positives, and relative ranking of features in each hit list.54 Figure 4 compares the ROC curves generated using the original hit list (Table 1; orange) and after clustering degenerate features based on a retention time window (Table S5; green),12,37,49 Pearson correlation (Table S6; purple),27,31–34 or LOF metric (Table 2; black). The true positive rate reflects the proportion of correctly identified features (or feature groups) due to the spiked analytes, while the false positive rate captures the number of features labeled “unknown” or unchanging in each hit list. The area under each ROC curve (AUC) quantifies the chemometric performance of each hit list,54 where an AUC of 1 indicates that all the spikes are discovered and are at the top of the hit list. Based on the AUCs, LOF clustering improved analyte discovery by ~48% relative to the original hit list. Notably, our LOF method outperformed clustering features based on a retention time window or Pearson correlation by ~18–48%. While the use of retention time windows or Pearson correlations improved hit list rankings by consolidating degeneracies, these methods also grouped overlapping spiked analytes together. As a result, only 16 or 20 analytes from the 21-spike mixture were discoverable as a distinct hit, respectively. Therefore, the incorporation of both retention time and peak shape similarity in the LOF approach ensures that unresolved features will not be erroneously clustered together. The next section further illustrates how the LOF metric improves degeneracy clustering for features at low Rs, which is critical for untargeted applications.

### Application to Unknown Feature Identification in Brain Metabolomics.

The brain extracellular space is a complex microdomain between brain cells with a chemical composition that reflects several physiological functions occurring simultaneously on different spatiotemporal scales.55 Untargeted monitoring the extracellular space in vivo provides an opportunity to correlate metabolites with different brain functional states.3,4,20,56–58 Recently, our group identified 479 analytes in dialysate from the nucleus accumbens using LC–MS/MS on preconcentrated samples.20 Like other untargeted studies, a large fraction of features detected in the brain metabolome remained unidentified.4,20 To better understand these features, we applied the LOF metric to determine whether they arise from degeneracies or different compounds.

Figure 5 highlights how adjusting mobile phase gradient length (20 vs 60 min) and sample preconcentration (1× vs 10×) affects the number of unknown and identified features. Five iterative MS/MS injections were acquired to improve compound identification in each combination (Figure S5). Starting with the 20 min gradient, the 10× dialysate sample provided a substantial increase in the signal compared with the 1× sample (Figure 5Ai). The sample preconcentration not only improved the number of identifications (151 vs 282) but also resulted in more unknown MS1 features (4016 vs 7943), as seen in Figure 5Aii,iii. Previous works have also demonstrated that larger mass loadings can improve MS/MS data quality to identify compounds via database matching.19,20 The concurrent rise in the number of unknown features can be attributed to compounds that were previously below the limit of detection. Use of longer mobile phase gradients like in Figure 5Bi can reduce coelution, which can increase the number of identified compounds. The separation of 1× dialysate with a 60 min gradient resulted in 316 identified analytes and 6251 unknown features (Figure 5Bii), while the same separation for 10× dialysate produced 499 identified compounds and 5182 unknown features (Figure 5Biii). For any combination of conditions, the number of compounds identified was <10% of the number of features detected. While this result is on par with previous metabolomics studies,4,6,19,22 it raises the question of what proportion of these unknowns represent degenerate signals.

This uncertainty in LC–MS metabolomics data highlights the need to develop robust methods to accurately cluster and separate degenerate features from coeluting analytes. As common methods for consolidating feature groups, both CAMERA and MS-CleanR use a Pearson correlation to evaluate peak shape similarity.27,33 Figure 6 shows the difference between LOF clustering and this Pearson correlation for discriminating between features from three unresolved analyte pairs: (A) 1-methyladenosine and 7-methylguanosine, (B) N-acetyllysine and N-acetylornithine, and (C) uric acid and nicotinic acid. The top row displays the chromatograms for the [M+H]+ ion of the first (red) and second (blue) analytes listed for each pair. The chromatograms were normalized such that the two features had the same peak area. Additionally, the bottom row plots the LOF (black) and Pearson correlation (r; purple) as a function of time. These metrics operate inversely, where clustering relies on either the minimization of LOF (≤20%) or maximization of r (≥80%). The example shown in Figure 6A demonstrates that both metrics can discriminate features belonging to different analytes with Rs ≥ 0.3. However, Figure 6B,C illustrates that a Pearson correlation fails to distinguish between coeluting analytes at Rs ≤ 0.3, which can cause errors in downstream data analysis and interpretation. In contrast, the LOF metric continues to distinguish between these features because of its sensitivity to local discrepancies in peak shape, whereas correlation coefficients capture only overall covariance.

To expand upon the results in Figure 6, the performance of LOF clustering on real metabolomics data was benchmarked against CAMERA, MS-CleanR, and MCR-ALS. These four methods were assessed using 65 known metabolites in the brain dialysate. As highlighted in Table S7 and Figure S6, these 65 metabolites represent the retention times, masses, logP values, and compound classes previously observed in dialysate samples.20 Table 3 demonstrates that correlation-based (CAMERA and MSClean-R) and advanced chemometric methods (MCR-ALS) have poor classification precision and accuracy. The poor precision of these methods has been noted previously27,37,42,43 and can be attributed to large number of false positives incorporated into the target feature groups when Rs ≤ 0.3 (Figures S7 and S8). Furthermore, chemometric methods like MCR-ALS require advanced computing power, longer analysis times (~2 h for 65 compounds), and estimates of the number of overlapped analytes present in a chromatographic region.37,45 In contrast, LOF clustering exhibited the highest overall precision (91.3%), sensitivity (91.0%), and accuracy (90.2%) for grouping real metabolite features. Further, the analysis time for LOF clustering is on par with correlation-based methods (~5–10 min per-chromatogram).

Figure 7 summarizes the number of unique feature groups detected throughout our analysis of different chromatographic conditions: (A) 1× dialysate with a 20 min run, (B) 10× dialysate with a 20 min run, (C) 1× dialysate with a 60 min run, and (D) 10× dialysate with a 60 min run. Prior to blank filtering, the number of features for each chromatographic condition was roughly equivalent (12,849, 13,143, 13,631, and 13,950 in order). The “all” category in Figure 7 represents the number of features discovered in each data set after blank filtering. Note, the differences in the number of features discovered between the four data sets were due to variations in the blank filtering process. Our LOF clustering approach then went through all those features to identify degenerate and singlet features. Degenerate features were defined as features that matched at least one other feature, whereas singlets consisted of only a single unmatched feature.6 Since the LOF threshold was selected to minimize the number of false negatives detected (Table S2), singlets in this data set arose from low-abundance analytes and/or ions that were not selected during iterative MS/MS acquisition. Figure S9 provides some examples of these singlet features. Meanwhile, degeneracies contributed to 79–86% of the total number of features detected. Tentative identifications for these feature groups were made using MS/MS library matching. Each feature group was labeled either identified or unknown if the MS/MS spectrum for the metabolite did not match that of one in the libraries. Overall, Figure 7 demonstrates that the sample preconcentration and longer mobile phase gradients improved identification rates while also decreasing the number of singlets and unknown feature groups. Accordingly, under these conditions, the detectable metabolome for brain dialysate contains about 583 compounds and of those, 499 can be identified based on the retention time and MS/MS. Integrating the results from LOF clustering to in silico16,17 databases can further increase identification rates for the unknown feature groups and singlets. Ultimately, these results give a clearer view of what is “known,” compared to the original peak list containing thousands of features.

## CONCLUSION

The proposed LOF metric is a powerful method for analyzing feature degeneracy in untargeted metabolomics data sets. LOF clustering can be applied to MS1-level data collected directly or through DDA or data-independent acquisition (DIA) methods, in either positive or negative ionization. This method improves analyte discovery by accurately clustering degeneracies in a feature list, with a classification success rate of 90%, outperforming existing approaches. LOF clustering strengthened our interpretation of the detectable brain metabolome, reducing thousands of features to ~583 unique metabolites. Ultimately, the LOF metric poses some unique advantages over the established methods for identifying degeneracies. First, this method does not require a predefined list of known adduct relationships, allowing it to operate in an unsupervised fashion. For instance, this work showed that LOF clustering can be used to discover both in-source fragments and other novel degeneracies from adduction. Additionally, the LOF metric can also resolve degeneracies from analytes at Rs ≤ 0.3, overcoming challenges associated with correlation-based and advanced chemometric methods. Although not observed in our data, coeluting analytes may form adducts that do not chromatographically align with their parent species; however, our LOF method can be easily integrated with complementary annotation tools for these situations.29 While demonstrated successfully herein, future work is necessary to confirm LOF thresholds and workflows for DIA experiments. Overall, the LOF strategy can easily be incorporated into existing LC–MS processing software to enable deeper metabolomic interpretations and guide annotation efforts into these detectable unknowns.7,23,53
