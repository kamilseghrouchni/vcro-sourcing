# C9orf72 in myeloid cells prevents an inflammatory response to microbial glycogen

## Abstract

Gut dysbiosis and neural inflammation occur in patients with Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) including those with a causal mutation in Chromosome 9 Open Reading Frame 72 (C9ORF72). How gut commensals interact with common ALS genotypes to impart risk of neural degeneration remains unclear. Here we identify ten phylogenetically diverse bacterial strains that promote cytokine release in a C9orf72-dependent manner. Meta-transcriptomics implicated the glycogen biosynthesis pathway as a driver of inflammation. Colonization of germ-free C9orf72-deficient mice with Parabacteroides merdae that produced inflammatory glycogen enhanced monocytosis, blood-brain barrier breakdown, and T cell infiltration in the central nervous system. Enzymatic digestion of glycogen in the gut promoted survival of C9orf72-deficient mice and dampened microglial reactivity in the brain. A survey of human fecal samples demonstrated that inflammatory forms of glycogen were present in gut contents from 15/22 patients with ALS, 1/1 patient with C9ORF72-Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) and 4/12 healthy controls. Together, the results of this work identify bacterial glycogen as a modifiable mediator of immune homeostasis in the gut and brain.

## INTRODUCTION

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is a heterogeneous neurodegenerative disease characterized by loss of upper and lower motor neurons leading to progressive paralysis and death within 2-5 years, though some people with ALS survive more than 10 years beyond their initial diagnosis1–4. Up to 15% of patients with ALS also develop symptoms of FTD, a neurodegenerative disorder due to the loss of cortical neurons important for behavior, speech or language processing5. ALS and FTD are appreciated to reside on a clinical spectrum with shared pathomechanisms5. The age of ALS/FTD onset can range from the third to ninth decade of life and varies substantially between members of the same family that experience a high prevalence of these disorders3,5–8. Despite extensive efforts to retrospectively profile biofluids and brain imaging from individuals before an ALS/FTD diagnosis, no biomarkers reliably predict when a person will develop ALS/FTD9–12. Factors that trigger conversion to ALS/FTD remain enigmatic.

Whereas in most instances the etiology of ALS/FTD is unclear, a G4C2 repeat expansion on Chromosome 9 within the first intron of C9ORF72 is the most common genetic cause of ALS and FTD, accounting for 10.4%13 and 9.3%13,14 of cases, respectively. The causal repeat expansion is most often observed within one allele of C9ORF72, although intermediate repeat expansions within both C9ORF72 alleles are associated with elevated risk of ALS15,16. Loss and gain of function mechanisms of the C9ORF72 mutation are hypothesized to contribute to neurodegeneration. Surveys of brain and blood have demonstrated that the repeat expansion in C9ORF72 leads to a reduction of the gene product normally encoded by the C9ORF72 locus17–20. C9ORF72 functions as a GTPase activating protein that regulates endo-lysosomal trafficking, autophagy, ATP production, cytoskeleton dynamics, and neuronal excitability21. The repeat expansion is also transcribed and translated into toxic repetitive peptides that are cleared through C9ORF72-dependent mechanisms22–26. Intra-individual expansions and contractions of the C9ORF72 mutation27 may contribute to variable sites of disease onset, although there is conflicting evidence on whether the size of the repeat differs between generations and whether repeat size correlates with the age of onset or rate of disease progression8,20,28–30. The observation that the C9ORF72 mutation acts with incomplete penetrance7,8,25 indicates that additional genetic or environmental factors could have a meaningful impact on whether a mutation carrier will develop ALS or FTD.

Neural inflammation is a hallmark of ALS/FTD that can be broadly described as alterations to supportive glial cells, disruption of blood-brain barriers and infiltration of peripheral immune cells into the central nervous system (CNS)31–33. Inflammation of the brain can negatively impact neuronal function and survival due to the local production of cytokines such as Interleukin 1β (IL-1β), exposure to circulating toxins and antibodies, and a reduction of blood flow that limits the availability of essential nutrients and oxygen33,34. Glial reactivity in ALS CNS is greatest at sites of motor neuron degeneration and is most intense in patients with a rapid ALS disease course35–37. This supports the idea that glial cells respond directly to the loss of motor neurons and propagate the disease in a feed-forward manner. Neural inflammation may also represent a more generalized response to chronic stress in ALS, as evidenced by alterations in blood-brain barrier integrity38, microglial morphology35, metabolic activity36,39, and gene expression across the CNS40–42. Signs of systemic inflammation are also apparent in patients with a rapid ALS disease course, which includes elevated neutrophils and pro-inflammatory T cells in the circulation, fewer T regulatory cells in the periphery and CNS, and greater levels of acute phase proteins, cytokines and chemokines in plasma2,43–47. It remains unclear whether systemic inflammation precedes or follows neural inflammation in patients with ALS/FTD and whether they contribute to disease pathogenesis. Clarity may come from the identification of environmental stimuli that promote inflammation in people with ALS/FTD.

The human gastrointestinal tract is a prominent source of neuromodulatory factors and represents the largest site of microbial-host interactions, with an estimated 100 trillion microorganisms interfaced by 70-80% of the body’s immune cells48. Lifestyle, dietary habits, and exposure to antibiotics play a pivotal role in shaping the composition of the gut microbiome, which in turn exerts regulatory effects on host physiology that include intestinal barrier function, metabolic homeostasis and immune modulation49–51. Primary modes of communication are microbial-derived metabolites, neurotransmitters, and microbe-associated molecular patterns that are sensed and integrated by the innate immune system to coordinate commensal-dependent intestinal homeostasis52. Disruption to the microbe-immune-brain axis may contribute risk for neurodegeneration53–55. However, studies to address how the gut microbiome is altered in patients with ALS have not reached a consensus. One report showed no difference in the composition of bacterial species between patients with ALS and healthy controls56, whereas another study found a link between communities of oral and intestinal bacteria and the site of ALS onset57. Other studies have identified groups of patients with ALS that harbor lower levels of bacteria capable of producing short-chain fatty acids58 or the vitamin B3 derivative nicotinamide59. Notably, reconstitution with nicotinamide-producing bacteria or delivery of nicotinamide itself extended lifespan in an ALS mouse model59. However, without functional studies to test whether individual commensals or complex bacterial communities can elicit inflammatory features of ALS/FTD, the observed variation in ALS gut microbiomes is largely limited to correlative associations. Here, we used genetically sensitized macrophages, germ-free mice exposed to bacterial strains and microbial communities, single cell RNA sequencing of the CNS and metatranscriptomic sequencing of gut contents to identify commensal bacteria that enhance C9orf72-dependent systemic and neural inflammation via the production of glycogen.

## RESULTS

### C9orf72 deficient mice die prematurely at Case Western Reserve University.

Mice that lack C9orf72 (C9orf72−/−) develop neural inflammation and motor deficits and die prematurely when reared in environments that contain gut bacteria with high levels of innate-immune stimulating factors such as the Harvard University Biomedical Research Infrastructure (hereafter referred to as C9orf72(Harvard) mice)60. In contrast, neural inflammation and motor deficits are reduced and lifespan is extended when C9orf72−/− mice are reared in environments with low levels of innate-immune stimulating factors such as the Broad Institute (hereafter referred to as C9orf72(Broad) mice) or the Jackson Laboratory (hereafter referred to as C9orf72(Jax) mice)60,61. To study the effects of C9orf72 deficiency on the survival of mice at the Case Western Reserve University Animal Resource Center (hereafter referred to as C9orf72(CWRU) mice), we followed a cohort of C9orf72(CWRU)+/+(n=76, 37F:39M), C9orf72(CWRU)+/− (n=139, 61F:78M) and C9orf72(CWRU)−/− (n=83, 33F:50M) animals up to 500 days of age (Figure 1A and Figure S1A). We found that C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice died significantly faster than C9orf72(CWRU)+/+ littermates (p = 0.0122 Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon) (Figure 1A) with no difference in survival between males and females (Figure S1A). The causes of death experienced by C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice were among those commonly observed for C9orf72(Harvard)−/− mice60,62 and myeloid conditional mutant C9orf72(Harvard)2Lox/2Lox LysM-cre+ mice63 and related to their underlying autoinflammatory disorder.

### Identification of bacteria that elicit C9orf72-dependent cytokine production.

To quantify innate-immune stimulating factors within communities of gut microbes, we utilized an assay in which murine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) are exposed to heat-killed fecal matter preparations normalized for their microbial content and then measured levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine Tumor necrosis factor alpha (Tnfα) in culture supernatants. We focused on Tnfα because it is elevated in ALS patient plasma and cerebral spinal fluids64,65 and because we previously demonstrated that Tnfα was among a group of cytokines elevated in the plasma of C9orf72(Harvard)−/− mice relative to C9orf72(Broad)−/− mice and Tnfα was the earliest and most sensitive cytokine to be produced by C9orf72−/− BMDM in response to Toll-like receptor agonists or murine fecal preparations60. Using this approach, we found that fecal matter from C9orf72(CWRU) mice and C9orf72(Harvard) mice induced more Tnfα in macrophage supernatants relative to fecal matter from C9orf72(Broad) mice or C9orf72(Jax) mice (Figure 1B-C). In response to C9orf72(CWRU) and C9orf72(Harvard) feces, C9orf72−/− BMDM released more Tnfα than C9orf72+/+ BMDM (Figure 1C). In contrast, C9orf72+/+ and C9orf72−/−macrophages exposed to C9orf72(Jax) or C9orf72(Broad) fecal matter released a similar quantity of Tnfα (Figure 1C). These findings align with the established role that C9orf72 acts in myeloid cells to oppose the release of cytokines in response to certain pattern recognition receptor agonists60,61. Overall, these results indicate that gut bacteria from C9orf72(CWRU) mice contain high levels of innate immune stimulating factors.

We previously identified 41 bacterial species that were enriched in environments where C9orf72−/− mice died prematurely60. To determine whether these bacterial species harbored high levels of innate immune stimulating factors, we acquired the eponymous type-strains from culture collections and added each individually into gut contents to test cultured macrophage cytokine release (Figure 1B). Among the type strains that successfully grew under anaerobic conditions, 72% (13/18) were originally collected from humans and 28% (5/18) were obtained from animals (Table S1 and Figure S1B). When administered along with C9orf72(Jax) feces, ten bacterial strains elicited greater Tnfα release from C9orf72−/− BMDM relative to C9orf72+/+ controls, including Parabacteroides merdae (P. merdae), Prevotella buccalis (P. buccalis), Prevotella loescheii (P. loescheii), Prevotella stercorea (P. stercorea), Paraprevotella clara (P. clara), Bacteroides faecichinchillae (B. faecichinchillae), Parvibacter caecicola (P. caecicola), Hespellia stercorisuis (H. stercorisuis), Limosilactobacillus vaginalis (L. vaginalis) and Lactococcus garvieae (L. garvieae) (Figure 1D). The same ten strains elicited a stronger cytokine response when combined with C9orf72(Harvard) fecal matter relative to C9orf72(Harvard) fecal matter alone, although cytokine release was largely independent of C9orf72 genotype under these stimulatory conditions (Figure 1E and Figure S1C-D). To assess whether differences in cytokine release were associated with pyroptosis, a form of inflammatory cell death66 that results in leakage of cytoplasmic contents, we measured macrophage viability in culture but did not observe overt cell loss under any conditions (Figure S1E-F).

The observation that certain bacterial strains elicited less Tnfα when mixed with highly inflammatory fecal preparations (Figure 1E) raised the possibility that our assay could be used to identify microbes with immunosuppressive properties. Bacteroides intestinalis, Phocaeicola vulgatus and Staphylococcus saprophyticus were among the species enriched in environments where C9orf72−/− mice were protected from premature mortality60 that promoted less Tnfα release when co-administered with C9orf72(Harvard) fecal matter (Figure S1G-H).

To evaluate whether simple mixtures of bacteria could elicit C9orf72-dependent cytokine release, we added the bacterial cultures alone or in pairwise combinations to BMDM and found that P. merdae, P. buccalis, P. caecicola and P. stercorea tended to induce more Tnfα than P. loescheii, B. faecichinchillae or L. garvieae (Figure 1F, Figure S1I). In each case, C9orf72−/− BMDM released more Tnfα relative to C9orf72+/+ controls (Figure 1F, Figure S1I). These findings demonstrate that C9orf72 suppressed an innate immune response to a group of phylogenetically diverse bacterial species (Figure 1G).

### Glycogen synthesis is enriched in fecal bacteria from pro-inflammatory environments.

We next sought to distinguish molecular features of gut bacteria that are associated with the susceptibility of C9orf72−/− mice to fatal inflammation. We performed metatranscriptomic sequencing of fecal matter from two pro-survival environments (C9orf72(Jax) n=5; C9orf72(Broad) n=1) and two pro-inflammatory environments (C9orf72(CWRU) n=5; C9orf72(Harvard) n=5) (Figure 2A). Principal component analysis of the abundance of bacterial species readily separated samples from different environments into groups, with the largest principal component (PC1, 26.43% of variance) separating the samples from pro-inflammatory and pro-survival environments (p = 0.001; PERMANOVA; Figure 2B). A closer examination of this axis of variance revealed an enrichment of Prevotella species (Figure S2A) and a reduction of α-diversity in samples derived from the pro-inflammatory environments (Figure S2B). Next, we mapped microbial transcripts to genes and trained a machine-learning algorithm to identify features that distinguished the two groups. The algorithm classified fecal samples with extremely high fidelity, as demonstrated by an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.98 (Figure S2C). Exemplifying the considerable difference in gene expression programs between the groups, 18 metabolic pathways were significantly altered in their abundance when jointly comparing the two pro-inflammatory and two pro-survival environments (p < 0.05) (Figure 2C and Figure S2D-E). Carbohydrate storage (e.g. Glycogen biosynthesis I, Gluconeogenesis I, Sucrose biosynthesis II) and energy production (e.g. Pyruvate fermentation to Acetate and Lactate I/II, Myo- chiro- and scyllo-inositol degradation) distinguished bacterial communities from pro-inflammatory and pro-survival environments (Figure 2D).

### Bacterial glycogen promotes C9orf72-dependent cytokine release.

Enrichment of bacterial genes associated with glycogen biosynthesis led us to hypothesize that C9orf72 functions within macrophages to limit the inflammatory response to microbial glycogen. To determine whether human-derived P. merdae, P. buccalis, P. stercorea, P. loescheii, or P. caecicola produced pro-inflammatory forms of glycogen, we treated heat-killed bacterial cultures with vehicle or αAmylase to cleave the α-1,4-glycosidic bonds of glycogen and administered these preparations to macrophages to measure the exocytosis of Tnfα in culture supernatants (Figure 3A). In each case, vehicle-treated bacteria elicited more Tnfα from C9orf72−/− BMDM than C9orf72+/+ controls (Figure 3B), a finding consistent with our prior observation (Figure 1F). Treatment of P. merdae, P. buccalis, P. stercorea, and P. caecicola, but not P. loescheii, with αAmylase lead to a reduction of Tnfα in the supernatant of C9orf72−/− BMDM relative to vehicle-treated bacteria, whereas αAmylase and vehicle-treated bacteria induced the same amount of Tnfα from C9orf72+/+ macrophages (Figure 3B). Additional controls demonstrated that exposure of BMDM to αAmylase alone did not induce Tnfα, and αAmylase treatment of Pam3csk4, a synthetic TLR2 agonist with no α-1,4-glycosidic bonds, did not affect Tnfα release (Figure 3B). Loss of C9orf72 function sensitized macrophages to the inflammatory effects of glycogen.

Next, we used transmission electron microscopy to visualize how αAmylase affected the ultrastructure of P. buccalis and P. merdae. Outer membrane vesicles67 were readily detectable in cultures of P. buccalis and P. merdae but did not differ between vehicle and αAmylase treated conditions (Figure S3A-D). Notably, the intracellular density of P. buccalis (Figure 3C-D) and P. merdae (Figure 3E-F) was reduced upon treatment with αAmylase consistent with depletion of bacterial glycogen stores.

Escherichia coli (E. coli) is commonly associated with chronic inflammation in the context of inflammatory bowel disease68. We tested the inflammatory potential of E. coli K-12 in our cytokine release assay and found that Tnfα production was not reduced by αAmylase pre-treatment or inactivation of the E. coli GlgA, GlgB, or GlgC genes necessary for glycogen synthesis (Figure S4A-B). Therefore, E. coli K-12 did not produce inflammatory forms of glycogen under the growth conditions tested here.

To begin to address the mechanism by which inflammatory forms of glycogen are produced, we compared the effect of nutrient availability during bacterial culture. Supplementation of bacterial culture broth with Vitamin K or restriction of exogenous glucose led to a reduction in the amount of Tnfα released in response to P. merdae but not P. buccalis (Figure S5A-B). To explore how these microbes are sensed, we pre-treated C9orf72−/− BMDM with a small molecule TLR2 antagonist or a TLR2 neutralizing antibody and found that both interventions potently mitigated the amount of Tnfα elicited in response to P. buccalis and P. merdae (Figure S6A-D). Together, these findings suggest that P. buccalis and P. merdae are primarily recognized in a TLR2-dependent manner and that nutrient availability contributes to the regulation of strain-dependent TLR agonist production.

### Short glucan chain lengths characterize inflammatory forms of glycogen.

To determine whether the inflammatory potential of microbial glycogen correlated with differences in structural composition, we extracted glycogen from fecal samples derived from C9orf72(CWRU) or C9orf72(Jax) mice and treated these preparations with isoamylase to cleave the α-1,6 branch points and yield linear polymeric glucose chains. Glycogen extracted from C9orf72(CWRU) mouse liver served as a control. Next we conjugated the linearized glucose chains to a negatively charged fluorophore through reductive amination69 and separated them by gel electrophoresis to visualize the degree of polymerization (Figure 4A). Glycogen from C9orf72(CWRU) fecal matter had a higher proportion of short linear glucan moieties (one or two glucose units) when compared with C9orf72(Jax) feces or C9orf72(CWRU) liver (Figure 4B-C).

Next, we stained undigested glycogen-enriched fractions with molecular Iodine, which intercalates within the helical structure of α-1,4 chains and, when measured by optical absorbance, indicates the relative degree of polysaccharide branching70. The absorbance pattern of glycogen enriched from C9orf72(Jax) fecal matter resembled amylopectin, an infrequently α-1,6 branched α-1,4 glucan, whereas the absorbance pattern of glycogen derived from C9orf72(CWRU) feces or cultures of P. buccalis or P. merdae were more similar to the frequently α-1,6 branched liver glycogen (Figure 4D-F). Thus, inflammatory forms of microbial glycogen tended to have a higher density of α-1,6 branches and shorter α-1,4 linear glucan chains.

### P. merdae promotes C9orf72-dependent systemic and neural inflammation in mice.

To examine the phenotype of C9orf72−/− mice reared in the absence of microbial stimuli, we re-derived our C9orf72+/− mice germ-free (hereafter referred to as C9orf72(GF)) and established a colony through C9orf72(GF)+/− intercrosses. To produce an abundance of C9orf72(GF)−/− progeny for study, we attempted to pair C9orf72(GF)−/− mice, but the homozygous mutant dams were infertile, so we continued to cross C9orf72(GF)+/− dams with C9orf72(GF)−/− and C9orf72(GF)+/− sires. After a series of experiments in which we exposed C9orf72(GF)+/− mice to individual bacterial strains with or without C9orf72(Jax) feces, we focused on P. merdae for additional investigation because this microbe engrafted germ-free mice, whereas other human-derived strains did not (Figure S7A-C).

To learn whether P. merdae could modify the development of systemic and neural inflammation in relation to complex microbial communities, we weaned cohorts of C9orf72(GF) mice (C9orf72(GF)+/+ & +/-, n=25 (10M:15F); C9orf72(GF)−/−, n=44 (22M:22F)) and allowed them to remain germ-free or reconstituted them with either P. merdae alone (hereafter referred to as C9orf72(P. merdae→GF), C9orf72(Jax) fecal matter alone (hereafter referred to as C9orf72(Jax→GF)), C9orf72(Jax) feces supplemented with P. merdae (hereafter referred to as C9orf72(Jax+P.m.→GF)), or C9orf72(CWRU) feces alone (hereafter referred to as C9orf72(CWRU→GF)) and assessed inflammatory phenotypes in transcardially perfused animals after 3 months (Figure 5A). We quantified mouse immunoglobulin (IgG) in the brain as a measure of blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, pro-IL-1β cytokine in the brain as a measure of neural inflammation, and the abundance of innate and adaptive immune cells in the mesenteric lymph node (mLN), spleen, peripheral blood and spinal cord (Figure 5B-D, Figure S8A). Each data form was z-score normalized to enable statistical comparison between the treatment groups.

When compared with C9orf72(GF)+/+ & +/− control mice, C9orf72(GF)−/− mice developed minimal signs of neural and systemic inflammation as evidenced by only mildly elevated T helper and B cells in mLN and spleen (Figure 5B-E). In the presence of bacteria with low levels of innate immune stimulating factors, C9orf72(Jax→GF)−/− experienced circulating neutrophilia but did not develop other measures of systemic or neural inflammation relative to C9orf72(GF)−/− mice (Figure 5B-E). In the presence of bacteria with high levels of innate immune stimulating factors, C9orf72(CWRU→GF)−/− mice accumulated neutrophils in mLN, spleen, peripheral blood and spinal cord relative to C9orf72(GF)−/− mice and experienced the greatest IgG leakage (p < 0.02) and pro-IL-1β cytokine accumulation in the brain (p < 0.0001) relative to C9orf72(Jax→GF)−/− mice (Figure 5B-E). Loss of C9orf72 function primed mice to experience more BBB breakdown and neural inflammation that was influenced by the complex microbial constituency of the gut.

Monoassociation with P. merdae did not cause systemic or neural inflammatory phenotypes in C9orf72(P. merdae→GF)+/+ & +/− mice (Figure 5B-E). In contrast, C9orf72(P. merdae→GF)−/− animals developed a suite of inflammatory phenotypes that included neutrophil accumulation in mLN, spleen and peripheral blood and elevated IgG in the CNS (Figure 5B-E). When P. merdae was competitively engrafted, C9orf72(Jax+P.m.→GF)−/− mice developed a severe inflammatory condition characterized by splenomegaly, monocytosis, anemia, enrichment of T cells in the spinal cord, and IgG leakage in the brain (Figure 5B-E). With C9orf72 functionally intact, C9orf72(Jax+P.m.→GF)+/+ mice displayed enrichment of T cells in the spinal cord without other indicators of neural inflammation and were largely protected from systemic inflammatory disease (Figure 5B-E).

Sequencing gut contents of C9orf72(Jax+P.m.→GF) mice confirmed the stable engraftment of P. merdae (Figure S9A). Principal component analysis revealed a global change in the microbial composition of the gut associated with P. merdae engraftment, characterized by enrichment of Lachnospiraceae and Muribaculaceae, whereas the presence of P. merdae did not affect alpha diversity (Figure S9B-C). Thus, P. merdae likely influenced C9orf72-dependent systemic and neural inflammatory phenotypes through direct interaction with the immune system as well as indirect alteration of gut community structure.

### Bacterial glycogen accumulates in C9orf72-/- lamina propria myeloid cells.

To examine the abundance of polysaccharides in the gut of C9orf72(CWRU) mice, we incubated sections of the terminal small intestine with Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), a histologic stain commonly used to detect glycogen, glycoproteins, glycolipids and mucins in tissues. Staining revealed an accumulation of PAS+ cells within the lamina propria of C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice that were largely absent in C9orf72(CWRU)+/+ controls (Figure S10A-H). Given the localization of polysaccharide enriched cells in the lamina propria, which is known to harbor a high density of dendritic cells and macrophages71, we stained the lamina propria of mice with conditional deletion of C9orf72 in the myeloid lineage63 and found that the number of PAS+ cells was enriched in C9orf72(CWRU)2Lox/2Lox LysM-cre+ mice relative to controls (Figure S10B, G) despite a more limited accumulation of PAS+ signal per cell relative to C9orf72(CWRU)−/− animals (Figure S10H). Moreover, when we profiled the gut of germ-free animals, the number of PAS+ cells per lamina propria in C9orf72(GF)−/− mice was significantly reduced relative to C9orf72(CWRU)−/− gut (p=0.0186, Figure S10C, G) that was recovered in C9orf72(Jax→GF)−/−, C9orf72(Jax+P.m.→GF)−/− and C9orf72(P. merdae→GF)−/− mice (Figure S10D-G).

To inform which polysaccharides had accumulated in the gut of C9orf72−/− mice, we treated terminal ileum sections with αAmylase before PAS staining and observed a reduction of PAS+ cells in the lamina propria of C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice and C9orf72(CWRU)2Lox/2Lox LysM-cre+ mice (Figure S11A-B). The PAS+ signal in C9orf72(GF)−/− mice was largely resistant to αAmylase (Figure S11C), suggesting that the PAS signal may represent poorly-branched polyglucosan72 in these microbe deficient mice.

To address whether polysaccharide enrichment in macrophages would manifest under sterile inflammatory conditions or whether glycogen would accumulate in response to microbes that produce it, we exposed BMDM to Pam3csk4 or heat-killed P. buccalis or P. merdae and stained the cultures with PAS. Exposure to P. buccalis or P. merdae, but not sterile TLR2 stimulation with Pam3csk4 caused C9orf72−/− BMDM to accumulate αAmylase-sensitive PAS+ signal (Figure S12A-B). The PAS+ signal did not appreciably accumulate in C9orf72+/+ BMDM under any condition (Figure S12A-B). Together, these data indicate that C9orf72 functions in the myeloid lineage to oppose the build-up of polysaccharide derived from the diet and the microbiota.

### Oral αAmylase mitigates systemic and neural inflammation in C9orf72-/- mice.

To determine whether enzymatic cleavage of glycogen in the gut would ameliorate inflammatory phenotypes after their establishment, we produced a cohort of C9orf72(CWRU) mice ((C9orf72(CWRU)+/+, n=6 (3M:3F); C9orf72(CWRU)−/−, n=28 (13M:15F); 6 month old) and demonstrated that the mutant animals performed poorly on the accelerating rotarod (Figure 6A-C and Figure S13A-D), had elevated levels of circulating neutrophils (Figure S13E-F) and monocytes (Figure S13G-H), and displayed mild anemia (Figure S13I-J) compared to controls. Then, we began daily administration of αAmylase by oral gavage and monitored associated phenotypes for 10 weeks. Treatment with αAmylase significantly improved the overall survival of C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice (7/7 males survived; 7/7 females survived) compared with vehicle-treated C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice (3/6 males survived p = 0.0404; 7/8 females survived p = 0.3496; male and female combined p = 0.0343) (Figure 6B and Figure S13A-B).

Next, we investigated whether the extension of lifespan upon αAmylase treatment was associated with an improvement of organismal health measures. Over the course of the study, αAmylase treatment did not affect the motor performance of C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice (Figure 6C and Figure S12C-D) and did not ameliorate phenotypes in peripheral blood (Figure S12E-J). However, male and female αAmylase treated C9orf72(CWRU)−/− animals harbored smaller spleens relative to vehicle-treated C9orf72(CWRU)-/- mice (Figure 6D-E and Figure S12K).

To probe the extent of neural inflammation and BBB breakdown in this cohort, we first measured pro-IL-1β and mouse IgG and found that both analytes were elevated in the brain cortex of vehicle-treated C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice (Figure S14A-F). Treatment with αAmylase reduced pro-IL1β and mouse IgG to a greater extent in the brains of male C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice (Figure S14D-E) and levels of pro-IL1β and mouse IgG were positively correlated in C9orf72(CWRU)−/− brain lysates regardless of sex or treatment status (R2 0.5269; p < 0.001) (Figure S14F).

Considering that glial reactivity associates with a more rapid rate of disease progression in patients with ALS35,37, we next explored the effect of oral αAmylase on glial cell expression programs by single cell RNA sequencing. We generated libraries from the brain cortex of 16 mice (4 C9orf72(CWRU)+/+ (2M:2F); 5 C9orf72(CWRU)−/− vehicle treated (3F:2M); 7 C9orf72(CWRU)−/− αAmylase-treated (3F:4M)) and recovered 138,771 intact transcriptomes with a mean of 16,269 genes per library and 2,421 unique molecular identifiers per cell (Figure S15A-B). We used Seurat73 to annotate cell types according to canonical markers for glutamatergic neurons, GABAergic neurons, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, astrocytes, pericytes, endothelial cells, vascular leptomeningeal cells, and microglia and clustered the populations by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (Figure 6G, Figure S15C). For this study, we focused our differential gene and pathway analysis on Hexb+ C1qa+ microglia.

Compared with C9orf72(CWRU)+/+ microglia, vehicle-treated C9orf72(CWRU)−/− showed enrichment genes and pathways associated with “Neutrophil aggregation”, “Positive regulation of blood coagulation”, and “Cellular oxidant detoxification” (Figure 6G). Microglia from αAmylase-treated C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice were depleted for iron binding genes Lcn2 and Ltf, the chemoattractant genes S100a8/9, and the anti-microbial genes Camp and Ngp relative to vehicle-treated C9orf72(CWRU)−/− microglia (Figure 6I). Accordingly, microglia from C9orf72(CWRU)−/− αAmylase-treated mice displayed a reduction in Gene Ontology signatures related to “Leukocyte cell-cell adhesion”, “Myeloid leukocyte migration” and “Defense response to other organism” (Figure 6J). Together, this confirms that targeting glycogen in the gut ameliorated a C9orf72-dependent microglial inflammatory gene signature.

Sex-dependent differences in microglial gene expression programs were also apparent. Vehicle-treated male C9orf72(CWRU)−/− microglia were enriched for genes related to “Leukocyte cell-cell adhesion”, whereas female C9orf72(CWRU)−/− microglia were enriched for genes associated with “Synapse pruning” (Figure S16A-C), a pathway known to be regulated by C9orf72 in the context of amyloidosis74. Upon αAmylase-treatment, female C9orf72(CWRU)−/− microglia were enriched for “Proton motive force-driven mitochondrial ATP synthesis” (Figure S16D-E), which aligns with the reported function of C9ORF72 to promote energy homeostasis through stabilization of mitochondrial complex I assembly75. Microglia from male αAmylase-treated C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice were enriched for “Small GTPase-mediated signaling” (Figure S16F). This observation is consistent with the known function of C9ORF72 as a GTPase-activating protein for small GTPases76,77.

### Gut microbes and metabolites correlate with neuroinflammation in C9orf72-/- mice.

To explore mechanisms by which oral αAmylase treatment altered microglial reactivity, we began by profiling the composition of gut. Before initiation of treatment, the community structure, but not the α-diversity differed between C9orf72(CWRU)−/− and C9orf72(CWRU)+/+ mice (Figure S17A-B). Although sex-dependent changes in bacterial abundance were noted, neither vehicle-treatment nor αAmylase treatment was sufficient to ameliorate the difference in gut bacterial constituency between C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice and C9orf72(CWRU)+/+ controls (Figure S17C-F).

We next examined whether αAmylase-treatment altered the levels of microbial-derived metabolites in circulation using targeted high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Propionic acid was enriched in vehicle-treated C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice relative to C9orf72(CWRU)+/+ mice, and propionic acid was reduced in αAmylase-treated female C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice relative to αAmylase treated males (Figure S18A-B). Although there was no difference in circulating levels of lactic acid, succinic acid, acetic acid, butyric acid or isovaleric acid between treatment groups or genotypes (Figure S18C-J), we found a positive correlation between lactic acid and propionic acid and a negative correlation between these circulating metabolites and levels of pro-IL-1β and IgG in the brains of C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice. Propionic acid and lactic acid levels were enriched in C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice with highest levels of pro-IL-1βhi and IgG in the brain (Figure S19A-B). Moreover, we identified specific operational taxonomic units (OTUs) within the gut of C9orf72(CWRU)−/− mice whose abundance correlated with metabolites and measures of systemic and neural inflammation (Figure S20A-H). Together, the findings suggest that targeting glycogen in the gut altered immunomodulatory factors in the circulation to mitigate microglial reactivity.

### Inflammatory forms of glycogen exist in the human gut.

To determine whether inflammatory forms of glycogen are present in the gut of humans including those who suffer with ALS or FTD, we obtained frozen fecal matter from 35 individuals that resided either in the United States of America (USA), which included 3 healthy controls and 6 patients with ALS, or Italy, which included 9 healthy controls, 1 patient with FTD and 16 patients with ALS. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the two cohorts can be found in Table S3. Fecal samples were thoroughly washed, centrifuged at high speed to pellet bacteria, heat killed, pre-treated with vehicle or αAmylase to degrade glycogen and applied to BMDM so that Tnfα could be measured in culture supernatant (Figure 7A). Exposure to αAmylase was not toxic to macrophages and did not suppress macrophage responsiveness to Pam3csk4 (Figure 7B and Figure S21A-B), indicating that αAmylase did not induce a broadly suppressive effect on cell function.

Overall, 20/35 fecal samples harbored inflammatory forms of glycogen, as indicated by αAmylase-dependent reduction in the amount of Tnfα released from macrophages relative to vehicle pre-treatment (Figure 7B). Samples with inflammatory glycogen were derived from both females (10/14; 71%) and males (10/21; 48%) and included samples derived from donors in the USA (4/9; 44%) and Italy (16/26; 62%) (Figure 7B). When stratified by disease status, donor samples with inflammatory glycogen included 11/17 patients with sporadic ALS (65%), 3/4 patients with C9ORF72-ALS (75%), 1/1 patient with C9ORF72-FTD, 1/1 patient with SOD1-ALS, and 4/12 healthy controls (33%) (Figure 7B). When the mean activity of samples was assessed in aggregate, we found that αAmylase-sensitive inflammatory glycogen activity was significantly enriched in fecal samples from patients with ALS/FTD (p = 0.01), but not in feces from healthy controls (p = 0.82) (Figure 7C). Although the presence or absence of inflammatory glycogen activity did not distinguish fast and slow ALS progressors as a group, we found that inflammatory forms of glycogen were present in fecal matter from the two patients with the fastest rate of ALS-related motor decline (Figure S21C). Moreover, inflammatory glycogen was present in 8/9 fecal samples that were collected from patients within the first 18 months of their ALS disease course (Figure S21D).

## DISCUSSION

Here we demonstrate that individual bacterial strains and complex microbial communities can be stratified based on their potential to induce cytokine release from myeloid cells, in part due to differential production of immunostimulatory forms of glycogen. Our studies provide a mechanistic link by which divergent gut microbial communities govern the lifespan of C9orf72 loss of function mice reared in geographically diverse environments60. We establish C9orf72 as an important regulator of polysaccharide clearance in the gut and identify microbial glycogen as a therapeutic target to potentially mitigate risk of systemic and neural inflammation. Our demonstration that microbes that accumulate inflammatory forms of glycogen are enriched in the gut of ALS patients suggests that microbial glycogen may be an important example among many environmental and lifestyle factors that interact with predisposing genotypes to contribute risk of ALS onset and progression.

The integrated response of myeloid cells to diverse microbes is dictated, in part, by recognition of foreign moieties that are sensed by pattern recognition receptors at the cell surface and within endo-lysosomes78. Myeloid cells process these diverse signals and release cytokines that orchestrate adaptive immune responses which are tailored to resolve the unique challenge and to re-establish tissue homeostasis79. Here we showed that when myeloid cells experience a reduction of C9orf72 function, as occurs in humans with a C9ORF72 mutation, the integration of microbial signals is perturbed such that normally benign commensals elicit excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Over time, inundation with circulating cytokines2,80 may predispose C9ORF72 mutation carriers to neural inflammation that enhances their risk of neurodegeneration.

Why do commensal bacteria accumulate glycogen that elicits an innate immune response? Unlike mammalian glycogen, which tends to be of uniform molecular weight (107-108), microbial glycogen varies substantially in size (106-109) and compactness81,82. Small, compact forms of glycogen have higher thermodynamic stability and persist during periods of stress and starvation, which provides a competitive advantage that supports long term bacterial survival83. Because compact, highly branched forms of microbial glycogen preferentially activate TLR2 on myeloid cells81 and are processed more slowly in the lysosome, persistent pattern recognition receptor signaling may occur in response to delayed amelioration of this inflammatory stimulus. The effect of persistent signaling may be enhanced if glycogen acts as a scaffold to sequester other immunostimulatory moieties within the lysosome. Given that the C9orf72-SMCR8-WDR41 complex exhibits GTPase activating protein activity84,85, the accumulation of glycogen within C9orf72-deficient myeloid cells may be a consequence of inefficient vesicle-mediated trafficking of glycogen to the lysosome or reduced lysosomal degradative capacity. The fact that we observe a reduction, but not complete absence, of PAS signal in lamina propria cells from C9orf72(GF)−/− mice indicates that a proportion of the accumulated polysaccharide in these cells was derived from non-microbial sources (i.e. diet). However, the relative accumulation of PAS signal in lamina propria cells did not explain the difference in organismal susceptibility we observed when mutant animals were reconstituted with microbes from different vivarium. Rather, the inflammatory potential of microbial communities correlated with the unique structure of glycogen that was produced. As other ALS/FTD risk genes such as TBK1, OPTN, SQSTM1, GRN, VCP have established roles in regulation of lysosome biogenesis and function86, aberrant immune response to microbial glycogen may be a convergent pathomechanism across familial forms of ALS/FTD.

Global changes in metabolism are a prominent feature in ALS patients that may underlie susceptibility to neural inflammation. Positron emission tomography has revealed chronic reduction of glucose utilization in motor cortex and enhanced glucose uptake in the cerebellum, hippocampus, and brainstem of ALS patients39. At autopsy, ALS spinal cord sections exhibit pathologic accumulation of glycogen87, which could be related to enhanced glycogen synthesis, impaired glycogenolysis or trafficking of microbial glycogen to the CNS. Glycogen is normally enriched in astrocytes88 where it provides a rapid energy reserve that supports activity-dependent neuronal metabolism through the astrocyte neuronal lactate shuttle89. Under conditions of blood-brain barrier breakdown and hypoxia, astrocytes sequester glycogen and the facilitation of glycogenolysis can improve neuronal survival following stroke90. Astrocytes derived from sporadic ALS and C9ORF72 ALS patients exhibit reduced metabolic flexibility that is correlated with impaired ability to degrade glycogen91.

Considering these observations, our findings raise the possibility that myeloid activation in response to microbial-derived glycogen may induce a prolonged stress response in neurons that necessitates additional astrocytic metabolic support normally mediated through breakdown of glycogen. The pathologic accumulation of glycogen in astrocytes might exacerbate lysosomal stress and metabolic energy insufficiency in motor neurons. In this scenario, αAmylase treatment could indirectly aid the CNS by preventing microbes from sequestering glucose in the form of glycogen, thereby increasing the pool of monosaccharides available to the host. Notably, a high sugar diet was protective in a Drosophila TDP-43 model of motor neuron disease by promoting glycolytic flux92. Moreover, mice that expressed the C9ORF72 repeat expansion by a bacterial artificial transgene experienced reduced glycolytic flux that promoted motor deficits via enhanced translation of toxic gain of function dipeptide repeat proteins93. The release of simple sugars by αAmylase may also benefit benign gut commensals that are normally at a competitive disadvantage due to the inability to produce glycogen94, thereby reducing the overall burden of proinflammatory microbes in the gut95. Our single cell RNA sequencing analysis demonstrated that a primary benefit of targeting glycogen in the gut was the reorientation of microglia away from preoccupation with circulating microbial moieties. The breadth and richness of this single cell sequencing dataset will provide a valuable resource to identify additional genotype-, environment- and sex-dependent modifiers of brain health and to inform the cell-type-specific functions of C9orf72.

How microbes participate in ALS pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. The study by Nicholson58 and colleagues identified enrichment of Prevotella copri (P. copri) in ALS stool compared with healthy controls, whereas another group found global reduction of Prevotella species (spp.) in ALS patient stool relative to spousal controls96. P. copri can dominate the human oral gastrointestinal tract97 and its presence is associated with an enhanced risk of new-onset Rheumatoid arthritis98. Four clades of P. copri have been identified and stratified based on their carbohydrate utilization profile99,100. Prevotella spp. enhance inflammation at mucosal surfaces in a TLR2- and T helper type 17-dependent manner101–103, are among the most highly IgA-coated microbes in the gut104 and can accumulate up to 60% of their body mass as glycogen105. Our demonstration that Prevotella spp. produce immunostimulatory forms of glycogen may explain why this genus is preferentially targeted by the innate and adaptive immune system. Conversely, microbes such as Bacteroides intestinalis, Phocaeicola vulgatus and Staphylococcus saprophyticus that diminish cytokine release in the presence of immunostimulatory microbial communities could be leveraged as probiotics to chronically dampen environmental triggers of inflammation.

Re-derivation of our C9orf72 loss of function mice in a germ-free setting proved that gut microbes and their derivatives are important modifiers of systemic and neural inflammation in this model. The most severe neural inflammation and breakdown of blood brain barriers occurred when C9orf72 mutant animals were reconstituted with fecal matter from the CWRU animal facility, which correlated with enhanced neutrophilia in the periphery and spinal cord – a phenotype associated with poor survival in patients with ALS106 that can be mitigated by neutralization of Interleukin-17A84. By introducing P. merdae into mice alone or in combination with a relatively innocuous commensal microbiota, we identify P. merdae as microbe with the potential to modify the balance of monocytes and neutrophils in the periphery and to enhance neural inflammation in a C9orf72-dependent manner. Indeed, we found that P. merdae was associated with more severe neural inflammatory phenotypes in the context of C9orf72 loss of function and that gut microbial communities differed between C9orf72 mutant mice and controls. Together, these findings further the concept that genotypes which impart risk of neurological disease may causally alter the composition of the gut and sensitize individuals to harmful gene-by-microbe interactions59,107–109. Intriguingly, P. merdae can protect Apoe-deficient mice fed a high fat diet from developing cardiovascular disease and atherosclerosis via cleavage of branched-chain amino acids110. P. merdae was associated with neural protection in a mouse model of epilepsy that is rescued by a high fat/low carbohydrate ketogenic diet by opposing gamma-glutamylation of amino acids thereby leading to enrichment of the neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutryic acid (GABA) in the brain111. Dietary intake and the underlying host genotype are likely critical modifiers that dictate whether individual commensals contribute to neural inflammation and degeneration.

In summary, we demonstrate that the production of immunostimulatory glycogen is a conserved feature of phylogenetically diverse bacteria that colonize the gut of humans and mice and identify microbial glycogen as a modifiable environmental risk factor that shapes immune homeostasis in the periphery and CNS. Our study provides a rationale to test whether accumulation of inflammatory glycogen in the gut coincides with conversion of C9ORF72 mutation carriers from health to motor neuron disease.

### Limitations of the study

We used enzymatic digestion of heat-killed bacteria to identify microbial glycogen as an important mediator of C9orf72-dependent inflammation. The identification of bacteria that elicited C9orf72-dependent cytokine release without production of inflammatory glycogen suggests the presence of additional microbial moieties that participate in the exaggerated immune response that manifests upon C9orf72 loss of function. It is also possible that inflammatory effects of bacterial strains may have been masked by the common media and anaerobic culture conditions that we applied to grow them. Although our fecal reconstitution experiments provided a framework to interrogate the inflammatory potential of commensal communities and strains, targeted alterations are needed to determine which constituents of the human gut are most important to elicit harmful neural inflammatory outcomes. Moreover, additional genetic studies are needed to uncover the bacterial enzymes responsible for production of immunostimulatory glycogen. We anticipate that compact, immunogenic forms of glycogen are produced upon coordinated expression and activity of glycogen branching and phosphorylase pruning enzymes within commensals and across microbial communities. The identification of regulatory elements of the glycogen synthesis pathway may uncover rate-limiting nodes of glucose sequestration in the gut. Moreover, perturbation of growth conditions to alter bacterial metabolic profiles may inform why some commensals adopt a pro-inflammatory state during nutrient restriction or oxidative stress. Lastly, much larger studies are needed to determine the prevalence of inflammatory forms of microbial glycogen across human populations with unique diets and divergent ancestries including those with risk alleles that predispose to ALS/FTD.

## STAR★METHODS

### EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

#### Animals

All experimental procedures involving mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Case Western Reserve University. The generation and rederivation of C9orf72−/− mice and C9orf722Lox mice established at CWRU WRB was previously described112. Mice were housed with nestlet bedding with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and provided ad libitum water and food (Envigo Mouse/Rat Diet). LysM-cre mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (#004781). Mice were rederived germ-free by Taconic Bioscience by aseptic transplantation of C9orf72+/− embryos into psuedopregnant germ-free mothers. Resultant embryos were transferred to the Germ-free Mouse Modeling core facility at Case Western Reserve and maintained in sterile isolators or nested housing113. For mice enrolled in the αAmylase study, αAmylase (MilliporeSigma A3176) was prepared in a biosafety hood under sterile conditions by dissolving the enzyme to 50mg/mL in sterile PBS. Mice were then orally gavaged daily with either αAmylase or vehicle so that each mouse received 500mg of αAmylase enzyme per 1kg of body weight. Sex stratified data are included in associated supplemental figures.

#### Human fecal samples

Fresh frozen fecal samples from healthy individuals or ALS patients were obtained by the Neurological Clinical Research Institute at Massachusetts General Hospital or the Azienda Ospedaliero Universitaria Modena ALS Centre with approval by the respective human ethics committees with informed consent obtained from each subject prior to participation. Individuals could participate in-person or remotely. Participants must be over 18 years at time of consent and are capable of complying with study procedures. ALS participants must have a diagnosis of familial or sporadic ALS or individuals with known gene mutations associated with ALS (regardless of clinical phenotype) per self-report. Control subjects are those with no diagnosis of ALS. Donor demographics can be found in Table S3.

#### Bacteria culture

Commercially available bacterial strains were received as lyophilized pellets and reconstituted with Brain Heart Infusion media (FisherScientific DF0037-17-8). Once reconstituted, bacteria were then immediately streaked onto sheep blood agar plates (Hardy Diagnostics A10) and placed in an anerobic chamber (10% Hydrogen, 80% Nitrogen, 10% CO2). Single colonies were picked and then transferred to stationary liquid Brain Heart Infusion cultures in anaerobic conditions for 3-7 days.

#### Macrophage primary cell culture

Femur and tibia bones from C9orf72+/+ or C9orf72−/− mice were dissected and washed in sterile PBS and any remaining tissue was removed. BMDM media containing IMDM (Gibco 12440-053) was supplemented with 10% FBS (Bio-Techne S11550), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco 15070063), 1% Glutamax (Gibco 35050061), and 1% MEM NEAA (Gibco 11140050), and M-CSF (PeproTech AF-315-02) at 20ng/mL was then flushed through the bones onto a 10cm tissue culture dish (ThermoFisher 130182) and placed in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 and media changed every 2-3 days. After 7 days of culture, cells were then replated in 96 well format at 10,000 cells per well for experimentation.

### METHOD DETAILS

#### Genotyping and bacteria PCR

Mouse tails were lysed in digest buffer (Viagen 102-T) supplemented with proteinase K (Viagen 502-PK) for 12 hours at 55°C. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was then performed using AmpliTaq Gold DNA Polymerases (ThermoFisher Scientific 4398901). C9orf72 and LysM-cre was amplified using the following PCR conditions: 95°C 3 mins, (95°C 30s, 58°C 30s, 72°C 45s, for 40 cycles), 72°C 5 mins, then 4°C. C9orf722Lox was amplified using the following PCR conditions: 95°C 2 mins, (95°C 30s, 62°C 30s, 72°C 3 mins, for 40 cycles), 72°C 5 mins, then 4°C. Fecal DNA or DNA from bacteria cultures was isolated using QIAmp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen 51804) and quantified using Nanodrop. 10ng of isolated DNA was used as input for each PCR reaction. Total eubacteria was amplified using the following PCR conditions: 94°C 4 mins, (94°C 30s, 53°C 1 min, 72°C 1.25 mins, for 35 cycles), 72°C 10 mins, then 4°C. All other bacteria DNA were amplified using the following PCR conditions: 94°C 4 mins, (94°C 30s, 53°C 1 min, 72°C 1.25 mins, for 25 cycles), 72°C 10 mins, then 4°C. See Table S2 for specific primers for each bacteria strain.

#### Macrophage stimulation and cytokine release analysis

Fecal samples or pelleted bacteria from cultures were thawed from −80°C, resuspended in sterile PBS and then strained through a 40uM filter. Strained material was spun at 10,000xg for 5 minutes and supernatants discarded. Bacterial pellets were then resuspended in sterile PBS and normalized by optical density at 600nm wavelength (OD600) to OD600 = .1 (DiluPhotometer™ with Implen’s DiluCell™ 10). Samples were then heat killed at 90°C for 30 minutes. For bacteria that received αAmylase treatment, bacteria were mixed with αAmylase (MilliporeSigma A3176) dissolved in PBS at 5 mg/mL and incubated for 20 minutes at 37°C and deactivated at 95°C for 10 minutes. Heat killed fractions were diluted 1:25 or 1:125 in sterile PBS and added to macrophage cultures in triplicate. After 24 hours, culture media was collected and stored at −80°C for downstream cytokine analysis. To assess cell viability, macrophages were incubated with Thiazolyl Blue Tetrazolium Bromide (MTT) at 0.5mg/mL for 2 hours at 37°C. After incubation media was removed and 50ul of Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to wells and placed on rocker at room temperature for 5 minutes. Well optical density at 560nm was then calculated using Tecan Infinite M1000 Pro plate reader and then normalized to unstimulated control cells.

Tnfα concentrations in supernatants were analyzed via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (Biolegend 430916) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 96 well plates (ThermoScientific 442404) were coated with capture antibody and then incubated at 4°C overnight. Supernatants from BMDM were thawed on ice and then diluted 3-fold in 1X assay diluent. Coated plates were washed and then supernatants were added to the coated plate and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. After washing detection antibody was then added and plates were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. After another wash Avidin-HRP solution was added and plates were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. After the final wash TMB substrate solution was added and plates were incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes. Plates were then analyzed by Tecan Infinite M1000 Pro plate reader at optical density 570nm. Multiplex cytokine analysis was performed on BMDM supernatants using BD Cytometric Bead Array (CBA) Mouse Inflammation kit (BD 552364) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, supernatants were thawed on ice and then mixed with capture beads and PE detection reagent and incubated for 2 hours at RT. Samples were then washed and then acquired using the Attune NxT flow cytometer. Data was then analyzed in FlowJo.

#### Tissue dissociation for flow cytometry

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane followed by transcardial perfusion with 1X Hank’s balanced salt solution (Gibco 14185-052) supplemented with 10U/mL heparin (MilliporeSigma H3149). Tissue was dissociated as previously described112. In brief, spleens and mesenteric lymph nodes (mLN) were manually mashed through a 100uM strainer and then further filtered through a 40uM strainer. Spleen samples were then subjected to RBC lysis buffer (eBioscience 00-4300-54) for 10 minutes. Spinal cords were transferred into papain and DNase diluted in EBSS (Worthington LK003150) and manually broken apart using a pipette. Spinal cord cells were then strained through a 40uM filter, centrifuged at 300xG, and then resuspended in isotonic Percoll Plus (Cytiva 17544501) diluted to 30% in autoMACS running buffer and centrifuged at 300xg for 15 minutes with no brake. Floating myelin was then removed using a plastic transfer pipette. Cells from all tissues were then washed in autoMACS running buffer (Miltenyi 130-091-221). Antibodies were then prepared in autoMACS running buffer and cells stained in the dark for 30 minutes, then washed and centrifuged at 300xG and resuspended in 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences 15714-S). For each tissue, 1,000,000 cells were stained in a total volume of 50ul. Antibodies for flow cytometry and their dilutions are listed in key resources table. Samples were acquired on an Attune NxT or LSRII flow cytometer and data was analyzed in FlowJo.

#### Western blot

Mouse cortex tissue was harvested and immediately homogenized in 250 uL lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl 1.0% Triton X-100, 50 mM Tris pH 8.0 + cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail tablets Roche) followed by centrifugation at 20,000 rpm for 20 minutes. Supernatant was transferred to a new tube and total protein concentration was calculating using BCA protein assay kit (ThermoScientific 23225). 30ug of protein was loaded into each well of a 10% acrylamide gel and gel electrophoresis was conducted using the PowerPac™ Basic (Biorad). The Trans-Blot Turbo Machine (Biorad) was used to transfer the protein on to PVDF membrane. After blocking, primary and secondary antibody blotting was completed using the iBind Flex System (ThermoFischerScientific). Primary antibodies include Pro-IL-1β [3A6] (Cell Signaling 12242S), GAPDH [D4C6R] (Cell Signaling 97166S), and C9orf72 [GT779] (GeneTex GTX632041). The secondary antibody used was Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (Southern Biotech 1030-04). After blotting, AttoPhos® Substrate (Promega S1000) was applied to the PVDF membrane and then dried. Imaging was completed using the ImageQuant™ LAS 4000 biomolecular imager.

#### Gut tissue processing and Periodic Acid Schiff staining

Mouse small intestine was harvested and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C. The next day, samples were washed with 1X PBS and submerged in a 1:1 mixture of PBS and OCT (FisherScientific 4585) overnight. Regions of the mouse ileum were mounted in OCT and cryostat-sectioned at 4μm. Tissue sections were hydrated in water and stained with Alcian Blue pH 2.5 for 30 minutes at room temperature. Sections were then washed in tap water and oxidized in 0.5% periodic acid solution for 5 minutes. Sections were then rinsed in distilled water, placed in Schiff reagent for 15 minutes, and then washed in lukewarm tap water for 10 minutes. Sections were then counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 1 minute and washed in tap water for 5 minutes. Sections were dehydrated in anhydrous ethanol, cleared with xylene, and cover slipped with a xylene-based mounting medium (Epredia 8312-4). Images were taken at 10X in brightfield on the Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope and PAS+ cells were quantified using GA3 (Nikon).

#### Motor function

Mice were trained on a Ugo Basile Rota-Rod (Stoelting 57624) at a constant speed of 5rpm for 300 seconds at least one day before assessment. For performance trials, the rotarod accelerated from 5rpm – 40rpm over 300 seconds. Each trial consisted of 3 tests per mouse, with each test separated by at least 20 minutes. Operator was blinded to animal genotype during trials.

#### Blood measures

Peripheral blood was collected via mandible puncture directly into EDTA-coated tubes (BD 365974). Blood counts were assessed using a Hemavet (Abaxis). Samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 minute to pellet cells and plasma was collected from the supernatants.

#### Feces 16s rRNA amplicon sequencing

Fecal DNA was isolated using QIAmp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen 51804) and quantified using Nanodrop. DNA concentration was standardized and amplified using 16s rRNA primers spanning the V3 and V4 regions (Illumina). The resulting amplified PCR products were analyzed on a Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), then purified and amplified with primers containing unique sample nucleotide barcodes (Illumina). Following the sequencing run, the sequence data were demultiplexed based on the nucleotide bar code, and then compared to the SILVA version 138.1 16s rRNA database. Relative abundance, α-diversity, and principal coordinate analysis were performed using Phyloseq114 and Microviz115 analysis software.

#### Feces metatranscriptomics

Fecal RNA was isolated using RNeasy PowerMicrobiome Kit (Qiagen 26000-50) and quantified using Nanodrop. Extracted RNA was quality filtered and sequenced using whole genome sequencing methods published by our group116–119. Raw reads from whole genome sequencing of microbial community RNA were quality filtered using trimmomatic120. Quality filtered reads were analyzed for microbial taxonomy and functional characterization using MetaPhlAn3 and HUMAnN 3 pipelines, respectively. De-novo metagenome assembly was performed using megahit assembler121,122. Metagenome assemblies were annotated using prokka123.

#### Glycogen extraction

Flash frozen tissues, feces, or cultured microbes were homogenized into glycogen isolation buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaF, 5 mM sodium pyrophosphate). Samples were briefly boiled and rapidly cooled prior to centrifugation at 6000g for 10 minutes. Supernatants were transferred into new tubes and centrifuged at 16000g for 10 minutes. Supernatants were mixed with anhydrous ethanol to a concentration of 55% and briefly boiled to solubilize glycogen. Samples were cooled at −20C for 30 minutes to precipitate glycogen and centrifuged at 16,000g for 10 minutes to pellet glycogen. Pellets were redissolved in water and the above ethanol purification was repeated. Final pellets were resuspended in water for subsequent assays.

#### Iodine Assay and Spectra

The iodine microplate assay was performed as previously described with some modifications124. Briefly, extracted glycogen was diluted at a 1:5 or 1:7 ratio with water or aqueous alpha amylase (≥5 units/mL; Sigma A3176). Samples were incubated at 37° for 30 minutes followed by 10 minutes at 100° to inactive alpha amylase. 100uL of each sample was added to a 96 well plate alongside standards prepared from bovine liver glycogen (Sigma G0885). 5uL of 2% I/NaI was added to each well and the plate was chilled to 4°. Concentration was determined by the difference between the 492nm and 670nm absorbance fitted onto a standard curve. Iodine-glycogen spectra were determined by scanning absorbance at 10nm intervals from 490 to 800nm.

#### Fluorophore-assisted carbohydrate electrophoresis (FACE)

FACE analysis was performed as previously described125,126 with some modifications. Briefly, glycogen samples were prepared in 50mM acetate buffer (pH 4.8) and water (1:1; v/v) to a final concentration of 0.5mg/mL or 0.25mg/ml based on the iodine microplate assay. Samples were digested for 16 hours with 400 units of isoamylase (Sigma #08124). Following digestion, samples were heated at 100°C to inactivate isoamylase. Debranched glucose polymers were derivatized on their reducing ends with the negatively charged fluorophore 8-aminonaphthalene- 1,3,6-trisulphonic acid (ANTS; Biotium #90010) and reduced with NaCNBH3.The resulting labeled chains were separated on a 25% polyacrylamide gel and visualized under a standard UV light imager (Biorad Gel Doc XR Imaging System).

#### Electron microscopy

P. buccalis and P. merdae were grown in BHI media and centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 2 minutes to loosely pellet. Supernatant was removed and bacteria is brought up in 1 mL alpha amylase supplemented in PBS for 20 minutes at room temperature or PBS vehicle alone. Then bacteria were centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 2 minutes, supernatant aspirated leaving only minimal residual liquid with pellet. 500 uL of freshly prepared triple aldehyde-DMSO fixative127 was gently added onto the bacterial pellets without resuspension, then centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 2 minutes at room temp to loosely pellet and sit at room temperature in fixative for 2 hours. Then fixative was aspirated, reserving bacterial pellet. 500 uL room temperature sterile PBS was added to wash pellets, centrifuged 1,000 rpm for 2 minutes, and washes and spins repeated 2 more times. Then 500 uL room temperature sterile PBS added to each pellet and stored at 4°C overnight prior to embedding. The specimens were thoroughly rinsed in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH7.4, then postfixed for 2 hours in an unbuffered 1:1 mixture of 2% osmium tetroxide and 3% potassium ferricyanide127. After rinsing with distilled water, the specimens were soaked overnight in an acidified solution of 0.25% uranyl acetate. After another rinse in distilled water, they were dehydrated in ascending concentrations of ethanol, passed through propylene oxide, and embedded in Poly/Bed resin mixture. Thin sections were sequentially stained with acidified uranyl acetate followed by a modification of Sato’s triple lead stain128 and examined in an FEI Tecnai Spirit (T12) with a Gatan US4000 4kx4k CCD. Bacterial density was measured by inverting images using ImageJ and measuring the mean gray value within each bacteria.

#### Metabolomics

The quantitation of acetic acid, butyric acid, isovaleric acid, lactic acid, propionic acid, and succinic acid was performed using isotope dilution GC-MS/MS by using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The absolute quantity of each SCFA was determined using calibration curves measured for each analyte. Measurements were normalized to sample dry weight. This was achieved by separating the plasma samples into 2 aliquots of equal weight (wet weight): 1 piece was processed for derivatization, and the other piece was dried overnight and then reweighed (dry weight). Dry weight normalization was calculated as (wet weight) × (dry weight/wet weight). Samples were analyzed by using the Thermo TSQ-Evo triple quadrupole in tandem with the Trace 1310 gas chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Chromatographic separation was achieved by using an HP-5MS fused-silica capillary column (30 m × 0.250 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coated with 5% phenymethyl siloxane. Each extract (1 μl) was injected in split mode (10:1). Helium as carrier gas flow was 1 ml/min. The GC oven temperature program was as follows. The initial temperature of 40°C was held for 2 min after injection before it was increased up to 50°C at 3°C/min1, followed by increase to 110°C at 5°C/min, then 250°C at 30°C/min and 310°C at 70°C/min, and then held at 310°C for 3 min. Argon was used as collision gas. The injector, transfer line, and ion source temperature were set at 260, 290, and 230°C, respectively. The mass spectrometer was tuned to an electron impact ionization energy of 70 eV in the MRM mode with the following parent to daughter ion transitions: m/z 61.0 → 43.0 for acetic acid, m/z 63.0 → 45.0 for [13C2]-acetic acid, m/z 61.0 → 43.0 m/z 71.0 → 41.0 for butyric acid, m/z 78.1 → 46.1 for D7-butyric acid, m/z 85.1 → 57.1 for isovaleric acid, m/z 87.1 → 59.1 for D2-isovaleric acid, m/z 135.1 → 45.1 for lactic acid, m/z 138.1 → 48.0 for D3-lactic acid, m/z 75.1 → 57.0 for propionic acid, m/z 77.1 → 59.0 for D2-propionic acid, m/z 101.1 → 55.0 for succinic acid, and m/z 105.1 → 57.0 for D6-succinic acid.

#### Single cell RNA sequencing

For each mouse (n=16), ⅛ of the right cerebral cortex was fixed and dissociated in accordance with 10X Genomics protocol CG000553 rev. B. After sample preparation, libraries were generated by the CWRU sequencing core on an Illumina NextSeq. Cell Ranger was used to demultiplex and convert sequencer reads to FASTQ format, which was analyzed via the R package Seurat v573. Data were normalized using the SCTransform129. Cell type annotation was done with Azimuth130. Differential gene expression was calculated via Seurat’s FindMarkers() function, using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Gene ontology enrichment was calculated via gProfiler131.

### QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Most of the data was analyzed using Prism10 (GraphPad Inc.). Survival data was assessed using the Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test. To assess normality of groups the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was utilized. For comparing two groups with normal distributions an Unpaired t test was used. For comparing two groups that did not meet the assumption of normality, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. For comparisons across more than two groups, one- or two-way ANOVA was performed with Šidák’s, Tukey’s, or Dunnett’s correction for multiple comparisons. For comparisons across more than two groups that did not meet the assumption of normality, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunns correction for multiple comparisons was used. Figure legends indicate which statistical test was performed and n values refer to the number of biological or well replicates. P values of significance are indicated in figure legends.
