# Longitudinal Metabolomics in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Implicates Impaired Lipid Metabolism

## Abstract

### Objective

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease characterized by altered metabolome and energy homeostasis, manifesting with body mass index changes and hypermetabolism—both prognostic of disease progression and survival. The cross‐sectional ALS metabolome has been characterized, but longitudinal correlations to functional decline are lacking.

### Methods

We longitudinally evaluated metabolomes from ALS plasma and terminal postmortem spinal cord and brain motor cortex tissue. We constructed 3 plasma models. A linear mixed effects model correlated all metabolite levels across all timepoints to their corresponding functional scores. An interaction model predicted a longitudinal change in function from baseline metabolites, whereas a progression model identified metabolites linked to a 20% or 50% drop in function. In postmortem samples, differential metabolites in onset versus second spinal cord segments served as a surrogate of disease progression. Mendelian randomization assessed potential causality from metabolites.

### Results

In plasma, all models primarily selected lipid metabolites and sub‐pathways, in addition to amino acids, xenobiotics, and various less frequently selected pathways. Among lipids, fatty acids and sphingomyelins were predominant, along with plasmalogens, phosphatidylcholines, and lysophospholipids. Sex interaction findings were nominal. In the spinal cord, sphingomyelin and long‐chain saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids were more abundant in the onset segment tissue, whereas phosphatidylcholines and phosphatidylethanolamines were less abundant. Mendelian randomization suggested that impaired carnitine and short chain acylcarnitine metabolism may be genetically determined in ALS, along with various antioxidant derivatives.

### Interpretation

Our findings suggest metabolomic changes primarily involving different lipid classes and carnitine metabolism may underscore ALS severity and progression. ANN NEUROL 2025;98:19–34

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease with death from respiratory failure within 2 to 4 years of symptom onset. 1 , 2 Although the primary pathology in ALS occurs in the central nervous system, 3 changes in the metabolome, 4 , 5 , 6 energy homeostasis, 7 and body mass index (BMI) 8 , 9 are an integral part of the disease process.

Altered metabolism in ALS is complex and multifaceted; patients with ALS experience global hypermetabolism 10 with elevated resting energy expenditure. 11 Metabolic changes occur across various tissues, including the brain, spinal cord, and muscles, and result from alterations in both glucose and lipid metabolism as well as impaired mitochondrial function with inefficient tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation activity. 9 This impaired metabolic function in ALS manifests by altered levels in energy substrates and intermediates; poor energy production in ALS could also impact downstream processes, inducing broad changes to the metabolome overall, including to bioactive metabolites and lipids. 9 In cross‐sectional analysis, specific metabolites significantly correlate with disease status, based on the revised ALS functional rating scale revised (ALSFRS‐R), 5 suggestive that metabolic status may reflect functional status.

Moreover, hypermetabolism 12 and steeper BMI changes 13 associate with faster disease progression and poorer survival, indicating that metabolome changes may occur over the disease course longitudinally. However, despite well‐established associations between the metabolome and ALS, 4 , 5 , 9 , 14 most previous studies were cross‐sectional. Our goal in this study was to overcome limitations of cross‐sectional analyses and assess longitudinal changes in the ALS metabolome throughout the disease course, including the terminal timepoint. We hypothesized that plasma metabolite changes would track with disease progression, serving as potential biomarkers, and offering new insights into ALS pathophysiology.

We performed untargeted plasma metabolomics early in the disease course and longitudinally 6 and 12 months later. In tandem, postmortem spinal cord and brain motor cortex were also analyzed in a participant subset, the first analysis, to our knowledge, of various central nervous system tissues in human ALS. In plasma, we generated models that were associated with metabolite abundance to ALS functional status by ALSFRS‐R across the disease course. An interaction model leveraged baseline metabolites to predict change in ALSFRS‐R and progression model identified metabolites linked to a 20% and 50% drop in ALSFRS‐R relative to baseline score. We also performed sex interaction analyses because the patient's sex can impact metabolism 15 and ALS, 16 , 17 including metabolites. 5 In tissue, mirroring disease progression, we assessed differential metabolites in the onset versus opposing spinal cord segment to identify species potentially linked to temporal changes in disease.

By leveraging longitudinal metabolomic profiles from a prospective cohort of individuals with ALS, our analysis identified several plasma lipid metabolites and lipid sub‐pathways that correlated with disease course, along with select amino acids and xenobiotics. In the spinal cord, select sphingomyelin and long‐chain saturated and monounsaturated fatty acid species were nominally more abundant in the onset tissue, whereas select phosphatidylcholines and phosphatidylethanolamines were less abundant. Mendelian randomization identified impaired carnitine and short chain acylcarnitine metabolism as possible genetic determinants in ALS, along with various antioxidant derivatives. Our findings reveal longitudinal changes in the ALS metabolome that associate with disease progression, yielding potential new ALS biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

## Materials and Methods

### Participants and Biosamples

Protocols were previously published and details are provided in the Supplementary Information (SI). 4 , 5 , 18 Briefly, all patients with ALS seen at the University of Michigan Pranger ALS Clinic older than 18 years and able to communicate in English were invited to participate and provided oral and written informed consent. Patients with ALS were diagnosed using the original/revised El Escorial criteria, and all were included meeting the criteria of definite, probable, probable‐laboratory supported, possible (revised criteria), 19 or suspected (original criteria). 20 All patients with ALS also met the Gold Coast criteria. 21 Patients provided non‐fasting plasma samples during their regular clinic visit, which were collected at baseline (T1) and approximate 6‐month intervals (T2 at 6 months and T3 at 12 months; Fig 1A). Brain and spinal cord samples were from patients who consented to donate tissue following their deaths. The University of Michigan institutional review board approved the study (HUM00028826).

### Metabolomic Profiling

Metabolon (Durham, NC) performed untargeted metabolomics of plasma and central nervous tissue samples by ultrahigh‐performance liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectroscopy; details are provided in the Supplementary Information. Metabolomics was performed in 2 batches. Internal standards accounted for instrument variability and a well‐characterized human plasma technical replicate accounted for process variability.

### Statistical and Data Analysis

Statistical and data analysis details are provided in the Supplementary Information.

#### Participant Demographics

Participant demographics were summarized by median (25th to 75th percentiles) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables.

#### Plasma Metabolite to ALSFRS‐R Models

The longitudinal plasma metabolomics dataset contained a total of 692 metabolites measured from 548 samples from 317 participants. Three plasma models were constructed: model 1 (linear mixed effects model), model 2 (longitudinal interaction model), and model 3 (progression model; Fig 1B). Model 1 used all metabolite abundances measured at all timepoints, that is, from baseline up to 12 months, and their corresponding ALSFRS‐R scores at the time of sample collection. Model 2 used baseline metabolite abundance to predict the longitudinal change in ALSFRS‐R, from baseline to 6 months, to examine disease progression, assuming a constant rate over time. There were 15 missing values for the ALSFRS‐R score out of 387 total scores, or 3.9%. Missing ALSFRS‐R scores were imputed with the average values between the data points preceding and following the timepoint of the missing value. Models 1 and 2 adjusted for age, sex, BMI at study entry, and onset segment. Benjamini‐Hochberg (BH)‐adjusted p values <0.05 were computed for models 1 and 2 (Supplementary Tables [Link], [Link], [Link], and S5). Model 1 results were reported using a threshold of BH‐adjusted p values of <0.05. Model 2 results were reported using a nominal p value <0.05 due to the more exploratory nature of this analysis, with lower power to detect due to the added variable of time versus model 1.

Model 3 was a Cox regression model that correlated baseline metabolite abundance to progression to an event (PTE), defined as a 20% (PTE20) or 50% (PTE50) decline in ALSFRS‐R relative to diagnostic ALSFRS‐R score. 22 PTE20 was defined as the first longitudinal ALSFRS‐R score that was ≤ 0.8 × initial ALSFRS‐R or death. PTE50 was defined as the first longitudinal ALSFRS‐R score that was ≤ 0.5 × initial ALSFRS‐R or death. Participants who did not experience PTE20 or PTE50 or were lost to follow‐up were censored. The model was adjusted for age at initial sample, sex, BMI at entry, onset segment (bulbar versus non‐bulbar, ie, cervical and lumbar), diagnostic ALSFRS‐R, time between symptom onset and diagnosis, and definite versus non‐definite (ie, probable, probable‐laboratory supported, possible, and suspected) diagnostic El Escorial criteria. BH‐adjusted p values <0.05 were computed for model 3 (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7), but results were reported using a nominal p value <0.05 due to the more exploratory nature of this analysis, with lower power to detect due to the right‐censored data.

#### Plasma Metabolomics Pathway Analysis

Unsupervised gene set variation analysis assessed pathway activity in each participant sample based on the abundance of all metabolites within each pathway for models 1, 2, and 3.

#### Plasma Metabolomics Sex Interaction Analysis

Sex interaction analyses were performed for models 1 and 2 by adding the interaction effects between sex and pathway as model covariates. Models were adjusted for age, BMI, and onset segment. BH‐adjusted p values <0.05 were computed for the sex interaction of models 1 and 2 (Supplementary Table S3), but results were reported using a nominal p value <0.05 due to the more exploratory nature of this analysis, with lower power to detect due to the added variable of sex versus models 1 and 2.

#### Spinal Cord and Brain Metabolomics Analyses

Analysis first examined unique and overlapping metabolites across lumbar spinal cord, cervical spinal cord, and brain motor cortex samples, summarized by sub‐pathways and super‐pathways. Next, analysis of differential metabolites in the onset versus a second spinal cord segment was used as a surrogate of disease progression. Pairwise Wilcoxon test identified significant metabolites and pathways; BH‐adjusted p values <0.05 were computed (Supplementary Table S9), but results were reported using nominal p values <0.05 due to the small sample size.

#### Mendelian Randomization

Mendelian randomization (MR) was performed using single‐nucleotide polymorphisms from published genome wide association studies of ALS 23 and plasma metabolites 24 in participants of European ancestry because participants in this study were 97% White. Significance was based on inverse variance weighted (IVW) p value <0.05.

## Results

### ALS Participant Characteristics of Plasma Cohort

From 317 participants with ALS, we had available 548 plasma samples, 317 at baseline (previously published), 5 142 at T2 (ie, 6 months), and 87 at T3 (ie, 12 months; Table 1). The cohort was typical of ALS, with baseline median age of 64.0 years, within peak ALS prevalence, greater proportion of male subjects, and a distribution of bulbar versus spinal (cervical and lumbar) onset within anticipated ranges, 2 which remained relatively constant longitudinally. As expected with time, participants grew older (p value <0.0001, T1 to T3) and disease progressed, based on an ALSFRS‐R decline (p value <0.0001, T1 to T3). Median BMI for the ALS cohort did not change significantly with time.

### Plasma Metabolomics Linear Mixed Effects Model 1

Model 1 investigated the association of metabolite abundance to the corresponding ALSFRS‐R score at the time of sample collection across all timepoints, from baseline to 12 months. This model is an extension of our previously published purely cross‐sectional baseline analysis 5 by further correlating metabolite levels to ALSFRS‐R across the continuum of disease. Model 1 selected 262 significant metabolites (adjusted p value <0.05), of which 80 contributed significantly to the model (|beta| > 0.2; Fig 2A, Supplementary Fig S1A, Supplementary Table S3). Lipids were prominently featured (54%, primarily positively correlated to ALSFRS‐R), followed by xenobiotics (19%, exclusively positively correlated to ALSFRS‐R), amino acids (19%, two‐thirds positively correlated to ALSFRS‐R), and a few percent of various additional metabolites. The lipid class constituted fatty acids (26%), sphingomyelins (23%), plasmalogens (16%), lysoplasmalogens and lysophospholipids (12%), phosphatidylcholines (9%), and a few percent of various additional lipids. The most significant metabolite was the plasmalogen 1‐(1‐enyl‐palmitoyl)‐2‐linoleoyl‐GPC (P‐16:0/18:2) (adjusted p value = 5.29E‐28), which also contributed the most to model 1 (β = 0.495).

Next, model 1 assessed the aggregated levels of sub‐pathway metabolite members to identify sub‐pathways significantly associated with ALSFRS‐R scores across all timepoints. There were 56 significant sub‐pathways (adjusted p value <0.05), of which 18 correlated positively and 38 negatively with ALSFRS‐R scores (Fig 2B, see Supplementary Fig S1B, Supplementary Table S2). Lipid sub‐pathways continued to be predominant, at 39% (of which approximately 60% correlated negatively with ALSFRS‐R), followed by xenobiotics (16%) and amino acids (14%), with fewer sub‐pathways from nucleotides (7%), cofactors and vitamins (7%), peptides (7%), carbohydrates (5%), and energy (3.6%). Of lipid sub‐pathways, most were related to fatty acids (41%); however, sub‐pathways were selected, for example, primary and secondary bile metabolism, that were not represented when we examined individual metabolites. Evaluating sub‐pathway level significance also identified candidates not selected by examining individual metabolites, for example, sub‐pathways belonging to the carbohydrate super‐pathway.

Sex interaction in model 1 was nominal, with 6 sub‐pathways belonging to amino acid, lipid, carbohydrate, and xenobiotic super‐pathways (see Supplementary Fig S1C, Supplementary Table S3).

### Plasma Metabolomics Longitudinal Interaction Model 2

We next assessed whether baseline metabolite abundances could predict ALSFRS‐R at 6 months, that is, predict net change in functional status 6 months from baseline. Given the interaction analysis, we focused on the 52 nominally significant metabolites with a time interaction, of which 31 correlated positively with ALSFRS‐R change, that is, with functional decline, and 21 correlated negatively with disease progression (Fig 3, see Supplementary Fig S2A, Supplementary Table S4). Most were lipids, compromising 52% (approximately 56% positively correlated to ALSFRS‐R change), followed by amino acids (25%, approximately equally positively or negatively correlated to ALSFRS‐R change), xenobiotics (12%, all positively correlated to ALSFRS‐R change), and various others (12%). Of the lipids, fatty acids (26%), diacylglycerols (22%), phosphatidylcholines (22%), and other phosphatidyl species (15%) were represented, with only a single sphingomyelin and single hexosylceramide species.

### Plasma Metabolomics Progression Model 3

Finally, model 3 was PTE, defined as a drop of 20% (PTE20) or 50% (PTE50) in function relative to baseline ALSFRS‐R. This model then correlated baseline metabolite abundance to time between diagnosis to this decrease, of 20 or 50%, in the baseline ALSFRS‐R score. For participants earlier in their disease course with higher ALSFRS‐R scores, a drop of 20% or 50% translates to a relatively larger ALSFRS‐R change compared with participants later in their disease course with lower ALSFRS‐R scores (see Fig 1B). Therefore, this model indirectly accounts for disease severity of participants. The choice of event was based on a survey of ALS clinicians and researchers, where 90% of respondents considered a 20% drop in ALSFRS‐R as “somewhat clinically significant,” and a drop of 50% as “very clinically meaningful.” 25 PTE has been used previously to evaluate ALS progression. 22

PTE20 and PTE50 selected 55 and 61 nominally significant metabolites, respectively, which overlapped to a significant extent (46 shared metabolites; see Supplementary Table S6). Most PTE20‐selected metabolites were lipids (55%), followed by amino acids (25%) and xenobiotics (13%), mirrored, as expected, by PTE50‐selected metabolites, which were 49% lipids, 26% amino acids, and 15% xenobiotics. For the PTE20 model, within lipid super‐pathways, there were 27% sphingomyelins, 13% lysophospholipids, 10% monoacylglycerols, and 27% of various fatty acid species. The breakdown by lipids for the PTE50 model was similar, as anticipated, with 39% sphingomyelins, 13% lysophospholipids, 10% monoacylglycerols, 19% of various fatty acid species, and, additionally, 10% hexosylceramides. We next performed pathway enrichment analysis of all selected metabolites to identify sub‐pathways especially relevant to ALS progression (Fig 5). Both sphingomyelins and monoacylglycerols were significantly enriched for PTE20 and PTE50, suggesting these lipid sub‐pathways are relevant to ALS progression, across the spectrum of disease severity. None of the amino acid or xenobiotic sub‐pathways were enriched.

When we summed sub‐pathway metabolites, new significant sub‐pathways emerged for PTE20 and PTE50, including oxidative phosphorylation and dipeptides (see Supplementary Table S7), in addition to sub‐pathways that represented singly selected metabolites, for example, monoacylglycerols, fatty acids. Interestingly, PTE20 and PTE50 sub‐pathway examination did not select sphingomyelins, as opposed to examination by single metabolites, which selected several sphingomyelins. This may again suggest dysregulation of specific sphingomyelin species in ALS as opposed to general impairment in the entire sub‐pathway.

Last, there was some overlap between model 3 PTE50 with both model 1 and model 2, although there were also uniquely selected metabolites, such as hexosylceramides and medium chain fatty acids (see Fig 4).

### ALS Participant and Sample Characteristics of Spinal Cord and Brain Cohort

Ten (9.7%) participants provided 1 sample, 27 (26.2%) provided 2 samples, and 66 (64.1%) provided all 3 samples (see Table 2, Supplementary Fig S3A). Among cervical tissue donors, median age at diagnosis was 64.2 years and median age at autopsy was 65.5 years with 37.2% bulbar, 33.0% cervical, and 25.5% lumbar onset ALS. Findings were similar among lumbar and motor cortex sample donors except they varied slightly in the distribution of onset segment.

### ALS Cervical and Lumbar Spinal Cord and Motor Cortex Metabolites

We detected 703, 665, and 701 metabolites in the cervical, lumbar, and motor cortex samples, respectively, that were annotated and with <20% missingness (see Supplementary Fig S3B). Regarding metabolite overlap, 564 were shared by all 3 tissues, 638 were shared by cervical and lumbar, and 593 and 595 were shared by cervical and lumbar, respectively, with motor cortex (see Supplementary Fig S3B, Supplementary Table S8). There were 36, 36, and 41 metabolites unique to the cervical, lumbar, and cortex, respectively. As anticipated for myelin‐rich nervous tissue, 256 (45%) of the 564 metabolites shared by all tissue types belonged to the lipid super‐pathway. Second most abundant were 146 metabolites (26%) of the amino acid super‐pathway, followed by nucleotide, carbohydrate, cofactors and vitamins, peptide, xenobiotics, energy, and partially characterized molecules. These 564 metabolites represented 97 sub‐pathways, top among them the lysophospholipid, methionine, cysteine, S‐adenosylmethionine, and taurine metabolism, and sphingomyelin sub‐pathways with 24, 22, and 21 members, respectively (see Supplementary Table S8). In sum, fatty acid acylcarnitines, spanning saturated long‐chain, saturated medium‐chain, short‐chain, monounsaturated, and polyunsaturated species, had 30 metabolite members.

### Differential Metabolites in Onset Versus Second Spinal Cord Segment in ALS

We did not have control autopsy tissue; therefore, to identify metabolites linked to ALS progression, we assessed differential species between the onset tissue versus the second segment as a surrogate. We compared the lumbar to the cervical metabolites in the 20 participants with lumbar onset ALS that provided both lumbar and cervical samples, and cervical to lumbar metabolites in the 18 participants with cervical onset that provided both samples. No metabolites significantly differed between the onset versus the second segment, although 33 were nominally significant (Fig 6, see Supplementary Table S9). Most were lipids (69%), especially sphingomyelins (15% of total) and long‐chain saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids (12% of total), that were more abundant in the onset tissue. In contrast, phosphatidylcholines (9% of total) and phosphatidylethanolamines (6% of total) were more abundant in the progression tissue. Other significantly differential metabolites included amino acids (3 species, 9% of total), cofactors and vitamins (3 species, 9% of total), energy metabolites (2 species, 6% of total), and very few others. Only sphingomyelins and lipids enriched (Supplementary Fig S4). Finally, progression was the strongest determinant of metabolite abundance compared to sex or onset segment.

### Mendelian Randomization and Possible Causal Relation of Metabolites to ALS

We performed MR using a large genome wide association study of patients with ALS (n = 27,205) and controls (n = 110,881) of European ancestry, 23 as well as one of 8,299 participants of European ancestry linked to 1,702 plasma metabolites. 24 Datasets of European ancestry were selected because participants in this study were 97% White. MR identified 114 significant metabolites (IVW p value <0.05), of which 98 were annotated (Supplementary Table S10). Heterogeneity and pleiotropy testing yielded no significant metabolites. As might be expected from models 1, 2, and 3, which consistently selected lipid, amino acid, and xenobiotic super‐pathways, MR metabolites were, in large part, lipids (25%), amino acids (20%), and xenobiotics (16%), with a smaller proportion of energy, carbohydrate, nucleotide, peptide, cofactor, and vitamin, and partially characterized molecules. Among lipid sub‐pathways, sphingomyelin and diverse fatty acid and carnitine metabolism sub‐pathways were more prominently featured, which contained short‐ and medium‐chain fatty acid acylcarnitine metabolites, along with a few various additional lipid sub‐pathways.

MR metabolites overlapped with metabolites both significantly and nominally selected by each of the models, 1, 2, and 3, and the central nervous system analysis (see Supplementary Table S10). Most of the overlapping metabolites were lipids and amino acids. One highly recurrent candidate was sphingomyelin (d18:2/24:1 and d18:1/24:2), which may have genetically determined elevated levels in ALS via 32 single‐nucleotide polymorphisms. In model 1, sphingomyelin (d18:2/24:1 and d18:1/24:2) levels correlated negatively with ALSFRS‐R, that is, higher levels with lower functional status, and it favored the time to event for a 20% or 50% drop in ALSFRS‐R in model 3. Finally, in the central nervous system model, sphingomyelin (d18:2/24:1 and d18:1/24:2) was more abundant in the onset than in the second segment, a reflection of more progressive disease.

Pathway enrichment yielded carnitine and fatty acid metabolism (BCAA metabolism), containing 2 short‐chain fatty acid acylcarnitine species (butyrylcarnitine and propionylcarnitine; Supplementary Fig S5). However, the most significantly enriched sub‐pathways were hemoglobin and porphyrin metabolism and partially characterized molecules, both containing bilirubin and biliverdin, and various related degradation products. Glutathione metabolism was also highlighted, comprising antioxidant derivatives and oxidized byproducts thereof.

## Discussion

ALS pathophysiology is characterized by changes to the metabolome 4 , 5 , 6 and energy homeostasis, 7 , 9 manifesting clinically by correlation of BMI 8 , 13 , 26 and hypermetabolism 12 to disease progression and survival. Low BMI at diagnosis or BMI loss in the years leading up to diagnosis may presage rapid ALS progression. 8 , 26 Similarly, hypermetabolism in ALS adversely affects survival, 12 , 27 with elevated resting energy expenditure. 28 Thus, overall, metabolic, and metabolomic changes may underscore ALS progression. Indeed, we 5 , 8 and others 14 demonstrated that specific plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) metabolites, lipids, and/or metabolic modules correlate with cross‐sectional ALS functional status 5 , 14 and BMI trajectories. 8 Specifically, we found that ALS plasma was characterized by altered lipid, amino acid, and xenobiotic sub‐pathways, with several lipid species, including sphingomyelins and long‐chain acylcarnitines, correlated to cross‐sectional ALSFRS‐R.

Herein, we hypothesized that ALS progression would be similarly marked by longitudinal changes in metabolome. We examined individual metabolites as well as sub‐pathways because the sum of their collective member metabolites contributes to biological processes. We extend our prior findings in this new longitudinal dataset comprising granular metabolomic and functional data. Across the continuum of disease, individual lipid species and/or lipid sub‐pathways continue to remain prominent in all models 1, 2, and 3, including sphingomyelins, fatty acids, plasmalogens, phosphatidylcholines, and lysophospholipids. Interestingly, fatty acid, but not sphingomyelin, sub‐pathways remained prominent in sub‐pathway analysis, possibly suggesting broadly impaired fatty acid metabolism in ALS, linked to functional status, but only alterations in specific sphingomyelin species. The 2 models that integrated changes to ALSFRS‐R, models 2 and 3, selected several fatty acid sub‐pathways, especially long‐chain saturated acylcarnitine species, which may suggest that impaired baseline fatty acid metabolism may be prognostic of disease progression, aligned with hypermetabolism in ALS. 12 , 27

We also build on prior work by analysis of differential metabolite species between onset versus second spinal cord segment, as a surrogate of ALS progression and as a terminal timepoint. This metabolomic analysis is, to our knowledge, the largest to date, in terms of sample size and the types of central nervous system tissue assessed. 29 We found that most differential metabolites were lipids; among them, a few sphingomyelin and long‐chain saturated and monounsaturated fatty acid species were nominally more abundant in the onset segment, whereas phosphatidylcholines and phosphatidylethanolamines were nominally less abundant.

Although impaired metabolism in ALS, especially of lipids, 4 , 5 , 30 is relatively well‐established, few prior studies were longitudinal. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 A relatively small analysis of ALS CSF identified altered glycolytic metabolite abundance at the 2‐year follow‐up by proton nuclear magnetic resonance, a method of limited resolution, so the study lacked granular metabolomic information. 32 A mass spectrometry analysis of serum found ALS cases (n = 40) were uniquely perturbed in sphingolipids and glycerophospholipids at the 2‐year follow‐up versus controls, as herein, but the study lacked correlation to functional status. Finally, a relatively small mass spectrometry lipidomics analysis of serum from ALS (n = 28) versus control (n = 22) participants identified 14 lipid classes that changed with time in ALS, especially diacylglycerols, 31 which we noted as a differential class herein. Although the investigators did not examine correlation of lipid abundance to ALSFRS‐R, they found that diacylglycerol (18:1/18:1) correlated to neurofilament light chain levels, 31 a marker of neuronal damage and progression in ALS. Collectively, the reported literature, albeit using small sample sizes, supports our current findings.

Besides tracking with disease progression, and potentially serving as biomarkers, metabolomic changes can also shed insight into ALS pathophysiology and potential treatment avenues. We previously reported altered β‐oxidation metabolites in cross‐sectional ALS plasma analysis, evidenced by partially metabolized acylcarnitine intermediates. 5 Here, we noted the presence of long‐chain acylcarnitines linked to ALSFRS‐R in plasma and spinal cord tissue, now also supporting aberrant β‐oxidation in ALS progression. 35 , 36 Evidence from mutant SOD1 mouse models also uphold altered β‐oxidation and fatty acid metabolism, including in the spinal cord. 37 , 38 Enhancing β‐oxidation capacity by administering L‐carnitine to SOD1 G93A mice slows motor function deterioration and extends life. 39 However, although the precursor acetyl‐L‐carnitine has been considered as a potential ALS treatment, 40 an idea supported by the current findings, it is not approved clinically. Moreover, our prior 41 and current MR analysis indicates possible genetically determined causality from specific fatty acid, acylcarnitine, and carnitine species, further bolstering dysfunctional β‐oxidation in ALS. Enrichment analysis persisted in selecting carnitine and fatty acid metabolism, underscoring the centrality of this metabolic pathway in ALS, along with sub‐pathways comprising antioxidant derivatives and oxidized byproducts, possibly linked to oxidative stress, another ALS hallmark. 3

We also previously reported differential plasma sphingomyelins in ALS versus control participants, linked to cross‐sectional ALSFRS‐R. 5 We expand these findings here by showing that sphingomyelins further correlate with disease progression longitudinally in plasma and in onset versus second spinal cord segment, adding to the growing literature on this lipid class in ALS. 42 Cross‐sectional studies have noted altered sphingolipids, including sphingomyelins, in ALS plasma 43 and spinal cord, 44 , 45 , 46 corroborated by longitudinal studies in early 45 and symptomatic stages. 33 , 37 Modifying activity of enzymes involved in specific sphingolipid species biosynthesis, for example, glucocerebrosides, alters ALS progression rate in mouse models. 45 , 46 Moreover, the occurrence of monogenic childhood‐onset ALS with mutant serine palmitoyltransferase subunit 1 47 further supports a causative or early role for sphingolipid metabolism in ALS. Indeed, our MR analysis identified a sphingolipid, sphingomyelin (d18:2/24:1 and d18:1/24:2), with potentially genetically determined elevated levels in ALS, which was shared with metabolites selected by models 1 and 3, as well as the central nervous system analysis. The specific role of this sphingomyelin (d18:2/24:1 and d18:1/24:2) in ALS remains unclear, but warrants further investigation.

In addition to fatty acids and sphingomyelins, we found plasma plasmalogens, phosphatidylcholines, lysoplasmalogens, lysophospholipids, and diacylglycerols and/or sub‐pathways correlated with ALSFRS‐R over time. In the spinal cord, phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine metabolites were more abundant in the second segment. ALS changes in plasmalogens, phosphatidylcholines, lysophosphatidylcholines, and/or phosphatidylethanolamines have been observed in cross‐sectional 44 and longitudinal assessments. 31 , 33 , 37 Plasmalogens, 48 phosphatidylcholines, and phosphatidylethanolamines 49 are cell membrane components that regulate various biological functions, for example, mitochondrial function, membrane fusion, proliferation, survival, and cholesterol efflux. Phosphatidylcholines and phosphatidylethanolamines are synthesized from diacylglycerols through the Kennedy pathway, 50 rendering the pool of these bioactive lipids interconnected.

Model 1 also selected bile acid lipid sub‐pathways, that may be indicative of impaired lipid absorption and metabolism in the gut; indeed, patients with ALS may have an altered microbiome 51 and some microbiome‐derived metabolites in the current study, for example, indolelactate, correlated with ALSFRS‐R. In our previous gut microbiome‐plasma metabolome correlation, gut microbial structure at the phylum and genera level differed in ALS versus control participants. 41 In the integrated analyses, several microbiota genera/species strongly correlated to multiple metabolites, primarily lipids, including bile acids, which associated to functional ALS status and differentiated cases from controls.

Besides lipids, amino acid metabolism and xenobiotics were recurrent in all 3 plasma models but to a lesser extent in spinal cord tissue. Altered amino acid metabolism in ALS has also been reported by cross‐sectional 52 , 53 , 54 and longitudinal 52 , 55 human and animal studies, including of histidine, arginine, and proline, 52 as well as creatine metabolism. 43 The latter, creatine, however, is likely a byproduct of muscle wasting. Selected xenobiotic metabolites in baseline and longitudinal analysis belonged mostly to xanthine and benzoate metabolism and food component/plant sub‐pathways, as previously seen in cross‐sectional studies. 5 Sub‐pathway analysis further selected various drug sub‐pathways. Notably, “drug, gastrointestinal” and “drug, respiratory” abundance increased with disease progression, likely reflecting symptom management of patients with ALS.

Our study has some limitations. First, a limitation intrinsic to most human studies, is the tissue issue, because our longitudinal analysis was restricted to accessible blood samples, which may not necessarily reflect the disease process in nervous tissue or may instead reflect downstream processes. However, there is no method to longitudinally capture brain and spinal cord tissue, which was only represented at the terminal timepoint by the spinal cord and brain cohort. Nevertheless, metabolomics analysis of plasma, though non‐organ specific, can still reflect complex biochemical processes occurring throughout the body, including impaired systemic metabolism in ALS. Second, we had some cohort attrition in our longitudinal analysis, frequent in ALS studies, 56 which may have biased our findings. Third, our study involved ALS participants that had not fasted prior to providing blood collection because it was deemed unethical to request it. However, although diet influences circulating lipid levels, studies have shown that a plasma lipidomics signature of disease can occur independent of diet and/or fasting state. 57 , 58 Moreover, although we collected blood from patients with ALS at the same time intervals, samples may have been collected at different timepoints in their disease course. In addition, we assumed a linear rate of disease progression to impute missing ALSFRS‐R scores, which is often, but not always, the case. 59 However, ALSFRS‐R scores had to be imputed for only 3.9% of the data. A fourth limitation was the time window between a patient's death and the time of autopsy, which may have allowed for postmortem changes in the metabolome. Additionally, the spinal cord and brain cohort was relatively small and did not include any tissues from healthy controls to facilitate direct comparisons with ALS, which instead relied on comparisons of the initial to the second affected spinal segment as a surrogate of disease progression. Finally, because this was an exploratory study that encompassed all detected metabolites, we lost power to detect significant correlations of specific metabolites and pathways to ALSFRS‐R scores and relied on nominal p values for models 2 and 3, sex interaction analysis, and central nervous system metabolites. Future larger studies that analyze select target metabolites and pathways are needed to confirm our more exploratory findings.

Nevertheless, our study also had many strengths, including a longitudinal design using an agnostic metabolome‐wide analysis platform that detects and identifies an extensive number of metabolites in plasma from a relatively large cohort of ALS participants. Additionally, the metabolomic platform included multiple standards to assure low day‐to‐day variability and ability to mitigate batch effects. In our data analysis pipeline, we performed a sex interaction analysis for the longitudinal plasma data, which is relevant to ALS, including differences to metabolites and lipids by male versus female patients 5 ; however, the spinal cord and brain cohort was too small to examine the impact of sex on central nervous tissue metabolomes. Finally, we also assessed both metabolites individually and cumulatively within sub‐pathways. The former method identified robust single metabolites, whereas the second measured the sum of metabolites within sub‐pathways, which considers minor but cumulative contributions from multiple metabolites, an appropriate approach for a heterogeneous disease like ALS.

In summary, we show for the first time, to our knowledge, that differences especially in plasma lipid species and sub‐pathways, amino acids, xenobiotics, and various additional less frequently selected pathways, track with ALS progression, specifically with functional status by ALSFRS‐R. Examination by lipid class pinpointed fatty acids and sphingomyelins, along with plasmalogens, phosphatidylcholines, and lysophospholipids. In the spinal cord, sphingomyelin and long‐chain saturated and monounsaturated fatty acid species were nominally more abundant in onset tissue, whereas phosphatidylcholines and phosphatidylethanolamines were less abundant. Overall, our findings suggest that metabolic and metabolomic changes may underscore disease severity and progression in ALS and offer new opportunities for both biomarker and therapeutic development.
